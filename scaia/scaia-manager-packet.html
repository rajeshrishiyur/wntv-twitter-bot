Say this — about 90 seconds

"At Flexport, clients wanted to track all their containers in one place, including ones we didn't ship. When the system couldn't pick up a container automatically, it opened a ticket and someone looked it up on the carrier's website and typed it in. About sixty percent was automated, and it cost sixty-six cents a container. Leadership's top goal was to automate it — and they asked for a weekly review that nobody owned.

I took the review. Three decisions mattered. First, I made one number the headline: the share of containers tracked with no human touch. Result: automation from sixty to ninety-seven percent, cost per container from sixty-six cents to three while volume grew five times, and customers from seven to sixty-eight."

"How did you decide what stays human?" — Where the data source was missing or unreliable, and where a wrong answer cost more than a slow one. Every manual case got a reason code, so the human loop doubled as the backlog. A carrier left the human loop when it stayed above 95% automated for four weeks; if it dropped below 90%, it came back in.

"How did you prioritize what to automate?" — Value per engineering week: tickets per week × cost per ticket, plus customer interactions removed, divided by the build effort. A terminal with fewer tickets but a half-week build beat a big carrier with a three-week build.

"Why hold a wave at 76 vs 80?" — Because adding customers to a system that's already missing on quality multiplies tickets. 70% of the misses came from one data source; we fixed it, added a kill switch, re-tested for a week, and boarded.

"What was yours vs the team's?" — The metric definitions, the review, the human-in-the-loop rules, the wave gate and the prioritization. Engineering built the service.
