60-second pitch — stop on the class-A accuracy

Error correction — what is record, what I am reconstructing

Record. A published data-quality gate per domain. Daily reconciliation to the stock-requirements list, the production-order list, the sales-order list and the warehouse stock list. False alarms worked as defects, weekly, with a root cause. Domain owners could fail their own wave. The exception workbench was staffed in hypercare.

My reconstruction — confirm the names. At landing I reject, and do not silently fix: schema, a missing SKU or location, a unit mismatch, a negative or impossible quantity, a duplicate ship notice. The reject sits with the source owner. Before the alert is trusted that morning I count rows source versus landed, the sum of quantities, the open-PO total versus ERP, and on-hand versus the warehouse snapshot. The fix goes back to SAP, the supplier or the carrier, and returns on the next delta. I do not patch the warehouse. That is the Austria rule: measure the data before blaming the process — about 55% true against 40% reported, then about 99% within a quarter.

Example B — the alert rule, and safety stock, on the packet’s other illustrative lane (demand 100 units a day, not ROUTER-100). Days of cover = on-hand ÷ daily demand = 1,200 ÷ 100 = 12. Lane 90th percentile = 14 days → 12 is inside 14 → the alert fires today, a lead time early. Same SKU on a lane whose 90th percentile is 9 days: 12 > 9 → no alert. That is how a measured lane kills a false alarm the brochure would have raised.

Safety stock on that lane, the form I use to explain the buffer, not a screen I claim was in production: SS = z × d × σ of lead time = 2.33 × 100 × 4 = 932 units. If measured σ of lead time falls from 4 days to 2, SS = 2.33 × 100 × 2 = 466, and 466 units are the release — only if forecast error and lead-time error have both improved. Position falls below that 932-unit safety stock on day 3 (1,200 − 300 = 900). Position hits zero on day 12. Two tiers: below safety stock inside the lead time (re-plan), and at zero (the line-down we used to discover). z = 2.33 because the signed service level was 99%.

Data-integrity angle for this interviewer — the same mechanism, different nouns

What I ran is not a planning trick. It is eight source domains, one owner each, a nightly re-baseline plus deltas, a quarantine row with an owner, and counted rows — source versus landed, sums, open documents versus the book of record — before anyone trusts a number. On a capital-equipment program the nouns change and the mechanism does not: the equipment list, the supplier, the purchase order, the ship notice, the acceptance, the asset tag, the site, the placed-in-service date. Each input has one owner. A nightly snapshot heals a missed change from the contract manufacturer’s tracker. An intraday delta keeps a receipt current. A row that fails referential checks waits, with an owner, and the fix goes back to the source spreadsheet or the vendor, not into a side file that makes the dashboard green. I would not put a capitalization number, or any later model, on top of a reconciliation that has not been counted. This is my analogy from the manufacturing program. It is not a description of Apple’s tool.

Six probes — answer the one asked, then stop

1. Why both a nightly full load and a delta?

The full load heals a missed change pointer so masters match SAP in the morning. The delta is the clock: a noon receipt or 214 changes cover today. Masters before the transactions that cite them. “Watermark” and “upsert” are my words for the change pointer and the business key.

2. Why exclude unfirmed planned orders?

They change every planning run and were inflating supply. Released orders only, agreed before the first screen.

3. Walk one Class A finished good.

ROUTER-100, illustrative. On-hand 400, in-transit 600 on day 5, production 800 on day 9, demand 500 / 700 / 400. Position −100 on day 3, −200 on day 7, +200 on day 10. Timing, not volume. The other illustrative lane: cover 12 inside a 14-day 90th percentile, so the alert fires now; below 932 units of safety stock on day 3; zero on day 12.

4. Why the 90th for the alert and the 99th for the promise?

One distribution, two decisions. The alert is a re-plan trigger: illustrative recall 98.6% at 71.2% precision. The promise sits at the ranked 99th. A bell-curve 99th on Lane A held 96.5%.

5. How do you separate a false alert from bad data?

About 29% of alerts at the 90th percentile are the price of the window, illustrative. A defect is the data lying — wrong on-hand, missing receipt, an unfirmed order counted — and that kind is burned down weekly. I have no program count of the burn-down.

6. What would you refuse to automate until the number is trustworthy?

The buffer release, and any model on top of it. Austria was 40% reported and about 55% true. I release capital only where both errors improved, and I do not put a capitalization or a model on a reconciliation I have not counted.
