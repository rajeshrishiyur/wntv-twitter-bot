7. How do you identify, investigate, and resolve data discrepancies when working across multiple systems or teams?

Story: Elementum, Austria routing-guide data quality. The mechanism I still use when a number disagrees across systems is the Shipment Visibility rule: events in versus events out at every boundary.

Say this first

I do not start from the explanation already in the room. At a transportation customer, on-time delivery could not be measured. The report said data quality was about 40 percent, and everyone, including my sponsor, believed the carriers were late. The team was spending eight to ten hours a week reconciling promised dates against actuals by hand. I pulled three years of shipment history in SQL myself. True data quality, at the grain we would actually score, was about 55 percent, not 40. The defects were ours: missing rates, lanes at the wrong grain, one carrier under five spellings, brochure transit times sitting where a promise should be. We burned those classes down by reason code. Data quality reached about 99 percent within a quarter. The carriers never disputed the scorecard, because Finance had signed the method before I claimed a dollar.

S — Austria site. Reported routing-guide data quality about 40 percent. Manual reconciliation eight to ten hours a week. No enforceable on-time standard. The room’s cause was “carriers.” record

T — An on-time number a carrier could not argue with, and any dollar claimed only on a method Finance co-owned. I owned the analysis and the rules. The data platform team built the engine. record

A — I classified every blank. The rules engine looked up ship-from, ship-to, mode, volume, Incoterm, and the service level. A no-hit paged a person. It never defaulted a carrier. The scorecard could not go out until the automated on-time figure matched a manual sample for that lane cohort. Savings were contracted rate minus paid rate, executed shipments only. Austria went first, inside a month, about 97 percent agreement between the automated figure and the manual sample, about five hundred thousand dollars a year at that site on the signed method. The same rules file then went to the other European locations. record

R — About 55 percent true, against 40 percent reported, to about 99 percent within a quarter. About eight million dollars of validated freight savings across 25 European locations, executed shipments only. I do not quote a two-month or a three-month bar; “within a quarter” is the line both of my own write-ups support. record

Data-integrity angle. The wrong input was the routing guide itself — identity, grain, and a promise that was really a brochure. The owner of each defect class burned it down weekly. The control was a no-hit that stops, plus a pre-release sample, plus a Finance-signed formula. When the discrepancy is between systems rather than inside one file, I use the same idea at the boundary: count events in versus events out at every hop, and give the load a trace id. On Shipment Visibility, data crossed six system layers and a missing trace id was costing two to four hours per blocker just to find the row. That design change is record-from-docs. A note I wrote then claimed about half the time to resolve; I treat that half as my estimate, not a measured result.

Failure / learning. I disproved the brochure times lane by lane, which was slow, and my sponsor was unhappy for weeks. I would start from a benchmark, measure before I blame a partner, and sign the method before the number. The answer to “why is this taking so long” was the defect classes trending down, not a speech.

8. Can you describe a situation where you had to manage multiple stakeholders who were resistant to adopting a new process or system? How did you drive change?

Story: Sephora, a 20-person Product, Engineering, and QA team, six-week hybrid cadence to four-week. No line authority. The gate was owned by the skeptic. This is the “they have always done it this way” answer, and it closes without escalation.

Say this first

A VP asked me to move a twenty-person team from a six-week cadence to four weeks. QA was a contracting firm. They were heading for a standoff with in-house engineering, and they were right to be afraid: a shorter cadence often means a shorter test window. I had no authority over anyone in the room. I did not write the definition of done and impose it. I asked the most skeptical person, the QA lead, to author it. I coached what a gate has to be able to say — no, on the day of the release, with the room watching — and she wrote the content. In the tracker, a story could not enter QA until its entry criteria were met, and every sprint had a drop date with a reason code, so a heavy story landing in the last two days was a visible re-scope, not a heroics night. We converted in six weeks. Critical defects per release fell by about half. She defended the gate after I stepped back. I did not escalate the standoff. I gave the resistance the artifact.

S — Newly in the role. VP of Supply Chain wanted a four-week agile cadence. QA feared compressed test windows. Engineering wanted the date. Neither reported to me. record

T — Convert the cadence in weeks, not quarters, without the standoff becoming the story, and without a mandate. record

A — One-on-ones with every person. One influencer named per function. The skeptic authored the definition of done; engineering reviewed and did not get a veto. Entry-criteria fields and a QA drop date with reason codes made the rule visible in the tool, so adoption was not a speech. I covered some of the low-impact stories the drop-date rule pushed out, so the team did not pay for my process with a silent miss. record on the mechanism; covering dropped stories is how I kept the window — I will not invent a count of those stories.

R — Six weeks from kickoff to a cadence the team ran without me. Critical defects per release down about 50 percent — that result is on the resume; the workbook chapter is thinner and does not restate the defect figure, so I cite the resume. No direct reports. The gate survived my leaving, which was the test. record

Data-integrity angle. The input people were protecting was “this story is ready for QA.” It had been a conversation. I made it a field: benefit rank, review status, entry criteria met or not. A story with a blank entry could not move. The owner of the field was the skeptic, which is why the control outlived me. A process change that lives only in my slides dies when I leave the meeting.

Failure / learning. I went to engineering first and QA second. The person who can stop the release should be in the room on day one, with her name on the review line. A gate I write myself is decoration the week I am not there.
