5. The story bank — seventeen stories, each unique, each 90 seconds

Every story: speak the box, stop, let them probe. Probes and answers follow each box. Results framed as record vs model exactly as in Section 10 — if the number is your model, say "my model" out loud.

MAIN — Flexport: the visibility program nobody owned

Primary: Deliver Results. Backup: Ownership, "most impactful program." This is also the writing-exercise story — expect them to connect it.

90 seconds

"At Flexport I was the TPM for shipment visibility. Leadership wanted a weekly review of container tracking; nobody owned the number. The tracking itself was about 60% automated — the rest was people updating containers by hand, at sixty-six cents a container, and we were about to grow the customer count tenfold. I owned the program end to end. I defined the seven metrics with an honest denominator — a container stayed in the count until fifteen days after it left the terminal, so nobody could flatter the number by dropping slow containers. I ran the review with the VP every Monday for a year, by hand at first, on purpose — you can't automate a metric you haven't survived defining. Then I wrote the requirements doc that turned four systems into one Snowflake pipeline, and priced doing nothing at over $100K a year so the infrastructure team funded it themselves. Automation went from 60 to 97 percent. Cost per container went from sixty-six cents to three. We grew from seven customers to sixty-eight — five times the container volume — and the tracking team didn't grow with it; the people moved from typing updates to handling exceptions. System accuracy went from 60 to 90 percent because everyone finally stared at the same number every Monday."

Numbers

Automation 60% → 97% (record). Cost to serve $0.66 → $0.03 per container (dollarized with Finance's loaded rates, not mine). Customers 7 → 68; volume 5× (2,000 → 10,000 containers/week). Accuracy 60% → 90%. Review prep 30 → 6 hours a week, about $103,680 a year (model, from the BRD). One red week kept red — a carrier format change dropped the number to 21.9%; published with cause and a dated fix.

"What did you personally build — the engineers built the pipeline?"

The definitions, the denominator, the BRD, the parallel-run cutover gate — old versus new within one percent for consecutive weeks. I didn't lay pipe; I made the pipe buildable, and the cutover call was mine. Most mismatches in the parallel run were the manual side being wrong.

"What went wrong?"

The security review. Ticket data carries customer content and I under-planned the access review by two weeks. The parallel run absorbed it so the review never skipped a week — but security lead time is now a line on my checklist, planned, not discovered.

"How would you do this at Amazon?"

Same order: definitions first, hand-run the metric until it survives argument, then automate, then gate the cutover on agreement. The honest denominator matters more at Amazon's scale, not less.

S2 — Sephora: one definition of "available"

Primary: Ownership. Backup: Insist on the Highest Standards (held the spec), "influence without authority."

90 seconds

"Sephora moved its inventory book of record to S/4HANA while a real-time inventory ledger ran beside it. The real problem was vocabulary: stores, the warehouse and dotcom each calculated 'available to sell' differently — five definitions across five teams. Most overselling is a vocabulary defect, not a systems defect. No engineer reported to me. I took ownership of the definition itself: I mapped every inventory bucket to a named owner, wrote one definition of available-to-sell as a lower-of-two equation, and got Planning, Finance and Operations to sign it before any configuration started. When teams pushed to start building before the spec was locked, I refused — a definition change after code is a defect; before code it's a sentence. We reconciled the two systems every night and treated every mismatch as a defect to root-cause, not a quirk. Result: zero customer-facing oversell incidents across two high-volume go-lives, the legacy inventory system fully retired, about a million dollars a year of support cost gone. And the definition outlived the project — new channels onboard against it instead of inventing their own."

Numbers

5 definitions → 1 (record). Oversell incidents: zero across two go-lives (record). Legacy system retired; ≈$1M/yr support cost (model, Finance's rates). Nightly reconciliation with drift treated as defect.

"How did you get five teams to give up their definition?"

I didn't argue definitions in the abstract. I showed each team the oversell incidents their definition had caused, priced them, and proposed the lower-of-two rule as the version that could never promise a unit twice. People give up a definition faster than they give up being blamed for oversells.

"What was the trade-off?"

Lower-of-two is conservative — it understates availability slightly and costs some sales at the margin. We priced that against oversell cost and customer trust, and Finance signed the conservative side. I'd make the same call at Amazon scale: a broken promise costs more than a hidden unit.

S3 — Elementum: 200 lanes, the brochure said 18 days

Primary: Dive Deep. Backup: Are Right A Lot, "data over opinion."

90 seconds

"A manufacturing customer planned 200 shipping lanes on the carrier's quoted transit times. Inventory buffers kept growing anyway, and nobody could say why. I didn't ask anyone's opinion — I pulled about a year of actual shipment history per lane and fit the real distribution: mean, spread, and the tail. On one busy lane the brochure said 18 days. The actual average was 21, and the day that 99% of shipments beat was 34. Planning was quoting a number that real shipments missed most of the time, and planners were quietly padding everywhere to survive it. I rebuilt the planning parameters on measured numbers — plan to the slow day you can actually promise, and because the padding came off everywhere else, safety stock on my example lane went from about 12,900 units to 8,100 at better service, not worse. Across the program that released about 580,000 units, roughly a million and a half dollars. Then I made it stay true: every lane got a drift flag — if the rolling 90-day tail got two days worse than plan, or the spread grew by a quarter, the lane came up for re-measurement before it hurt anyone. What I'd do differently: run the alerts in shadow mode against history before the first one fired."

Numbers

Example lane: n≈400 shipments ≈ one year of history; brochure 18d, measured mean 21d, σ 4d, p99 = 34d. Safety stock 12,900 → 8,100 units on that lane (record). Program: ≈580K units ≈ $1.5M released (record). Drift flag: rolling-90d p99 > plan + 2d, OR 90-day σ > 1.25× baseline — either trips a re-measure. Keep this $1.5M separate from the $2M release in S5 — different program, never added.

"Why p99 and not the mean?"

A promise built on the mean breaks half the time by construction. p99 is the day 99 of 100 shipments actually beat — you can promise it out loud. And lead-time tails aren't bell-shaped; a normal-curve estimate understated the real p99 by about three days on my lanes, which is why I rank the history instead of assuming a curve.

"How is safety stock lower if you plan to a slower day?"

Because the old plan paid twice: the brochure number was wrong, so planners padded informally on top — the worst of both. One honest number replaced wrong-number-plus-panic-padding. Less total buffer, better service.

S4 — Elementum: promise dates customers could trust — and buffers sized to risk

Primary: Customer Obsession. Backup: Invent and Simplify (if S8 is used), functional lead-in to Section 7. This is the marketplace-style promise problem, told from the Elementum lane program.

90 seconds

"Once the 200 lanes were measured, the next fight was the promise. The customer quoted delivery dates to its own customers with one uniform pad on every lane — same cushion whether the lane was steady or wild. That's the worst of both worlds: on steady lanes the date is padded days longer than it needs to be, so you look slow and lose orders; on volatile lanes the pad isn't enough, so you still break promises. I reframed it around what the end customer actually experiences: the promise should be the measured 99th-percentile day per lane, and the cushion should be proportional to each lane's measured volatility — a steady lane earns a short promise, a wild lane pays for its own risk. I modeled both designs on our lane data. Same overall reliability — about 95 percent of orders arriving by the promised date — but the share of orders that could carry a fast promise went from under 10 percent with the uniform pad to roughly 40 percent with risk-proportional cushions. Nothing physically moved faster; we just stopped making steady lanes subsidize wild ones. The planners kept one dial — the service target — and the math set every lane's cushion from its own history. Customer-facing dates got shorter where it was safe and honest where it wasn't."

Numbers

Buffer rule: cushion per lane = k × that lane's measured σ; one k for the whole network, solved to the service target. My model on the lane data: uniform pad → under 10% of orders on a fast promise at ~96% reliability; risk-proportional → ~40% fast at ~95%. Say "my model" — the mechanics are exact, the proportions are modeled. Promise = measured p99 + the cushion rule + the S3 drift flags so it stays true.

"Isn't a 1-point reliability drop a real cost?"

Yes, and it was priced: the question for the business is whether 5× more fast promises is worth one point of reliability. That's a leadership trade-off to present, not to bury — I brought both designs with the numbers and let the owner of the customer experience choose. That's also how I'd present it at Amazon: promise speed and promise reliability on the same page, with the dial visible.

"How do you keep it honest over time?"

The same drift flags as the lane program: when a lane's tail or spread degrades past threshold, its promise reverts to the conservative date until it's re-measured. The promise follows the data, not the other way around.

S5 — Elementum: eight SAP domains, one supply view, and the $2M that was earned, not grabbed

Primary: Earn Trust. Backup: Invent and Simplify, "ambiguous problem with no defined strategy."

90 seconds

"A six-site manufacturer learned about component shortages when the line stopped. Supplier, material, BOM, inventory, production, transportation, sales-order and planning data all lived in SAP — eight domains that never met — so leadership's question, 'which customer orders are at risk this quarter,' had no data path. Nobody owned the seam; I took the definitions, the data-quality program and the delivery. The trust move came first: before any screen was built, I got planners to sign what 'supply' means — on-hand, plus the production plan, plus released planned orders only. Planning wanted to count unfirmed MRP orders because the number looked better; I refused — unfirmed orders change every run, and a leaner number planners trust beats a fatter one they don't. We onboarded the believable domains first, reconciled daily against the SAP screens planners already lived in, and worked every false alarm as a defect. The shortage alert went live in three months, and forecast accuracy on the A items went from 70 to 85 percent. Then the part I'm proudest of: we only released safety stock where both forecast error and lead-time error had improved — a two-signal rule signed with Planning and Finance. That released about two million dollars of stock, and nobody above us ever had to walk it back."

Numbers

Forecast accuracy 70% → 85% on scoped class-A SKUs (record). $2M safety stock released under the two-signal rule (record, one-time working capital); ≈$0.8M/yr modeled around it. Alert live in 3 months. Daily reconciliation to MD04 / COOIS / VA05 / MB52. Planner effort −50%. Never add this $2M to S3's $1.5M — different program.

"Why did planners trust this alert when they ignored every other one?"

Three reasons: they signed the supply definition before the first screen existed; the view reconciled daily against the SAP screens they already believed; and false alarms were worked as defects in a weekly loop, so the alert got quieter and righter every week. Trust was the designed outcome, not a hope.

"Two million released — what if demand had spiked?"

That's exactly why the rule needed two signals. Stock was cut only where the forecast had gotten more accurate AND the lead time more reliable — both error bars shrinking. And Finance co-signed the rule, so the release was a policy, not a hero call.

S6 — Elementum: making a contract manufacturer's BOM safe to plan on

Primary: Learn and Be Curious. Backup: Dive Deep, external-supplier management (the JD's supplier/compliance paragraph).

90 seconds

"Same manufacturer — their contract manufacturer supplied the bill of materials our component visibility depended on, and BOMs from outside carry two traps the documentation never confesses. Phantom assemblies — logical groupings that pass demand through without being stocked — double-count component timing if you load them as real. And overlapping revisions — two versions of a BOM both partly true for weeks — rewrite history if you auto-load one. I didn't know contract-manufacturing data when I started; I learned it by math, not by meetings. I sampled finished goods, exploded the BOMs in our model, and compared component counts against the manufacturer's own MRP — the mismatches were the phantom signature, and their engineers confirmed what the numbers had already proven. Then I made the rules: phantoms get flattened with an explicit marker; any overlapping revision goes to a quarantine queue for a human decision, never auto-loaded; and each site goes live only when a reconciliation report matches their system within tolerance. Six sites, three waves, five months. The ugliest site had a legacy ERP with flat files and no change tracking — we invented snapshot-diffing, comparing full daily extracts, and absorbed it in six weeks against a three-week average, in plan. Zero auto-loaded revision overlaps, ever. Lesson that stuck: reconcile to the source's math, not its documentation."

Numbers

0 → 6 contract-manufacturer sites with planning-grade BOM/supply visibility, 3 waves, 5 months (record). Zero auto-loaded revision overlaps (record). Hostile-ERP site: 6 weeks vs 3-week average, solved with snapshot-diffing. ≈$0.3M/yr modeled (reconciliation labor + expedites avoided). Say "six sites," never "six NA sites"; don't quote a tolerance value — it isn't in the record.

"What did you personally learn here?"

What a phantom looks like in the deltas, how revision effectivity actually behaves, and that a supplier's documentation describes intent while their MRP describes truth. I now start every external-data integration by reconciling against the source's own math before believing a single field description.

"How does this apply to Amazon?"

Any vendor or carrier feed at Amazon has the same shape: the data is generated by someone else's process with someone else's semantics. The pattern — sample, reconcile to their math, gate go-live on the reconciliation, quarantine ambiguity for a human — ports directly.

S7 — Elementum: 1,000 lanes asked, 597 built first

Primary: Have Backbone, Disagree and Commit. Backup: Insist on the Highest Standards (the held cohort), influence two levels up — the L6 scope question.

90 seconds

"The customer's Global Head of Supply Chain — two levels above my sponsor — wanted inbound visibility on all 1,000 supplier and carrier lanes in two months. Shipment data was 60% complete and the highest-value lanes were effectively blind. Saying yes would have produced a thousand lanes of garbage on time. I disagreed with the scope, not the goal — and I disagreed with data, not opinion. I built a spend Pareto and scored every lane: 597 lanes carried 80% of freight spend. I pre-validated that cut with Transportation and Procurement before the executive meeting, so when I proposed 597 first, then 200, then the rest, nobody in the room could contradict it — and the executive accepted the plan measured in spend share, not lane count. Then I protected quality with a published gate: a carrier cohort activates only at 95% valid shipment notices, and the standard was the data, not the transport — carriers without EDI got portal templates instead of exclusion. One cohort failed twice on a timestamp format; I held it twice, because the gate was a published rule, not my mood. Data completeness went from 60 to 95 percent; freight spend dropped about 10 percent — about two million dollars in six months — and the hardest part was committing fully to the two-month urgency while refusing the thousand-lane scope."

Numbers

Completeness 60% → 95% (record). 597 lanes = 80% of spend, live in month one. Freight −10% ≈ $2M in six months (record; the split into routing-guide compliance, mode discipline, expedites is my model). Gate: ≥95% valid ASN per cohort before the next activates; one cohort held twice. X12 850/855/856/940/945/214/997 with an XML/portal floor. Don't quote cohort sizes — not in the record.

"The executive wanted 1,000 — you delivered 597. Isn't that failing the ask?"

The ask was visibility, measured in spend covered, not lane count. 597 lanes was 80% of spend in month one — the executive got the business outcome earlier than the original plan would have gotten it. The remaining lanes followed behind the same gate. Disagree on the scope, commit to the goal.

"What if he'd insisted on all 1,000 anyway?"

Then I'd have committed, out loud, and asked for one condition: the quality gate stays. The gate is what keeps the data worth collecting at any scope. You can flex scope and schedule; automating dirty data just manufactures confident wrong answers.

S8 — Elementum: the carrier bid — six weeks to three

Primary: Invent and Simplify. Backup: the JD's "competitive sourcing environment" question, process improvement. Owns its own failure beat (procurement joined late).

90 seconds

"The customer ran a carrier bid twice a year: hundreds of lanes, dozens of carrier rate files, six weeks per cycle. Everyone assumed it was an optimization problem and wanted a fancier solver. Before building anything, I sat through one full bid cycle and logged where the hours actually went. The answer: reconciliation, not analysis — every carrier named lanes and modes differently, and the team spent the six weeks translating. Wrong problem class, so a solver would have optimized garbage. The simple fix: a lane master — one normalized table of origin, destination and mode — and every constraint the leads carried in their heads written down as an explicit rule: volume commitments, incumbency floors, mode rules. I rejected a black-box optimizer in writing; the output had to be scenario comparisons a human could defend to an incumbent carrier. And the gate came before the tool: the model had to reproduce the last two real awards within 5 percent before it was allowed to run on a live bid. We parallel-ran one region's actual bid — tool versus manual — and the pilot region finished three weeks early. The cycle went from six weeks to about three, and other regions adopted it without a mandate, which is the only adoption that lasts. My miss: Procurement joined late — I treated it as Transportation's problem and it wasn't. They were in discovery from day one of the next cycle."

Numbers

Cycle 6 weeks → ~3 (record); pilot region 3 weeks early. Validation gate: <5% mismatch vs the last two award cycles, published before any optimizer run. Weekly rate-file refresh; stale banner past 10 days. ≈$190K/yr modeled analyst time returned. No dollar claim on the award itself — Transportation and Procurement ran the award.

"How do you decide to simplify instead of extend?"

Diagnose the problem class from a time audit before building anything. If the hours go to reconciliation, the answer is a data model; if they go to analysis, the answer might be a solver. The fanciest tool pointed at the wrong problem class is pure waste — that's the sentence I'd bring to any Amazon mechanism review.

"Why not a proper optimizer once the data was clean?"

Because the award has to survive a meeting with a losing incumbent carrier. Scenarios with explicit constraint rules are defensible; a black box is not. Where a solver genuinely helps, it sits behind the rules, never instead of them.

S9 — Flexport: apples to apples — the comparison that almost killed the product

Primary: Are Right, A Lot. Backup: Dive Deep. Never with the same interviewer as S10.

90 seconds

"The biggest risk on the visibility program was retiring the legacy tracking product — still billing 79 customers — while leadership stared at our 25 percent schedule reliability against a competitor's 63. That comparison was about to get the new product written off. My instinct said measurement gap, but instinct isn't an argument, so I rebuilt the comparison instead of debating it: normalized both numbers to the same data sources, same definitions, same week. Their 63 collapsed to about 27 against our 25 — a measurement artifact, not a product gap. And the honest version cut both ways, which is what made it credible: on the same basis we still trailed on variance, sixteen days against their eight. I named our gap myself and handed it to the source-addition backlog as the real work. Then I closed the root cause with a one-canonical-source rule — one definition of reliability, one scoreboard — because parallel dashboards had let every team pick its flattering number. The team whose dashboard lost got a dignified retirement: cited as the prompt for the source work, never mocked. Reliability climbed from the mid-twenties to 90 against an 80 percent north star as sources were added — real work, not normalization math — and the norm survived me: two numbers that disagree get reconciled before they get compared."

Numbers

Comparison: 25% vs "63%" → normalized 25% vs ~27% (record). Residual honest gap: variance 16 days vs their 8 — named by me. Reliability mid-20s → 90% vs an 80% north star. Rule that stuck: one canonical source; reconcile before you compare.

"Isn't normalization just making your number look better?"

A normalization that only flatters is spin; one that also exposes is analysis. Mine capped our own claim — it surfaced the variance gap against us and I said so in the same readout. That's why it held.

S11 — Sephora: robots at 94 against a 98 gate

S12 — Sephora: flipping an 18-month roadmap

S13 — Sephora: the $25 task that now costs 40 cents

S14 — Manhattan: Cyber Monday, and the half-hour rule

Primary: Bias for Action. Backup: Customer Obsession (if S4 used), "calculated risk under time pressure."

90 seconds

"At Manhattan Associates I ran order promising and allocation for a retailer with three mega-DCs and about a hundred stores, through Cyber Monday. Peak doesn't give you time to convene a committee — orders age against shipping cutoffs in hours. So we built the triage to make decisions fast and reversible: every at-risk order got scored by dollars times urgency — one over the hours remaining to its cutoff, floored at half an hour so the formula couldn't divide by zero and panic. That put the next-most-expensive-to-lose order at the top of the queue automatically; reallocation to a different DC or a store was a two-minute decision with a known cost, and if the move was wrong you could move it back — reversible, so speed was cheap. Through the peak we held 99 percent fill and protected about eight million dollars of orders. The bias-for-action lesson I've carried since: build the decision rule before the crunch, so that when the crunch comes, acting fast is the default and deliberating is reserved for the genuinely irreversible calls."

Numbers

Promise-at-risk score = order $ × 1 / max(hours to cutoff, 0.5). 99% fill through Cyber Monday (record); ≈$8M of peak orders protected (model on protected order value). 3 DCs + ~100 stores as flexible capacity.

"When is fast wrong?"

When the decision is one-way. Reallocating an order is two-way — move it back if wrong. Changing the promise logic itself mid-peak is one-way-ish — that one I'd slow down, test, and stage. The skill isn't speed; it's sorting decisions by reversibility first.

S15 — Sephora: the migration where the fallback never fired

S16 — Elementum: the UAT that crashed — the failure story

Primary: "Tell me about a time you failed." Also: Earn Trust (owning it with the sponsor), "what would you do differently."

90 seconds

"Early at Elementum I ran a customer acceptance test and I owned the entrance criteria. My checklist was functional — did the features work, was the data mapped, were the test scripts signed. It said nothing about performance. UAT opened, dozens of the customer's users logged in at once, and the system fell over under concurrent load — in front of the exact audience whose confidence we needed. The sponsor escalated, and they were right to. The failure was mine specifically: not the code, the gate. I had defined what 'ready for UAT' meant and defined it incompletely — we had tested that it worked, never that it worked with everyone on it at once. I owned it with the sponsor without an excuse in the sentence, we load-tested, fixed, and re-ran UAT — and it passed. But the fix that matters is the permanent one: from that day, every UAT entrance gate I've written has performance criteria in it — concurrent load at realistic numbers, tested before a single customer logs in. That rule has followed me through every program since, including two go-lives at Sephora where load testing before the customer sees the system is simply non-negotiable. The lesson in one line: a gate only protects you from the failure modes you wrote down."

"Why didn't you think of performance the first time?"

Because every demo had been one user at a time, and nothing in my experience to that point had made concurrency fail loudly. That's the honest answer — gates encode scars, and I didn't have that scar yet. What changed permanently is that I now ask "what haven't I written down" about every gate, and I steal failure modes from other people's postmortems instead of waiting for my own.

7. The functional block — the math they may hand you

The JD says forecasting, modeling, decomposing complex network problems. Expect at least one interviewer to go functional. Each block below is a two-minute whiteboard answer; speak the logic, write only the one formula.

F1 — "How would you set a delivery promise?"

Say it like this

"Never on the average — a promise built on the mean breaks half the time by construction. I take the actual delivery history per lane, rank it, and read the day that 99 percent of shipments beat. And I rank rather than assume a bell curve, because delivery tails are long: on my data a normal-curve estimate understated the true p99 by about three days — a promise that breaks three days' worth of the time you claimed it wouldn't. Then two guards: a drift flag per lane so the promise re-opens when the lane degrades, and a cushion sized to each lane's own volatility rather than one pad for everyone."

Worked example (say it, don't write it all):
20 shipments, mean 12.5 d, sigma 2.64 d.
Normal-curve p99 = 12.5 + 2.33 x 2.64 = 18.6 d.
Actual ranked 99th percentile of the same data = 22 d.
The curve understates the honest promise by ~3.4 days.

F2 — "One buffer for the network, or per lane?"

Say it like this

"Per lane, proportional to risk. A uniform pad makes steady lanes subsidize wild ones — you look slow where you're reliable and you still break promises where you're not. Set each lane's cushion as one global multiplier times that lane's measured volatility, and solve the single multiplier to the service target. On my Elementum lane data the two designs hit the same roughly-95-percent reliability, but the share of orders eligible for a fast promise went from under ten percent to about forty. Nothing shipped faster — the math just stopped charging every lane for the worst lane's sins. One dial for leadership, per-lane honesty underneath."

F3 — "Walk me through safety stock" — the one-lane before/after

One lane, real numbers:
BEFORE  plan on brochure 18 d; actual mean 21, sigma 4 -> late all the time,
        so planners pad informally on top -> ~12,900 units held, service still shaky.
AFTER   plan on measured p99 = 34 d promise, buffers from measured sigma only
        -> ~8,100 units, ~99% service.
Less stock AND better service, because one honest number replaced
a wrong number plus panic padding. Program-wide: ~580K units, ~$1.5M.

F4 — "You have a fixed budget — where does it go?"

Say it like this

"Two rules. First, improvements have diminishing returns — the first week of effort on an initiative buys more than the tenth — so I model each candidate as a saturating curve and spread a fixed budget by marginal return: fund every initiative up to the point where its next unit of effort earns the same as every other's. Second, compare lever classes honestly: in my promise work, re-allocating effort across the right initiatives moved the customer-experienced time by whole days, while tuning buffer sizes moved it by tenths. Structural levers beat parameter levers; spend on parameters only after the structure is right. And re-solve when the world changes — peak-season volatility isn't a footnote; when sigma scales up for Q4, the whole allocation shifts and you re-run it, not hand-adjust it."

F5 — "Decompose a complex network problem"

Say it like this

"Same four moves every time. One: define the output metric and its grain before touching anything — most 'complex' problems are two teams using one word for two numbers. Two: Pareto the volume or spend — complexity is rarely uniform; at Elementum, 597 of 1,000 lanes carried 80 percent of freight spend, so the thousand-lane problem was really a six-hundred-lane problem with a long tail behind a gate. Three: sequence by trust — onboard the data or the sites people already believe first, so early wins fund the hard ones. Four: publish the quality gate before the first wave, so scaling is mechanical — the same gate absorbs lane two and lane nine hundred. Decomposition isn't cutting the problem into pieces; it's finding the 20 percent that is the problem and putting a gate behind it for the rest."

F6 — "How do you measure forecast health?"

Say it like this

"Accuracy and bias, separately — MAPE tells you the error size, bias tells you whether it leans, and a forecast can score well on one while rotting on the other. Then drift detection on the inputs, not just the outputs: my lanes carried a standing flag — if the rolling 90-day tail ran two days past plan, or the spread grew past 1.25 times baseline, the lane came up for re-measurement automatically. And one rule I'd defend anywhere: parameters only relax on two signals. We released two million dollars of safety stock only where forecast error and lead-time error had both improved — one signal is noise, two signals is evidence."

8. The writing exercise — defending your essay

Submitted: "What is the most inventive or innovative thing you have done?" — the Flexport story: making automation measurable (the automation rate with the honest denominator), running the WBR by hand for a year, then the BRD that turned four systems into a Snowflake pipeline. Any of the five may have read it. If an interviewer opens it, that interview's Flexport budget is spent — do not also tell MAIN there; use S15 for Deliver Results instead.

"Why is this innovative? It sounds like reporting."

The invention is the measurement design, not the dashboard. Before it, "how automated are we" had no answer — the number didn't exist. The honest denominator (a container counts until 15 days after it leaves the terminal) is what made the number impossible to game, and that's what let a VP steer on it. Inventive doesn't have to mean novel technology; it means the thing didn't exist, and afterwards the company ran on it.

"Your essay says you ran it by hand for a year. Why so slow to automate?"

Deliberate. You can't automate a metric you haven't survived defining — every definition fight I lost and re-fought in those months went into the BRD, which is why engineering's build matched the review on the first parallel run. Hand-running was the cheapest possible prototype.

"The results in the essay — which are measured and which are modeled?"

Automation 60 to 97, customers 7 to 68, volume five-fold, accuracy 60 to 90 — measured. The dollar figures — 66 cents to 3 cents per container, and the hundred-thousand-a-year cost of doing nothing — were built with Finance's loaded rates, not mine, and survived their review. I keep the two categories separate on purpose.

11. Traps — the sentences that cost you the loop

  • Never "50×." Customers 7 → 68. Volume 5× — two thousand to ten thousand containers a week. Say the real numbers.
  • Never add the $1.5M (lane program) to the $2M (safety-stock release). Two programs. One interviewer will test whether your dollars reconcile across stories.
  • Never repeat a story across interviewers. They compare notes. The tracker in Section 3 exists for exactly this; S9/S10 and MAIN/S17b are the dangerous pairs.
  • Never present a modeled dollar as an audited one. The phrase "my model, Finance's rates" costs nothing and buys credibility on every other number.
  • "Six sites," never "six NA sites." No cohort sizes on the EDI story. No tolerance value on the BOM reconciliation. No dollars on the carrier-bid award. If it isn't in the record, don't invent it under pressure.
  • Don't lead planning questions with the omni DC. It's an execution story (S11); the planning stories are Elementum and S/4. Use each where it's strong.
  • "We" hides you. Every action sentence starts with "I" and names an artifact — the gate, the rule, the table, the memo. Credit the team in the result, not in the actions.
  • Stop at 90 seconds. The interviewer's probes are how they fill their worksheet; a five-minute monologue reads as unstructured, not thorough.
  • Don't guess Amazon vocabulary. If they say a mechanism you don't know, ask what it owns. Asking reads as curiosity; guessing reads as bluffing.

12. Video execution + what to ask each interviewer

Video, specifically

  • Camera at eye level, light in front of you, interviewer's window directly under the camera so eye contact is real. Test Chime/the link the day before; phone number as audio backup written on paper.
  • Print Section 6 and the Section 3 tracker; tape them beside the screen. Glancing at a one-page map reads fine on video; visibly reading paragraphs does not.
  • Video eats silence — but keep the pause anyway. Finish the 90 seconds, then stop. Two seconds of quiet while they write is them filling the worksheet in your favor.
  • Say numbers slowly and repeat the units: "sixty-six cents — to three cents — per container." On compressed audio, numbers are the first thing lost.
  • Five interviews is a stamina problem. Between calls: stand up, water, mark the tracker, read the next likely story's numbers box. Nothing else.
  • If the connection drops mid-story, rejoin and restart the story from the top in one line: "Short version of where I was: …" — don't resume mid-sentence.

  • Stakeholder-focused: "Which partner team is hardest to align with today, and why?" · "When this team disagrees with Science or Product on a parameter, where does the decision actually get made?"
  • Execution-focused: "What broke last peak, and what mechanism came out of it?" · "What does the weekly review look like here — who reads it, and what got stopped because of it recently?"
