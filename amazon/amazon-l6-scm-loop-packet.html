MAIN — Flexport: the visibility program nobody owned (the WBR and the automation rate)

Primary: Deliver Results. Backup: Ownership, "most impactful program." This is also the writing-exercise story — if an interviewer opens the essay, this story is spent with them.

90 seconds

"At Flexport I was the TPM for shipment visibility. Leadership wanted a weekly review of container tracking; nobody owned the number. Tracking was about 60% automated — the rest was people updating containers by hand at sixty-six cents a container — and we were about to grow the customer count tenfold. I owned it end to end. I defined the seven metrics with an honest denominator — a container stayed in the count until fifteen days after it left the terminal, so nobody could flatter the number by dropping slow containers. I ran the review with the VP every Monday for a year, by hand at first, on purpose — you can't automate a metric you haven't survived defining. Then I wrote the requirements document that turned four systems into one Snowflake pipeline and priced doing nothing at over $100K a year, so the infrastructure team funded it themselves. Automation went from 60 to 97 percent, cost per container from sixty-six cents to three in Finance's loaded rates, and we grew from seven customers to sixty-eight — five times the container volume — without growing the tracking team; the people moved from typing updates to handling exceptions. System accuracy went from 60 to 90 percent because everyone finally stared at the same number every Monday."

S — Flexport, 2021–23, TPM for shipment visibility. The business was about to scale the product from 7 customers to the full client base, but container tracking was ~60% automated; the rest was manual updates at $0.66/container, and there was no agreed measure of "how automated are we" — the number did not exist. The VP asked for a weekly business review; nobody owned it. Risk of doing nothing: the manual team scales linearly with volume, and leadership steers blind.

T — I owned the metric definitions, the weekly review itself, and then the automation of the data pipeline beneath it — end to end, with no engineer reporting to me.

A — 1. I defined the seven steering metrics and the honest denominator: a container counts as "active" until 15 days after it leaves the terminal, so slow containers cannot fall out of the number. 2. I ran the WBR with the VP every Monday for a year, hand-building the pack — deliberately, because every definition fight those months surfaced went into the spec. 3. I kept a red week red: when a carrier format change dropped automation to 21.9%, I published it with the cause and a dated fix instead of smoothing it. 4. I discarded a flattering week: 99.4% computed on three touches in a truncated week — a VP steering on that is a decision-quality defect, so it went out as a data miss. 5. I wrote the BRD that consolidated Zendesk, the scraper registry, the product event DB and the CRM into Snowflake, priced doing nothing at >$100K/yr with the infra team's own rates, and froze the seven definitions in the spec. 6. I gated the cutover on a parallel run — manual vs pipeline within 1% for consecutive weeks; most mismatches were the manual side being wrong.

R — %: automation 60% → 97%; system accuracy 60% → 90%. $: cost to serve $0.66 → $0.03 per container (dollarized with Finance's loaded rates); doing-nothing cost >$100K/yr retired. #: customers 7 → 68; volume 5× (2,000 → 10,000 containers/week) with no growth in the tracking team. Time: WBR prep 30 → 6 hours/week. Quality: first automated catch — a 4% ticket-sync drop invisible in the manual era. Vs goal: the goal was a trustworthy weekly review; it became the program's operating system.

Numbers

Automation 60→97% (record). CTS $0.66→$0.03 (model, Finance's rates; platform priced one eliminated interaction at $0.18 — $0.66 ≈ 3.7 residual touches/container, $0.03 ≈ 0.17). Customers 7→68; volume 5×. Accuracy 60→90%. WBR 30→6 h/wk ≈ $103,680/yr (model, from the BRD). Red week 21.9% published; truncated 99.4% week discarded. Parallel-run gate |manual−pipeline| ≤ 1%.

The trade-off: scope for fundability. The BRD froze the seven existing metrics and named new metrics out of scope — a narrower pipeline this quarter beat a perfect one someday. The other side: cutover waited on consecutive matching weeks, not on my confidence. What I gave up: ~4 hours/week of manual stitching continued until the gate passed.

"The engineers built the pipeline — what did you build?"

The definitions, the denominator, the BRD, the parallel-run gate, and the cutover call. I didn't lay pipe; I made the pipe buildable — and afterward nobody could argue about the numbers. Programs like this die as complaints and ship as documents; the document was the work.

"What went wrong?"

The security review — ticket data carries customer content and I under-planned the access review by two weeks. The parallel run absorbed it so the review never skipped a week. Standing rule since: security lead time is planned, not discovered.

"How would you do this at Amazon?"

Same order: definitions first, hand-run the metric until it survives argument, automate, gate the cutover on agreement. The honest denominator matters more at Amazon's scale, not less.

S4 — Elementum: the marketplace promise — dates customers could trust, buffers sized to risk

Primary: Customer Obsession. Backup: "balance customer and business," functional lead-in. This is the marketplace promise/buffer story, told from the Elementum lane program — the full math is §7 M3–M4.

90 seconds

"Once the 200 lanes were measured, the next fight was the promise. That's the worst of both worlds: on steady lanes the date is padded days longer than it needs to be, so you look slow and lose orders; on volatile lanes the pad isn't enough, so you still break promises. I modeled both designs on our lane data: same overall reliability, about 95 percent of orders arriving by the promised date, but the share of orders that could carry a fast promise went from under 10 percent with the uniform pad to roughly 40 percent with risk-proportional cushions. M3 — Buffer design: uniform vs risk-proportional, with the Φ math

Mechanism. If a lane's promise = expected transit + buffer b, and transit noise is roughly symmetric with std dev σ, the chance the promise holds ≈ Φ(b/σ) — the standard normal cumulative. One uniform b gives every lane a different hit rate; b = k × σ gives every lane the same one.

LaneUniform b = 1.10 dRisk-proportional, k = 1.65What the customer sees

Volatile lane, σ = 2.0 dΦ(1.10/2.0) = Φ(0.55) ≈ 71% — broken promisesb = 1.65 × 2.0 = 3.30 d → Φ(1.65) ≈ 95%honest (longer) promise, actually kept

Same physics, ~5× the fast-promise share, one point of reliability — a presented leadership choice. One dial (k), re-solvable in a day.

M5 — Where a fixed budget goes: diminishing returns and equal marginal value

Mechanism. Each candidate initiative i improves the target metric by valuei(w) = ai × (1 − e−c·w) for w units of effort — steep first, flattening after. With a fixed budget (illustrative: 60 engineer-weeks / $900K across 7 initiatives), the optimum funds every initiative to the point where the next unit of effort buys the same marginal value everywhere: ai × c × e−c·wi equal across funded initiatives; anything whose first unit is below that bar gets zero.

Toy version with two initiatives, 10 weeks to split, c = 0.3:
A: a=4.0 days of promise improvement   B: a=1.5 days
Marginal at w: A' = 1.20 e^(-0.3w)     B' = 0.45 e^(-0.3w)
Equalize:  1.20 e^(-0.3 wA) = 0.45 e^(-0.3 wB),  wA + wB = 10
-> wA - wB = ln(1.20/0.45)/0.3 = 3.27  -> wA ~ 6.6, wB ~ 3.4
Not 50/50, and not winner-take-all: the big lever flattens too.

The two findings to say out loud. One: re-allocating effort across initiatives moved the customer-experienced promise by whole days (~3.75 in my model); re-tuning buffer sizes moved it by tenths — structural levers beat parameter levers, spend on parameters only after the structure is right. Two: re-solve when the world changes — peak season scales lane σ by roughly 1.45 in my model, and the optimal allocation shifts; you re-run the solve, you don't hand-adjust last quarter's answer.

M6 — The drift flag: exactly how it's computed

Per lane, every week, over the trailing 90 days (~100 shipments at ~8/wk):
flag if  p99(90d)  >  LT_plan + 2 days          (the tail moved)
     or  sigma(90d) > 1.25 x sigma(baseline)     (the spread grew)
     or  late count >= 4 per 100 arrivals        (service slipping now)
Example, Lane A (plan 34, sigma_base 4):
   flag at p99_90 > 36, or sigma_90 > 5.0, or >= 4 late per 100.
Action on flag: planner review + lane re-measure - never a silent re-plan.
Why 3 tests: tail, spread, and outcome degrade at different speeds;
any one alone is late or noisy. What I'd add today: shadow-mode the
flags against history before the first live fire.

   Automation(week) = 1 - (human-touched containers / Active)

M8 — Forecast health: accuracy, bias, and the two-signal release

MAPE  = mean( |forecast - actual| / actual )     - size of the error
Bias  = mean( forecast - actual ) / mean(actual) - which way it leans
Measure BOTH: a forecast can have flat MAPE while bias rots (or vice versa).
Forecast accuracy (the 70 -> 85): scored weekly against outcomes;
false alarms worked as defects in the weekly loop.
Two-signal release rule (the $2M): relax a buffer parameter only where
   forecast error improved AND lead-time error improved.
One signal = noise. Two signals = evidence. Co-signed by Finance.

M9 — Promise-at-risk triage: the half-hour floor, worked

score = order $ x 1 / max(hours to cutoff, 0.5)
Order A: $9,000, 6.0h  -> 9,000/6.0  = 1,500
Order B: $2,400, 0.8h  -> 2,400/0.8  = 3,000   <- acts first
Order C: $20,000, 24h  -> 20,000/24  =   833
Without the 0.5 floor, a $50 order at 3 minutes outranks everything;
with it, urgency saturates and dollars decide. Reallocation is two-way
(move it back if wrong) so the queue runs fast; one-way changes
(promise logic itself) are excluded from fast-path. 99% fill, ~$8M.

M10 — Decomposing a network problem: the four moves, with the numbers

1. Define the output metric and its grain FIRST
   (most "complex" problems are two teams using one word for two numbers).
2. Pareto the volume/spend: score = spend x criticality x mode x volume
   -> 597 of 1,000 lanes = 80% of freight spend.
3. Sequence by trust: onboard what people already believe first
   (inventory + production before transportation; cleanest CM sites first).
4. Publish the gate before wave one, then scaling is mechanical:
   >=95% valid ASN per cohort; 90/90/80 wave graduation; <5% bid back-test.
Decomposition is not cutting the problem into pieces; it is finding the
20% that IS the problem and putting a gate behind the rest.

Event-trace math (the S19/S21 spine): per hop, completeness = events at hop N ÷ events at hop N−1; drop rate = 1 − completeness. The broken hop is the first ratio that sags — localization becomes reading a table. That one design change halved blocker-bug localization time.

14. Traps — the sentences that cost you the loop

  • Never "50×." Customers 7 → 68. Volume 5× — two thousand to ten thousand containers a week. Say the real numbers.
  • Never add the $1.5M (lane program) to the $2M (safety-stock release). Two programs. One interviewer will test whether your dollars reconcile across stories.
  • Never repeat a story across interviewers. They compare notes. The tracker in §3 exists for exactly this; S9/S10, MAIN/S17b, and S18/S21/S23 (all pilot-era) are the dangerous clusters.
  • Never present a modeled dollar as an audited one. The phrase "my model, Finance's rates" costs nothing and buys credibility on every other number.
  • "Six sites," never "six NA sites." No cohort sizes on the EDI story. No tolerance value on the BOM reconciliation. No dollars on the carrier-bid award. If it isn't in the record, don't invent it under pressure.
  • Pilot numbers are pilot numbers. 500 containers / 99.6% was a perf test; 45 was a waitlist, not revenue; 86% tracked was the gap that justified monitoring. Claiming any of them as steady-state production results is the kind of inflation a Bar Raiser is listening for.
  • Keep the attribution you've already given. The hop-by-hop test design credits a test lead (§9 Are Right A Lot Q6, §10 Learn & Be Curious Q6); the trace ID is yours. Swapping those under pressure reads as either theft or inconsistency.
  • Don't claim a DEI program you didn't run. The honest claim is mobility equity — the super-user pipeline — with the scope said out loud. The overclaim is worse than the modest truth.
  • Don't lead planning questions with the omni DC. It's an execution story (S11); the planning stories are Elementum and S/4. Use each where it's strong.
  • "We" hides you. Every action sentence starts with "I" and names an artifact — the gate, the rule, the table, the memo. Credit the team in the result, not in the actions.
  • Stop at 90 seconds. The interviewer's probes are how they fill their worksheet; a five-minute monologue reads as unstructured, not thorough.
  • Don't guess Amazon vocabulary. If they say a mechanism you don't know, ask what it owns. Asking reads as curiosity; guessing reads as bluffing.
