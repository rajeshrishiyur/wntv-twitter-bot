The sixty-second answer — what outbound is, as stock and flow

Say this version out loud, then stop. It is the whole packet in one paragraph.

Say this — 183 words

Outbound is a chain of stocks and flows running against a clock. An order becomes a promise, the promise becomes a critical pull time, and from then on the package moves through a series of queues: pick, pack, label, sort, the dock, the trailer, the sort center, the second trailer, the delivery station, the van. Each queue is a stock; each process is a flow with a rate and a capacity; each handoff has a cutoff. The package makes the promise when every stock empties ahead of its cutoff, and it costs what each resource cost divided by how many packages rode it. So the design job has three objects. The clock: set the pull times from the measured transit tail, not the average. The denominators: fill on the trailer, packages on the route, volume through the sorter. And the plan: a weekly scorecard that reads cost per package and on-time against plan, decomposed into the inputs a building can move this week. Everything else is which node to build, which lane to run, and how to prove it before you scale it.

The one mental model to keep on top. Outbound is a reservation system running against a promise. The promise is made first, from inventory position and a transit table. Capacity is then reserved behind it: labor hours, sorter throughput, trailer positions, van routes. Most outbound failures are a stock that did not empty before its cutoff, and most outbound cost is a denominator that was smaller than the plan assumed. The metrics in Part 6 exist to catch those two things one week earlier than the customer does.

Part 1 — The outbound machine as stocks and flows

1.1 The vocabulary and the eight equations

A stock-and-flow model has three kinds of object. A stock is a count of things sitting or moving at one point: packages waiting to be packed, trailers in the yard, packages on vans. A flow is a rate into or out of a stock: packages packed per hour, trailers departed per cutoff. A cutoff is a vertical line on the clock that a stock must be empty by. The whole outbound network is sixteen stocks in series, each drained by a process with a capacity, each with a cutoff inherited from the one after it. The eight equations below are the only arithmetic the packet uses; everything in Parts 2 to 6 is one of them substituted with the reference day's numbers. industry standard for the equations; illustrative for every substitution.

Cumulative curves: the picture under every cutoff

The cleanest way to see a stock against a cutoff is two cumulative curves. The upper curve is cumulative arrivals into the stock; the lower curve is cumulative departures out of it. A cutoff is a vertical line; the vertical gap at that line is the miss. Capacity is the maximum slope of the lower curve (equation 2). 2.1 Order, promise, and assignment

What happens. The customer sees a delivery date. Amazon's help page describes that date as the shipping date plus transit time in business days, with an "order within" countdown while the clock is open public Amazon practice. Behind the date are three decisions that happen in seconds: which building ships it, by which ship method, and against which critical pull time. The building is chosen from the ones that hold the units, have outbound capacity in the window, and can reach the customer's delivery station by its cutoff, at the lowest cost. The CPT attached to the order is the latest departure from that building that still makes the promised date. That is the clock everything in the building runs to.

2.2 Release and pick

What happens. Assigned orders wait in S2 until they are released to the floor. Release is by CPT priority: the orders whose pull time is soonest go first, and orders for later departures fill idle capacity. Released work is batched into pick tasks so that one picker's tour serves many orders. In a pod-to-person building the pods come to the picker; in a manual building the picker walks the path; either way the unit of work is a unit picked into a tote, and the rate is units per hour. The tote rides conveyance to consolidation or pack. The stock that matters is the pick backlog in hours, not units: units ÷ (pickers × rate).

2.3 Pack and SLAM: the label is the commitment

What happens. Single-unit orders go straight to pack. Multi-unit orders are consolidated first, because their units were picked by different people into different totes. The packer selects the box or bag, adds dunnage, and closes it. The package then goes to SLAM: it is scanned, weighed, the weight is compared with the expected weight of its contents, the shipping label is printed and applied, and the package is manifested to its ship method and departure public Amazon practice for the SLAM step and its position between pack and the outbound trailer. Two things happen at SLAM that matter to the network. The weight check is the last automatic chance to catch a mis-pick before it leaves the building. And the label fixes the lane and the departure: after this point, changing the package's path is a rehandle.

2.4 Ship sort, the dock, and the critical pull time pile

What happens. The labeled package rides the ship sorter, which reads the label and diverts it to a chute. A chute is a destination and a departure: the SC-1 lane's 21:00 trailer, the SC-2 lane's 21:00 trailer, direct DS-3's 20:00 truck, the carrier's 19:00 pickup. At the door, loaders build gaylords or fluid-load the trailer that is live for that chute. Packages that arrive at the chute before the trailer closes ride; packages that arrive after it closes become the pile for the next departure. The stock at the door at the moment of CPT is the single most important number in the building's day: it is the miss.

The site verdicts go to the annual network capacity review with finance and the regional directors; the design standards go to the facility design authority as a versioned document with the math attached. The trap. Comparing the site to "nothing" instead of to the alternative path. Every sort center beats a carrier; the question is whether it beats its neighbor and the direct lane, and that cushion is usually under 2×.

The small version I have run. The Flexport cost-per-container work record endpoints: $0.66 to $0.03 a container, with the worksheet rates behind it labeled illustrative and the finance rate for one interaction ($0.18) kept separate from the ticket price. That is the step-2 discipline: build the variable cost from the ledger's own rates, label what is assumed, reconcile to the endpoint finance already believes.
