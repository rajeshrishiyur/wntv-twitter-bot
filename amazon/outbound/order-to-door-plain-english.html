Plain-English companion to the outbound network design packet

Order to door, in plain English

One package, happy path. One whole day, happy path. Then the things that go wrong, one at a time. Then the measurements, the weekly scorecard, and how this job would build the dashboard.

Who this is for. Rajesh Rishiyur Chandrasekaran, preparing for a role that owns the design of a large outbound transportation and sorting network. Written so that someone with no logistics background could follow every step and every calculation.
How it is written. No abbreviations. Every calculation is shown as arithmetic you can check with a pencil. We use one made-up network with one set of numbers, stated in full in Chapter 1, and we never change them. We walk one customer's package first with nothing going wrong. Then one whole day with nothing going wrong. Only after that do we add problems, one at a time, and show what each one costs and how you would measure it. The measurements are explained from that same example, so there is nothing to memorize.
What is real and what is made up. The shape of the journey (warehouse, truck, sorting hub, truck, neighborhood depot, van) is how Amazon has publicly described its network; the sources are in Chapter 8. Every rate, time, and dollar amount is made up for the example so the arithmetic is clear. None of them is an Amazon figure. Chapter 8 also gives the words you will hear in the interview for the plain words used here.

Two words we will use constantly. A pile is packages waiting somewhere: in a bin at the door, in a trailer in the yard, on the hub floor. Almost everything that goes wrong in this business is a pile that was not empty when its deadline came. Almost everything that costs too much is a truck, a building, or a van that carried fewer packages than it was paid for. Keep those two sentences in mind and the rest follows.

It has a formal name, transportation cost per package, and Chapter 5.1 defines it word by word.

 Maria ordered at 2:12 PM, nearly three hours before the 5 PM deadline, so the website showed "arrives Tuesday."
 Had she ordered at 5:30 PM, her package would have been assigned to the 3 AM truck, and the website would have said Wednesday.

Three things to notice. First, every deadline on this list is set by the deadline after it; move the van departure to 8 AM and every line above it moves an hour earlier, including the time Maria must order. Second, the 9 PM truck departure is the most important deadline in the warehouse: it is what every pile inside the building is racing. (The industry word for it is critical pull time. This document just says "the truck leaves at 9 PM.") Third, the "spare" minutes in the brackets add up to about 5 hours across the whole chain. That spare time is what absorbs a slow trip or a slow unload. Chapter 4 is mostly about what happens when it runs out.

A dashboard is the scorecard made live: the same measurements, the same definitions, refreshed as the scans come in, with the ability to click any number and see the packages, trailers, or vans behind it. 7.5 Checking the numbers before anyone sees them

A dashboard that is wrong once is ignored forever. So three checks run automatically before each screen refreshes, and their result is printed at the top of the screen ("all three tie-outs: OK"). If a check fails, the affected tiles still show, greyed, with "not reconciled" and no cause, so nobody acts on a number that might be a data problem.

I start with a table of who looks, how often, and what decision they are about to make, and I only build tiles that answer a question on that table. Then I write a definition card for each of the twelve measurements, with the fraction, the owner, the plan, and the source, and get finance and operations to sign them before any pipeline exists. The data is the scans: fourteen per package, folded once a day into four tables, one row per package, per trailer trip, per van route, per building-day. Three tie-outs run before every refresh and print at the top of the screen. There are three screens: today, which is counts and clocks for the people on the dock; yesterday, which is causes for the directors; and this week, which is the scorecard and the mix-versus-rate bridge generated, not typed. Every tile drills to the list of packages behind it. I build the weekly screen first because that is where decisions are made and trust is earned, the hourly screen last because it is the most fragile, and alerts after that, with a two-day rule so they are not muted. And I measure whether people open it.
