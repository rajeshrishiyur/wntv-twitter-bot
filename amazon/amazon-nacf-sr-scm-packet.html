Why was this important? What was the goal? Every unit stowed twice is cube paid for twice and a transfer waiting to happen. Action probes

The promise-at-risk ranking became the standing allocation priority, and structural stockouts were down twenty percent over the engagement. Afterward I wrote a scheduler heartbeat and a DST checklist into peak readiness. I never want a second 4 AM."

Supply · Demand · Capacity. Demand: peak-day orders by promise tier and cutoff. Supply: inventory by DC and store — adequate, as it turned out. Capacity: two kinds — the physical pick/pack hours by node, which were fine, and the planning-system capacity to assign orders to nodes, which had gone to zero. What bound: a false capacity signal. The lesson for a capacity planner: when the plan says "capacity is short," first prove the shortage is physical.

What was innovative. The standard move at 4 AM is FIFO — clear the oldest backorders first — and a blanket promise downgrade to stop the bleeding. I ranked by dollar-weighted hours-to-cutoff so the scarce decision bandwidth protected the most promise per minute, and I classified every available action as a one-way or two-way door before taking it. The timestamped log turned a firefight into a dataset.

Situation probes

Why important? Cost of nothing? Every oversell was a cancelled order and a customer who did not trust the site; every return promised before grading was a damaged unit shipped to a second customer. Finance was issuing credit on units the floor had not touched.

What was the initial scope? Configure S/4HANA to match the legacy definitions. I changed it to: define first, configure second, because the legacy definitions were the problem.

Action probes

Why a signed policy before configuration? Most oversell bugs are vocabulary bugs. A config change nobody signed becomes next quarter's argument. Once the sentence was signed, the design arguments got small — the plant and the STOs were just the implementation of a sentence everyone had agreed to.

Why a separate returns plant? That's heavy. Because a returned unit is not the same supply as a new one until someone has graded it, and the only way to make the system enforce that was to make it physically and logically elsewhere. The heaviness is the point: nothing in that plant is promiseable until it leaves.

Why pick-reserve for RTV? So finance's credit memo could not run ahead of the physical unit. The reservation ties the money to the pick. Before, credit was issued on paper and the unit was sometimes still on a store shelf.

Why rollback as a number? A rollback that is a paragraph gets debated at 2 AM by tired people. A number gets called by whoever is on the bridge. The triggers existed so the weekend could start.

How did you keep sixty stakeholders from re-litigating reds? Frozen metrics in the weekly pack. A red could not be made green by changing the chart; it could only be made green by moving the number.

What did you do versus the team? Wrote the stock-state definitions and the failure modes; designed the returns plant and the pick-reserve RTV model; owned the RAID, the pack, the runbook, and the rollback triggers; made the MCS retirement call. SAP and the integrator configured.

Results probes

Walk me through the ATS computation. Supply basis is WMS physical on-hand in sellable locations. Subtract what is already promised — pack reserve for stores, e-comm pick reserve and pack reserve for dotcom — and subtract shipped-not-billed because it has physically left. Add back mech reserve, units parked in automation buffers that WMS flags as reserved but nobody has promised, and lost-and-found, units physically present that will be sellable once re-binned. That is ATS, split by channel policy. Then reconcile: SAP supply is unrestricted minus SAP-side reservations; WMS supply is basis plus shipped-not-billed, because SAP still carries it until the goods issue posts. The difference is the OOB.

Why add back shipped-not-billed for the SAP comparison but subtract it from ATS? Two different questions. ATS asks "what can I promise" — SNB is gone, so it is out. The OOB asks "do the two books agree" — SAP has not posted the goods issue, so SAP still holds it; to compare like with like I add it back on the WMS side. If SNB ages past 24 hours it stops being a timing difference and becomes a defect with an owner.

What did the OOB usually come from? Timing — IDocs in flight at snapshot — was the biggest bucket and self-cleared by morning. Then one-sided adjustments, SNB ageing, and lost-and-found not re-binned. The nightly pack showed the buckets; a bucket that repeated three nights became a defect, not a nightly fix.

How did you verify "non-sellable promises → zero"? Promise log joined to stock state at promise time, daily in hypercare. The count of promises against QI, blocked, returns-plant or lost-and-found stock: zero.

How did you know the in-transit rule was wrong? Sellable units by channel fell while units in transit by lane rose. Two lines diverging is the signal. Fix: a per-lane in-transit promise window from measured lane transit — the 200-lane idea applied to promising instead of planning.

What did the wrong rule cost? Weeks of dotcom running with fewer sellable units than it physically had. I will not quote a dollar I did not measure; I can say the recovery was visible in sellable units the week the window went live.

Compared to goal? Definitions signed: yes. Returns first-class: yes. Zero incidents: yes. Rollback never fired: yes. MCS off: yes. One rule wrong and corrected: owned.

Challenge

Differently? Set the in-transit window from lane data on day one instead of starting from "never." Conservatism has a price, paid in the same currency as risk. And sample the OMS-to-WMS handshake daily in hypercare from day one.

The follow-up discipline

  • "What did you do, specifically?" One sentence that starts with "I." Name the artifact: the gate, the table, the log, the SQL.
  • "Why not [the obvious alternative]?" Name the cost of the alternative in the same currency as the result.
  • "How did you measure?" Metric, grain, baseline, target. Never "we saw improvement."
  • "What would you do differently?" A real change you have since made. Not "nothing" and not "communicate more."
  • "Walk me through the data pull." Tables joined, grain, filters. If you did not pull it yourself, say who did and what you checked.

7A.0 Supply, demand, capacity — three words, four equations

Say these three words in this order, with these meanings, and the interviewer knows you are speaking the team's language. Demand is units customers will order, by week, by region, by building type, by speed tier — the forecast, with its error. Supply is units arriving to be stowed — vendor inbound, FBA inbound, transfers, returns — and the inventory already on hand. Capacity is what a building can do with those units: store them (cube) and move them (hours through each process path). Demand and supply are flows; inventory is the stock between them; capacity is the pipe and the tank. The plan is the statement that for every building and every week the four equations below hold.

(1) INVENTORY BALANCE
    I(t) = I(t−1) + Supply_in(t) − Demand_out(t) − Shrink(t)
    what you stow this week minus what you ship is the change in what you hold

(2) STORAGE CONSTRAINT                                   (Ceiling_storage ≈ 0.85–0.90)
    I(t) × cube_per_unit(mix)  ≤  UsableCube × Ceiling_storage
    what you hold must fit — at the ceiling, not at 100%

(3) INBOUND THROUGHPUT                                   (steps: unload, receive, decant, stow)
    Supply_in(t)  ≤  min over steps s of [ Hours_s(t) × Rate_s ]
    you can only stow what every inbound step can push in the hours you have

(4) OUTBOUND THROUGHPUT                                  (steps: pick, rebin, pack, SLAM,
   ship-sort)
    Demand_out(t, CPT)  ≤  min over steps s of [ Hours_s(window before CPT) × Rate_s ]
    you can only ship what every outbound step can push BEFORE EACH TRUCK LEAVES

Rate_s is not a constant:
    Rate_s = DesignRate_s × f(utilization) × g(new-hire share) × h(flow evenness)
    f falls past ~85–90% storage utilization (walk, hunt, congestion)
    g ≈ 0.6 → 1.0 over ~2 weeks of ramp;   h < 1 when work arrives in bursts

How to read a gap with these. If (2) fails, you have a storage gap: lower I(t) (days of cover, slow movers out, inbound pushed back) or raise UsableCube (overflow, retrofit, new build). If (3) fails, inbound gap: hours, appointment shaping, IXD absorbs. If (4) fails, outbound gap: hours, placement to buildings with slack, CPT structure. If (1) is drifting up week over week with (2) approaching the ceiling, you have a placement or forecast problem wearing a capacity costume — supply is being sent to a building whose demand does not justify it. Name the equation that failed before you name a lever. That is the whole discipline.

The one sentence: "Demand tells me how many units must leave each building each week; supply tells me how many must arrive; inventory is the difference; capacity is whether the difference fits in the cube and whether the flows fit in the hours — before each truck leaves."

7A.5 Downstream — sort center, delivery station, last mile

  • Sort center. Receives trailers from many FCs, sorts packages by destination delivery station or carrier injection point. Capacity in packages/hour through the sorter and outbound doors by destination. Metrics: sort rate, missort rate, trailer utilization, sort-center CPT attainment to delivery stations.

A region can have FC slack and still miss speed because the delivery stations near the demand are full. Say "FC capacity and downstream capacity have to be planned on the same page" — it is the regional version of "storage and throughput both."

Inbound vs outbound cost, said plainly. Inbound cost per unit (freight + receive + stow) is small per unit but front-loaded and placement-sensitive. Outbound cost per unit (pick + pack + packaging + transportation) is larger and speed-sensitive. Placement is the one decision that moves both: put the unit near its demand and you pay more inbound and less outbound; the capacity plan has to know which side of that trade a region is on.

Four layers, each feeding the next. Build the simplest version of each first; add fidelity where the plan is actually sensitive.

                      the plan is sized to a quantile you choose on purpose

LAYER 2 — PLACEMENT   which building absorbs which demand: minimize inbound + outbound

                      throughput ceilings → units/week per building. The network LP lives

                      answer is plannable

LAYER 3 — BUILDING    per building per week: required hours by step (7A.7) and required
                      cube (7A.3) vs available; gap by currency by step; rate curves as a
                      function of utilization and new-hire share; CPT structure (how many
                      trucks, when) — flow inside the week, not just the weekly total

LAYER 4 — COST        per building per week: fixed + labor + packaging + transport +
                      carrying + defects (7A.8); cost per unit; incremental cost of each
                      lever; the U-curve

OUTPUTS               gap by week × building × currency; ranked lever stack with cost and
                      lead time; triggers; sensitivity — which forecast error, rate miss or

                      the buildings shipped? Where it missed, which layer was wrong —
                      demand, placement, rate, or ceiling?

Three modeling judgments to say out loud. (1) Plan to a quantile, not a point: if you size to P50 you will be short half the time. Decide with Finance which quantile the cost of shortage justifies. (2) Rates are curves, not constants: a model with fixed rates will tell you a 97%-utilized building is fine. It is not. (3) Weekly totals hide CPT misses: a building can hit its weekly number and miss every 6 PM truck. Model at least the daily CPT structure for peak weeks.

What you would ask on day one. What is the current ceiling by building type, who set it, and when was it last re-measured against actuals? Does placement see the throughput ceiling or only storage? Which lever fired last peak that was not in the plan? Where does forecast bias live by region? And: which of these numbers does Finance accept, and which does it dispute?

7A.11 Whiteboard math — the ten computations you must be able to do cold

Each one: the formula, why it is the right formula, a worked number (ILLUSTRATIVE), and what it tells a capacity planner. Do them on paper once before Friday. In the room, say the formula first, then the numbers, then the implication.

1. Peak sizing from a forecast with error — plan to a quantile, not a point

Required capacity at service level α  =  Forecast_P50 × (1 + z_α × CV)
   CV = forecast error (σ ÷ mean) at the planning grain and horizon
   z_0.80 ≈ 0.84   z_0.90 ≈ 1.28   z_0.95 ≈ 1.65

Worked: peak-week P50 = 1.6M units at FC-A; 8-week-ahead CV = 12%
   P80 = 1.6M × (1 + 0.84 × 0.12) = 1.76M
   P90 = 1.6M × (1 + 1.28 × 0.12) = 1.85M
   P95 = 1.6M × (1 + 1.65 × 0.12) = 1.92M

Implication. Sizing to P50 is short half the time. The quantile is a business decision: the cost of being short (premium transport, speed misses, overflow at spot rates) vs the cost of being long (undiluted fixed cost, idle hours). Choose it with Finance, write it down, and size every lever to it. If CV is 12% at 8 weeks and 6% at 2 weeks, the plan can hold reversible levers (hours, placement) at P90 and commit irreversible ones (overflow contracts) at P80, then upgrade as the forecast tightens.

2. Storage required and utilization — the cube currency

Inventory units     = DailyOutbound × DaysOfCover
Cube needed         = Inventory units × cube_per_unit(mix) ÷ BinFillEfficiency
Utilization         = Cube needed ÷ (GrossCube × Ceiling)

Worked (FC-A, same as 7A.6):
   Inventory  = 229K units/day × 18 days = 4.12M units;  × 0.25 cu ft = 1.03M cu ft of product
   Usable     = 2.86M gross bin cu ft × 0.70 bin fill × 0.90 ceiling = 1.80M cu ft
   Utilization = 1.03 ÷ 1.80 = 57%
Sensitivity: +1 day of cover = 229K × 0.25 = 57K cu ft ≈ 3.2 points; +10 days ≈ 32 points → 89%,
             at the ceiling

Implication. Days of cover is the biggest storage lever and it is an inventory-policy decision, not a real-estate one. Three days of cover is ten points of utilization — the difference between comfortable and congested. The capacity planner must own the days-of-cover conversation with inventory planning.

3. Throughput required vs available — the hours currency

Required hours_s   = Units_s ÷ Rate_s
Available hours_s  = Heads_s × ShiftHrs × Shifts/wk × Attendance
Gap_s              = Required − Available ;  binding step = largest positive gap

Worked (FC-A peak week, 1.6M outbound):
   pack: 1.6M ÷ 90/hr = 17,800 hrs needed;  staffed 420 heads × 8 h × 5 days × 0.92 = 15,460 hrs
   gap = 2,340 hrs ≈ 210K units at pack → pack binds even though pick (13,300 hrs needed) is
         staffed to 14,000
Fixes priced in hours:
   +1 shift-day/wk on pack  = 420 × 8 × 0.92 = +3,090 hrs at 1.0× wage
   OT on existing heads     = 2,340 hrs at 1.5× wage
   move 210K units to FC-C  = transport Δ (7A.6: ~$0.40/unit)

Implication. A building's throughput is its slowest step at the hour the truck leaves. Always compute the gap per step; the binding step is where the money goes. Pack binds more often than pick because its rate is lower and stations are fixed.

4. Little's Law — turning a flow metric into a backlog and back

WIP = Throughput × CycleTime        ⇔        CycleTime = WIP ÷ Throughput

Inbound:  215K units/day arriving, dock-to-stow target 24 h
          → WIP allowed on the inbound floor = 215K × 1.0 day = 215K units
          if the floor holds 430K units, dock-to-stow is running 48 h, whatever the dashboard says
Outbound: a CPT with 180K units due in 6 h needs 30K units/h through pack
          at 90 units/h that is 333 pack heads working that CPT

Implication. You can measure cycle time by counting what is on the floor. Dock-to-stow hours, backlog units and receive rate are one relationship, not three metrics; if two are green and one is red, one of the greens is wrong.

5. Congestion — why cost per unit turns up past the ceiling

Queueing approximation:  Wait ∝ ρ ÷ (1 − ρ),  ρ = utilization
   ρ = 0.70 → 2.3    0.85 → 5.7    0.90 → 9.0    0.95 → 19    0.97 → 32   (relative wait)

Storage analog — stow rate falls as empty bins get scarce (illustrative curve):
   Rate(ρ) = DesignRate × [1 − k × max(0, ρ − 0.85)² ÷ 0.0225],  k ≈ 0.3
   ρ = 0.85 → 100% of design    0.90 → 97%    0.95 → 87%    1.00 → 70%

Implication. Going from 90% to 95% utilization doubles waiting; going to 97% triples it again. That is the arithmetic behind "plan to a ceiling, not to 100%." It is also why the last five points of utilization cost more per unit than they save in fixed-cost dilution (7A.8).

6. Fixed-cost dilution vs variable-cost inflation — the U-curve, derived

CPU(ρ) = Fixed ÷ Units(ρ) + VarLabor(ρ) + Packaging + Transport(ρ) + Defects(ρ)
   Fixed ÷ Units falls like 1/ρ
   VarLabor, Defects rise past the ceiling (Rate(ρ) falls, queueing grows)
   Transport rises when CPT misses force premium modes

Worked (7A.8 numbers):  CPU at 60% = $8.75   at 85% = $7.95   at 97% = $8.48
   60 → 85  dilution: fixed/unit $2.02 → $1.43 (−$0.59);  labor/unit $2.38 → $2.27 (−$0.11)
   85 → 97  inflation: fixed/unit $1.43 → $1.25 (−$0.18);  labor +$0.38, transport +$0.25,
            defects +$0.10 (+$0.73 total)  →  net +$0.53 per unit

Implication. The minimum of the curve is the economic ceiling, and it is below the physical ceiling. When Finance asks why you will not plan to 100%, this is the table you show: the last 12 points cost 53 cents a unit.

7. Lever cost per unit of capacity — ranking the stack

Lever cost ($ per unit of gap closed) = Incremental cost ÷ Units of gap it closes
Rank ascending, subject to lead time ≤ weeks remaining to peak

Worked (7A.6 lever stack, 650K/wk gap over a 4-week peak):
   placement shift   $60K/wk × 4 ÷ (150K × 4)   ≈ $0.40/unit    lead 1–2 wks    reversible
   inbound shaping   carrying cost on pulled-forward stock ≈ $0.30/unit   lead 6–8 wks
      reversible
   OT / extra shift  $1.2M ÷ (250K × 4)         ≈ $1.20/unit    lead 4–6 wks    reversible
   peak hiring       $2.0M ÷ (200K × 4)         ≈ $2.50/unit    lead 10–12 wks  semi-reversible
   overflow / 3P     $1.50–3.00/unit all-in                      lead ~8 wks     contractual
   demand shaping    conversion loss — unpriced, so last

Implication. Flow before hours, hours before hiring, hiring before overflow, overflow before the promise — but the ranking is an output of this arithmetic, not a slogan. If placement shift costs $0.40 and the next region has no slack, it drops off the list and hours move up.

8. Build vs no-build — the break-even forecast

Build (annualized)     = Capex ÷ Life + Lease/fixed opex + Fixed labor        ← paid regardless
                         of volume
No-build (annual)      = Σ_weeks [ Overflow$ + Transfers$ + PremiumTransport$
                                   + SpeedDowngrade × ConversionCost + P(peak miss) × PeakMiss$ ]
Break-even demand D*   = the demand at which the two are equal; report it as a forecast quantile

Worked (illustrative): build = $28M/yr all-in
   no-build cost at P50 demand = $19M/yr;  at P75 = $31M/yr;  at P90 = $44M/yr  →  D* ≈ P68
   sizing to P80: the build pays.  sizing to P50: it does not, and overflow is the plan.

Implication. Do not argue "we need the building." Show D*, show where the planning quantile sits, and let the decision fall out. Also show the middle path — retrofit for +20% throughput at a third of the capex — on the same axis.

9. Learning curve and attrition — why peak heads are not peak hours

Effective hours (wk) = Σ_cohorts Heads_c × ShiftHrs × Attendance × Productivity_c(weeks since
                       hire)
   Productivity ≈ 0.60 (wk 1)  0.80 (wk 2)  0.95 (wk 3)  1.00 (wk 4+)      [illustrative]
   Ramp attrition ≈ 10–15% of a cohort → hire ~1.15× the heads you need

Worked: need +700 effective associates in peak week 0
   hire 300 in wk −3 and 500 in wk −2 (800 gross)
   effective in wk 0 ≈ 300 × 0.95 + 500 × 0.80 = 685 → 15 short, borderline → add 50 to the wk −3
                       cohort

Implication. A labor plan in heads is wrong by the learning curve. Plan in effective hours by cohort, and the hiring trigger moves earlier than the heads math suggests.

10. Speed — the CPT cliff and what a late unit costs

A unit that misses its CPT by 1 minute loses a full truck cycle — often a calendar day of promise.
Speed loss (units/day)  = Σ_CPT max(0, Units due at CPT − Pack capacity in the window before CPT)
Cost per missed unit    ≈ premium-transport uplift (if upgraded)
                          or P(cancel | late) × margin + trust (unpriced)

Worked: 18:00 CPT has 180K due;  pack capacity 14:00–18:00 = 4 h × 36K/h = 144K  →  36K miss,
        daily
   60% upgraded to premium at +$2.50 → 21.6K × $2.50 = $54K/day in transport alone, before cancels

Implication. Weekly utilization can be 80% and the 18:00 CPT can miss every day. Capacity must be planned at the CPT window grain for peak weeks. This is Case C in Section 7, in numbers.
