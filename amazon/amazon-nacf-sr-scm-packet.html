S3 — Two hundred lanes: promise at the tail, release the inventory (Elementum)

LPs: Customer Obsession · Ownership · Dive Deep · Think Big · Frugality. Maps here: turning measured data into planning parameters; the kind of input SCOT models consume and S&OP depends on.

"A planner asked me for a lead-time-variability report. The room had normalized carrier-brochure lead times — one optimistic number per lane, never measured — and the inventory plan ran on them. I treated the brochure as a lie the business was paying for in safety stock. I pulled shipment history across two hundred lanes and built mean, spread and tail per lane, then moved every planning lead time to the tail — the p99 — not the average. Ninety-nine percent lead-time accuracy held. Because the measured spread was tighter than assumed on most lanes, 580,000 units and about a million and a half of safety stock came free. Then a drift flag per lane, so a widening spread triggered a review instead of a silent re-plan. The ask was a report. The product was lane-level planning as a system."

What did YOU do? Pulled the history myself, built the distributions, pitched leadership with mockups, productized it, designed the drift flag.

Why the tail and not the mean? A lead time set at the mean is a coin flip — in a right-skewed lane, roughly two in five shipments arrive after it. The p99 is the smallest number of days by which 99 of 100 have already arrived. I set it off the empirical quantile per lane, not a normal approximation, because the normal understates the tail.

How did you measure? Actual arrival inside the planned lead time, by lane, by week — 99%. Safety stock released only on lanes where measured spread was tighter than assumed.

Differently? Start every new domain with a measurement audit in week one instead of discovering the brochure problem mid-project.

Data pull? Shipment-level: lane key, ship date, arrival date, carrier, source of the transit number (brochure vs scan).

7A.5 Downstream — sort center, delivery station, last mile

  • Sort center. Receives trailers from many FCs, sorts packages by destination delivery station or carrier injection point. Capacity in packages/hour through the sorter and outbound doors by destination. Metrics: sort rate, missort rate, trailer utilization, sort-center CPT attainment to delivery stations.
  • Delivery station. Receives sorted packages overnight, sorts to routes, dispatches to delivery partners. Capacity in packages/day by route count × packages/route. Metrics: packages per route, dispatch on time, delivery success rate.
  • Why NACF capacity planning cares. FC outbound capacity is only useful if there is downstream sort and delivery capacity on the same lanes on the same night. A region can have FC slack and still miss speed because the delivery stations near the demand are full. Say "FC capacity and downstream capacity have to be planned on the same page" — it is the regional version of "storage and throughput both."

7A.10 How you would model it

Four layers, each feeding the next. Build the simplest version of each first; add fidelity where the plan is actually sensitive.

LAYER 1 — Demand          units by week × region × building type × speed tier, from S&OP; peak factors by week;
                          range (P10/P50/P90), not a point — the plan is sized to a quantile you choose on purpose

LAYER 2 — Placement       which building absorbs which demand; an optimization (minimize inbound + outbound
                          transport + transfer cost subject to each building's storage and throughput ceilings);
                          outputs units/week per building. This is where the network LP lives (SCOT partner owns
                          the engine; you own the constraints and whether the answer is plannable)

LAYER 3 — Building        per building per week: required hours by step (7A.7), required cube (7A.3), vs available;
                          gap by currency by step; rate curves as a function of utilization and new-hire share;
                          CPT structure (how many trucks, when) — flow inside the week, not just the weekly total

LAYER 4 — Cost            per building per week: fixed + labor + packaging + transport + carrying + defects (7A.8);
                          cost per unit; incremental cost of each lever; the U-curve

OUTPUTS                   gap by week by building by currency; ranked lever stack with cost and lead time; triggers;
                          sensitivity (what forecast error, rate miss, or launch slip breaks the plan)

VALIDATION                backtest on last year's weeks: did the model's "available" match what the buildings actually
                          shipped? Where it missed, which layer was wrong — demand, placement, rate, or ceiling?

Three modeling judgments to say out loud. (1) Plan to a quantile, not a point: if you size to P50 you will be short half the time. Decide with Finance which quantile the cost of shortage justifies. (2) Rates are curves, not constants: a model with fixed rates will tell you a 97%-utilized building is fine. It is not. (3) Weekly totals hide CPT misses: a building can hit its weekly number and miss every 6 PM truck. Model at least the daily CPT structure for peak weeks.

What you would ask on day one. What is the current ceiling by building type, who set it, and when was it last re-measured against actuals? Does placement see the throughput ceiling or only storage? Which lever fired last peak that was not in the plan? Where does forecast bias live by region? And: which of these numbers does Finance accept, and which does it dispute?

7. The functional block — capacity planning

You will get at least one of these. Format they expect: clarify out loud (60–90 seconds) → structure (60 seconds) → a math checkpoint → recommendation with owners, triggers and risks. Say "both constraints — storage and throughput" early in every answer. Label every number you make up as illustrative, and say what you would replace it with on day one.

Primer: the shape of a capacity plan

Demand side          units by week × building type × speed tier, with a peak factor
                     (forecast owner: S&OP / SCOT; your job is to interrogate its grain and mix)

Required capacity    storage:    units on hand ≈ daily outbound units × days of cover → cube needed
                     throughput: units/week each process step must push (inbound, stow, pick, pack, ship)
                     both, by building type; the binding one is the one you plan to

Available capacity   buildings × design rate × a utilization ceiling (congestion starts before 100%)
                     minus planned downtime, retrofits, launches still ramping

Gap by week          required − available, by region, by type; where and when it binds

Levers, cheapest and fastest first
   1. flow: move units — placement / allocation to buildings with slack (days to weeks)
   2. hours: shifts, overtime, labor plan (weeks)
   3. inventory policy: pull inbound forward or push it back; trim slow movers (weeks)
   4. overflow: 3P / temporary space (weeks to months)
   5. retrofit / automation: more throughput in the same cube (quarters)
   6. expansion / new build: more cube (OP cycle; years)
   7. demand shaping: speed tier, promise, selection (the lever nobody wants to admit)

Plan                 named owner per lever, trigger that fires it, review cadence, and what you will not do

Case A — "Build next year's capacity plan for a region."

Clarify (say these out loud). Which building types are in scope? Is the demand forecast handed to me or do I own it? What is the peak factor and what week does it peak? What are the policy ceilings on utilization? Which launches and retrofits are already committed? What is the planning grain — week, building, type?

Structure. The primer, top to bottom. Spend the most time on the gap-by-week and the lever ranking.

Math checkpoint (illustrative). "If a building ships 1.0M units a week at average and the peak factor is 1.6, peak week is 1.6M. If its throughput design rate is 1.4M and the ceiling I am allowed to plan to is 90%, usable is 1.26M. The gap is 340K units in peak week. Flow first: how much of that can neighboring buildings absorb inside their own ceilings? Then hours. Overflow only for what is left. I would replace every one of those numbers with the S&OP demand file and the engineering design rates on day one."

Recommendation. A one-page plan: gap by week by region, the lever stack with owners, the triggers that promote a lever (e.g., "if the week-8 forecast is still 5% above plan, overflow contracts sign"), and the two things you will not do — plan to 100% of design rate, or pad every building's forecast to hide a placement problem. Risk: forecast mix shifts between types; you plan by type, not by total.

Case B — "Peak forecast just came in 10% above capacity. Go."

  1. Verify the number before you spend on it. Grain and mix: is the 10% real demand, or a type-mix shift that makes one building type bind while another has slack? Same cohort weights as last forecast?
  2. Find where it binds. Storage or throughput? Inbound dock, stow, pick, pack, or outbound? One region or network? "A network 10% is usually three buildings at 25% and the rest fine."
  3. Cheapest lever first, with a trigger. Placement and allocation shifts this week. Labor plan next. Pull slow-moving inbound back; pull fast-moving inbound forward. Overflow for the residual. Speed tier only if the customer promise is the last protection.
  4. Publish the plan with a date on every lever and a freeze on non-essential change into peak — the UDC October-to-December freeze.
  5. What you will not do. Spread a national pad across every building. That hides the cell that is actually breaking and spends cost everywhere.

Story to attach: S2 — rank by promise-at-risk, rebalance across nodes, timestamped log, then a mechanism so you never discover the regime in November again.

Case C — "Utilization looks fine but speed is missing."

Utilization is a weekly average. Speed misses live at the hour, the dock door, the sort type, the cutoff. Change the grain: by building by hour by process step. Check mix: did fast-promise demand land in buildings far from the customer? Check cutoff cliffs: units arriving an hour late lose a calendar day. Story: S5 — the reports were wrong in both directions because they measured the wrong grain. Line to say: "If I am still talking about network utilization after two follow-ups, I am failing the question."

Case D — "Finance says the new building is not justified."

Do not argue. Price both columns. Cost of the build, annualized. Cost of not having it: overflow contracts, transfers and split shipments, speed downgrades and their conversion cost, labor premiums, the risk-adjusted cost of a peak miss. Show the sensitivity: at what forecast does the build break even? Offer the middle path — retrofit or flow redesign — priced in the same table. Story: S7 — the two-column table that flipped an eighteen-month plan; Finance attacked it and it survived. Line: "I would rather lose this argument to a better number than win it with a louder voice."

Case E — "Define the metrics for a network capacity program."

Outputs (what the business feels): capacity delivered vs plan by week; utilization vs ceiling by type; cost per unit; customer speed attainment. Inputs (what a named owner can move this week): launch and retrofit milestones on dated gates; placement compliance; forecast accuracy at the planning grain; labor plan attainment; inbound ASN accuracy. Mechanism: a weekly review where reds arrive pre-decomposed — which input moved, whose lever, what correction, verified by the same number next week — and a correction-of-error write-up for any miss above a published threshold that ends in a mechanism change, not "try harder." Line: "Inputs without outputs are a hobby. Outputs without inputs are weather."

Fermi, if they throw one

  • State assumptions. Compute out loud. Label it illustrative. Say what you would replace it with.
  • "How many units does a large sortable FC ship in a week?" Structure: shifts × hours × headcount × units per hour, cross-checked against outbound dock capacity. Do not quote an Amazon number you have not seen. "I would take the design rate from Ops Engineering on day one."

  • "Cost of a 4-point speed miss at peak?" Volume × delta × cost per late unit, plus the unpriced trust cost. Name the grain. Never say "huge."
