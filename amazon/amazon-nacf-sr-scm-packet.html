Situation. "In 2022 Flexport had just launched Shipment Visibility — tracking containers Flexport did not forward, so a client could see all their freight in one place. Task. I took it. Grow from seven to sixty-eight customers, three times the monthly active users, double the weekly volume — with cost to serve going down and accuracy going up. Which meant the growth had to be absorbed by automation, not by hiring operators.

Action. Four things. First, I made the ticket the sensor. Every Zendesk ticket was one container the machine could not track. So tickets per active container became the input metric that measured the human-in-the-loop, and automation rate — containers tracking-initiated with no human touch over containers submitted — became the number the engineering backlog was ranked on: tickets times cost per ticket, carrier by carrier, terminal by terminal, trans-Pacific eastbound and trans-Atlantic westbound lanes first because that is where the volume was. Third, I decided where the human loop applied: client tracking opened tickets, because a paying customer needs a consistent experience and manual updates fill the automation gaps; the public tracking site did not, because the bar was lower and the volume would have buried Data Services. Fourth, I gated onboarding: six graduation criteria a wave had to clear at current load — data quality at or above ninety against source, ETA accuracy at or above eighty, verification SLA, engagement, stability, CSAT — and when wave three missed on ETA accuracy I held it, publicly, with the source that caused it and the date we would re-test.

Supply · Demand · Capacity. Demand: tracking requests — containers clients submit, growing 5× as waves boarded. Supply: the data — carrier APIs and EDI, terminal and port feeds, later CargoSmart — the raw material; a missing or wrong feed is the defect. Capacity: the system's ability to turn a submitted container into trustworthy milestones = automated capacity (the Visibility Service, per carrier × terminal × milestone) + human capacity (Data Services operators, metered by tickets). What bound: capacity at quality. Waves metered demand to measured capacity; the backlog grew automated capacity where tickets said the human loop was most expensive; coverage-with-human minus coverage-automated was the human loop's share, and it fell every week. This is a capacity plan with two currencies — automated and manual — the way an FC plan has cube and hours.

What was innovative. The usual plan was a headcount curve for Data Services and a dashboard someday. I did five things differently. (1) The ticket as the automation-gap sensor — a Zendesk ticket is a container the machine failed; tickets per active container made the human-in-the-loop a measured, shrinking input metric instead of a department. (2) The WBR before the pipeline — I ran the review by hand on the engineering org's conventions so the definitions were debugged on paper before they were coded, then wrote the BRD and priced the status quo in our own labor so the data-infra team prioritized it themselves. (3) Where the human loop applies is a product decision — tickets for client tracking, none for public tracking, source toggles per calling product. (4) An ROI-ranked automation backlog — tickets × cost per ticket by carrier × terminal × milestone, TPEB and TAWB first, "easy to get the data" only as a tiebreaker. (5) Graduation gates — admission to the platform earned at current load, so cost per container fell as the base grew instead of rising.

Situation probes

Why was this important? Cost of doing nothing? Visibility was free to the client by design — the E-Team decided to give it away to win the rest of the client's freight. A free product with a sixty-six-cent human cost per container scales into a loss the day it succeeds. Sixty customers at pilot economics was a support organization, not a product.

Who set the goals? The E-Team set Goal 1 (cost-to-serve) and the direction; sales set the 68 and the date. I added the two conditions — cost down, accuracy up — and owned the WBR nobody had been assigned.

What was the initial scope you were handed? "Report progress." I made it "own the metric tree, the cadence, and the backlog ranking." The E-Team action said "publish a WBR"; I treated the WBR as the operating system of the ramp, not a report on it.

Who was the customer? Importers and exporters — enterprise and global key accounts first, SMB later through the public site. The internal customer was Data Services, whose work I was trying to shrink; I told them that on day one.

Action probes

Why make the ticket the metric? Because it already existed, it was already one-to-one with an automation failure, and it carried the labels — carrier, terminal, lane, reason. Inventing a new "automation failure" event would have taken a quarter; the ticket was free and honest.

Why run the WBR by hand first? That is two hours a week of a program manager. Because the expensive part of a WBR is the definitions, not the pipeline. A quarter of running it by hand changed four of the seven definitions — "active container," for one — before anyone had coded them. Then the BRD had a fixed target and the build was cheap. Automating a review you have not run yet automates your mistakes.

Why not simply hire Data Services operators to the volume? The volume was going to grow five times. Operators scale linearly with containers; automation scales with carriers. There are a few hundred carriers and terminals and millions of containers. The only cost curve that bends is the one indexed to carriers.

Why tickets for client tracking and none for public tracking? A paying client needs a consistent experience — manual updates fill the automation gaps. A public request is a lead, not a commitment; routing it to an operator would have consumed the human loop on people who were not customers yet, and the public site's job was to tell us where demand and coverage were, not to be perfect.

Why TPEB and TAWB first? Volume. The ROI stack is tickets × cost; the trans-Pacific eastbound and trans-Atlantic westbound lanes carried most of the containers, so a carrier fixed there retired more tickets per engineering week. "Easy to get the data" was only the tiebreaker, because easy data on an empty lane retires nothing.

Why not buy Project44's data? The E-Team's call, which I agreed with: visibility was a competitive advantage, and buying the data would have bought the gap rather than closed it. We measured ourselves against them instead and invested in our own sources, Data Services included, until automation took over.

What did you do versus the team? I defined the metric tree and the roll-ups, ran the Monday review, wrote the BRD and priced it, decided where the human loop applied, ranked the backlog weekly, and made the wave-three hold. Engineering built the source integrations and the kill switch; data infra built the Snowflake feed; Data Services ran the loop.

What decision did you challenge? Hiring support ahead of the ramp — and the assumption that the public site was a distraction. I argued the public site was a cheap volume source for finding where coverage was weak, as long as it created no tickets. The E-Team kept Goal 1 first, and the site launched behind it.

Results probes

How did you compute cost-to-serve? Data Services hours on visibility tickets at a loaded rate, plus the platform and data-source cost attributable to visibility, divided by containers tracked that month. Sixty-six cents at seven customers; three cents at sixty-eight. The method was agreed with finance before the first number was published.

Isn't the 95% mostly denominator — five times the containers? Partly, and I say so. Fixed cost diluted five times would have taken sixty-six cents to roughly thirteen. The rest is the automation rate going from about sixty to ninety-seven percent: tickets fell in absolute terms while volume quintupled. The WBR shows both lines so nobody has to take my word for the split.

How did you measure "system accuracy" — 60 to 90? A weekly audit sample of milestones compared to the source of truth — the carrier or terminal record — share matching within tolerance. Sixty percent when I started; ninety at the end. Not self-reported by the system.

How did you measure coverage, and why two coverage lines? Containers with at least six of eight expected milestones present, over active containers — once counting only automated sources, once counting any source. The gap between the two lines is the human loop. One line would have hidden the thing I was trying to shrink.

Compared to goal? 68: met, two months early. MAU 3×: met. Volume 2×: beaten, 5×. Cost down: −95%. Accuracy: 60 → 90. Response: 72 → 90 at 5× volume. The WBR's own cost: −$103K a year.

Trade-offs? One week on wave three and a hard conversation with sales. The public site launched later than its advocates wanted. Data Services shrank by attrition into other roles, which I had told them would happen.

Challenge

What would you do differently? Build the kill switch per source class before wave one — I knew bad sources existed and let the first miss force it. And land Zendesk in Snowflake in month one instead of month four; I ran the review by hand a quarter longer than the definitions needed.

At Amazon. A capacity plan has a human-in-the-loop too: every manual override of the model's output — a planner moving volume, a site manager rejecting a forecast — is a ticket. I would run the same tree: automation rate of the plan (share of building-weeks planned without override), overrides per building per week as the human-loop cost, coverage of the network by a measured ceiling instead of a nameplate, and a backlog ranked by overrides × cost, so the model earns the buildings back one at a time. The WBR conventions are the same ones this role already runs.

The math — available-to-sell (ATS) and the end-of-day reconciliation

This is the computation the program lived on. S/4HANA is the aggregated book of record (plant / storage-location stock in MM-IM); the Inventory Supply Management (ISM) layer computes supply and demand across store, retail DC and dotcom and publishes ATS by channel; the WMS holds the physical and the reserves. Every night the three had to agree, and the number that proved it was the out-of-balance (OOB).

STEP 1 — SUPPLY BASIS  (from WMS, by SKU × DC, EOD snapshot)
   Supply basis = physical on-hand in sellable locations
                  (excludes QI, blocked, damaged, returns-plant stock)

STEP 2 — AVAILABLE TO SELL  (ISM computes by SKU × DC, then splits by channel policy)
   ATS = Supply basis
         − Pack reserve         store/retail orders waved, picked, at pack (committed)
         − Ecomm pick reserve   dotcom orders allocated, not yet picked
         − Ecomm pack reserve   dotcom orders picked, at pack
         − Shipped-not-billed   left the dock, goods issue not yet posted in SAP
                                (gone physically, still in SAP stock)
         + Mech reserve         units parked in automation buffers — physically
                                present and sellable; WMS shows "reserved"
         + Lost & found         found, not yet re-binned — present, sellable once binned

STEP 3 — RECONCILE TO SAP  (the OOB)
   SAP supply  = SAP unrestricted (MB52 / MARD) − SAP reservations, same SKU × plant
   WMS supply  = Supply basis + Shipped-not-billed
                 (add SNB back: SAP still holds it until GI posts)
   OOB (units) = SAP supply − WMS supply ;   OOB ($) = OOB × standard cost
   Tolerance   = |OOB| ≤ 0.5% of SAP supply per SKU × DC
                 AND  Σ|OOB $| ≤ [materiality] per DC per day
   Buckets     = timing (in-flight IDocs) · SNB > 24 h · adjustment posted one side
                 · receipt posted one side · L&F ageing

WORKED EXAMPLE — one SKU, one DC, one night (illustrative units)
   Supply basis (WMS physical, sellable)     10,000
   − Pack reserve                            −1,200
   − Ecomm pick reserve                        −800
   − Ecomm pack reserve                        −500
   − Shipped-not-billed                        −300
   + Mech reserve                              +150
   + Lost & found                               +50
   = ATS                                      7,400  → split by channel policy
                                                       (dotcom floor, store allocation)
   SAP unrestricted                          10,420
   − SAP reservations (STO / delivery)         −120
   = SAP supply                              10,300
   WMS supply = 10,000 + 300 (SNB)           10,300
   OOB = 10,300 − 10,300 = 0  ✔
   (if SAP showed 10,420 after reservations → OOB = +120
    → classify: 90 timing IDoc, 30 adjustment posted one side)

   reserves     subtracted: already promised to someone — selling them twice is the oversell

   mech + L&F   added back: physically here and sellable — WMS "reserved" ≠ "promised"
   returns, QI, blocked   NOT in supply basis at all — nothing is promiseable until

Action probes

Why p99 and not p95 or the mean? The mean is a coin flip — in a right-skewed lane, roughly two in five shipments arrive after it. p95 leaves one shipment in twenty late, which at our order frequency was a stockout a month on a busy lane. 99% was the service level the business had already committed to; I made the lead time honor it. On lanes where the cost of carrying justified less, the method allows a lower quantile per lane — the point is to choose it, not inherit it.

Why empirical quantile and not a normal distribution? Transit times are right-skewed — a shipment can be very late but not very early. A normal fit understates the tail, which is exactly where the stockouts live. I checked: on long ocean lanes the normal p99 was days short of the empirical p99.

How much history per lane? Seasonality? Enough shipments per lane to make a p99 meaningful — thin lanes were pooled with similar lanes (same origin region, mode, carrier) and flagged as lower confidence. Peak-season transits were kept as their own regime where the data supported it; the drift flag catches the seasonal widening on lanes where it did not.

What did you do versus the team? I pulled the data, built the distributions, chose the quantile rule, pitched leadership, wrote the release rule with finance, and designed the drift flag. Engineering productized the pipeline from my spec.

How did you get planners to trust a number that cut their safety stock? I showed each planner their own lanes: brochure number, measured p99, actual late arrivals under each. Where the data said "add stock," I added it first. Credibility came from the lanes where I gave inventory back.

What did you challenge? The assumption that the carrier's number was a fact. And the plan to ship a report — a report would have been read once.

Results probes

How did you measure 99%? Actual arrivals inside the planned lead time, by lane, by week, from scan data — the same data the distributions came from, held out forward in time.

How did you compute $1.5M? Safety-stock units before and after, by SKU-lane, valued at standard cost. Released only on lanes where the measured spread was tighter than the assumed spread — the finance-signed rule — so no lane's release depends on a judgment call.

Was the whole 580K units a release? Did any lanes get more stock? The 580K is net. A minority of lanes got more stock because their measured tail was wider than the brochure. I would rather not quote the lane count without the file in front of me.

Trade-off? Lanes with wider tails carried more inventory than before. That was the right trade: we had been running them short and calling it luck.

Compared to goal? 99% accuracy: met. Less safety stock, not more: met, net $1.5M. Permanence: drift flag live.

What would you do differently? Start every new domain with a measurement audit in week one — "which planning parameters are measured, and which are inherited?" — instead of discovering the brochure problem mid-project. I have done that on every program since, including S/4HANA.

7A.0 Supply, demand, capacity — three words, four equations

Say these three words in this order, with these meanings, and the interviewer knows you are speaking the team's language. Demand is units customers will order, by week, by region, by building type, by speed tier — the forecast, with its error. Supply is units arriving to be stowed — vendor inbound, FBA inbound, transfers, returns — and the inventory already on hand. Capacity is what a building can do with those units: store them (cube) and move them (hours through each process path). Demand and supply are flows; inventory is the stock between them; capacity is the pipe and the tank. The plan is the statement that for every building and every week the four equations below hold.

(1) INVENTORY BALANCE
    I(t) = I(t−1) + Supply_in(t) − Demand_out(t) − Shrink(t)
    what you stow this week minus what you ship is the change in what you hold

(2) STORAGE CONSTRAINT                                   (Ceiling_storage ≈ 0.85–0.90)
    I(t) × cube_per_unit(mix)  ≤  UsableCube × Ceiling_storage
    what you hold must fit — at the ceiling, not at 100%

(3) INBOUND THROUGHPUT                                   (steps: unload, receive, decant, stow)
    Supply_in(t)  ≤  min over steps s of [ Hours_s(t) × Rate_s ]
    you can only stow what every inbound step can push in the hours you have

(4) OUTBOUND THROUGHPUT                                  (steps: pick, rebin, pack, SLAM,
   ship-sort)
    Demand_out(t, CPT)  ≤  min over steps s of [ Hours_s(window before CPT) × Rate_s ]
    you can only ship what every outbound step can push BEFORE EACH TRUCK LEAVES

Rate_s is not a constant:
    Rate_s = DesignRate_s × f(utilization) × g(new-hire share) × h(flow evenness)
    f falls past ~85–90% storage utilization (walk, hunt, congestion)
    g ≈ 0.6 → 1.0 over ~2 weeks of ramp;   h < 1 when work arrives in bursts

How to read a gap with these. If (2) fails, you have a storage gap: lower I(t) (days of cover, slow movers out, inbound pushed back) or raise UsableCube (overflow, retrofit, new build). If (3) fails, inbound gap: hours, appointment shaping, IXD absorbs. If (4) fails, outbound gap: hours, placement to buildings with slack, CPT structure. If (1) is drifting up week over week with (2) approaching the ceiling, you have a placement or forecast problem wearing a capacity costume — supply is being sent to a building whose demand does not justify it. Name the equation that failed before you name a lever. That is the whole discipline.

The one sentence: "Demand tells me how many units must leave each building each week; supply tells me how many must arrive; inventory is the difference; capacity is whether the difference fits in the cube and whether the flows fit in the hours — before each truck leaves."

7A.5 Downstream — sort center, delivery station, last mile

  • Sort center. Receives trailers from many FCs, sorts packages by destination delivery station or carrier injection point. Capacity in packages/hour through the sorter and outbound doors by destination. Metrics: sort rate, missort rate, trailer utilization, sort-center CPT attainment to delivery stations.
  • Delivery station. Receives sorted packages overnight, sorts to routes, dispatches to delivery partners. Capacity in packages/day by route count × packages/route. Metrics: packages per route, dispatch on time, delivery success rate.
  • Why NACF capacity planning cares. FC outbound capacity is only useful if there is downstream sort and delivery capacity on the same lanes on the same night. A region can have FC slack and still miss speed because the delivery stations near the demand are full. Say "FC capacity and downstream capacity have to be planned on the same page" — it is the regional version of "storage and throughput both."

Three modeling judgments to say out loud. (1) Plan to a quantile, not a point: if you size to P50 you will be short half the time. Decide with Finance which quantile the cost of shortage justifies. (3) Weekly totals hide CPT misses: a building can hit its weekly number and miss every 6 PM truck. Model at least the daily CPT structure for peak weeks.

7A.11 Whiteboard math — the ten computations you must be able to do cold

Each one: the formula, why it is the right formula, a worked number (ILLUSTRATIVE), and what it tells a capacity planner. Do them on paper once before Friday. In the room, say the formula first, then the numbers, then the implication.

1. Peak sizing from a forecast with error — plan to a quantile, not a point

Required capacity at service level α  =  Forecast_P50 × (1 + z_α × CV)
   CV = forecast error (σ ÷ mean) at the planning grain and horizon
   z_0.80 ≈ 0.84   z_0.90 ≈ 1.28   z_0.95 ≈ 1.65

Worked: peak-week P50 = 1.6M units at FC-A; 8-week-ahead CV = 12%
   P80 = 1.6M × (1 + 0.84 × 0.12) = 1.76M
   P90 = 1.6M × (1 + 1.28 × 0.12) = 1.85M
   P95 = 1.6M × (1 + 1.65 × 0.12) = 1.92M

Implication. Sizing to P50 is short half the time. The quantile is a business decision: the cost of being short (premium transport, speed misses, overflow at spot rates) vs the cost of being long (undiluted fixed cost, idle hours). Choose it with Finance, write it down, and size every lever to it. If CV is 12% at 8 weeks and 6% at 2 weeks, the plan can hold reversible levers (hours, placement) at P90 and commit irreversible ones (overflow contracts) at P80, then upgrade as the forecast tightens.

2. Storage required and utilization — the cube currency

Inventory units     = DailyOutbound × DaysOfCover
Cube needed         = Inventory units × cube_per_unit(mix) ÷ BinFillEfficiency
Utilization         = Cube needed ÷ (GrossCube × Ceiling)

Worked (FC-A, same as 7A.6):
   Inventory  = 229K units/day × 18 days = 4.12M units;  × 0.25 cu ft = 1.03M cu ft of product
   Usable     = 2.86M gross bin cu ft × 0.70 bin fill × 0.90 ceiling = 1.80M cu ft
   Utilization = 1.03 ÷ 1.80 = 57%
Sensitivity: +1 day of cover = 229K × 0.25 = 57K cu ft ≈ 3.2 points; +10 days ≈ 32 points → 89%,
             at the ceiling

Implication. Days of cover is the biggest storage lever and it is an inventory-policy decision, not a real-estate one. Three days of cover is ten points of utilization — the difference between comfortable and congested. The capacity planner must own the days-of-cover conversation with inventory planning.

3. Throughput required vs available — the hours currency

Required hours_s   = Units_s ÷ Rate_s
Available hours_s  = Heads_s × ShiftHrs × Shifts/wk × Attendance
Gap_s              = Required − Available ;  binding step = largest positive gap

Worked (FC-A peak week, 1.6M outbound):
   pack: 1.6M ÷ 90/hr = 17,800 hrs needed;  staffed 420 heads × 8 h × 5 days × 0.92 = 15,460 hrs
   gap = 2,340 hrs ≈ 210K units at pack → pack binds even though pick (13,300 hrs needed) is
         staffed to 14,000
Fixes priced in hours:
   +1 shift-day/wk on pack  = 420 × 8 × 0.92 = +3,090 hrs at 1.0× wage
   OT on existing heads     = 2,340 hrs at 1.5× wage
   move 210K units to FC-C  = transport Δ (7A.6: ~$0.40/unit)

Implication. A building's throughput is its slowest step at the hour the truck leaves. Always compute the gap per step; the binding step is where the money goes. Pack binds more often than pick because its rate is lower and stations are fixed.

4. Little's Law — turning a flow metric into a backlog and back

WIP = Throughput × CycleTime        ⇔        CycleTime = WIP ÷ Throughput

Inbound:  215K units/day arriving, dock-to-stow target 24 h
          → WIP allowed on the inbound floor = 215K × 1.0 day = 215K units
          if the floor holds 430K units, dock-to-stow is running 48 h, whatever the dashboard says
Outbound: a CPT with 180K units due in 6 h needs 30K units/h through pack
          at 90 units/h that is 333 pack heads working that CPT

Implication. You can measure cycle time by counting what is on the floor. Dock-to-stow hours, backlog units and receive rate are one relationship, not three metrics; if two are green and one is red, one of the greens is wrong.

5. Congestion — why cost per unit turns up past the ceiling

Queueing approximation:  Wait ∝ ρ ÷ (1 − ρ),  ρ = utilization
   ρ = 0.70 → 2.3    0.85 → 5.7    0.90 → 9.0    0.95 → 19    0.97 → 32   (relative wait)

Storage analog — stow rate falls as empty bins get scarce (illustrative curve):
   Rate(ρ) = DesignRate × [1 − k × max(0, ρ − 0.85)² ÷ 0.0225],  k ≈ 0.3
   ρ = 0.85 → 100% of design    0.90 → 97%    0.95 → 87%    1.00 → 70%

Implication. Going from 90% to 95% utilization doubles waiting; going to 97% triples it again. That is the arithmetic behind "plan to a ceiling, not to 100%." It is also why the last five points of utilization cost more per unit than they save in fixed-cost dilution (7A.8).

6. Fixed-cost dilution vs variable-cost inflation — the U-curve, derived

CPU(ρ) = Fixed ÷ Units(ρ) + VarLabor(ρ) + Packaging + Transport(ρ) + Defects(ρ)
   Fixed ÷ Units falls like 1/ρ
   VarLabor, Defects rise past the ceiling (Rate(ρ) falls, queueing grows)
   Transport rises when CPT misses force premium modes

Worked (7A.8 numbers):  CPU at 60% = $8.75   at 85% = $7.95   at 97% = $8.48
   60 → 85  dilution: fixed/unit $2.02 → $1.43 (−$0.59);  labor/unit $2.38 → $2.27 (−$0.11)
   85 → 97  inflation: fixed/unit $1.43 → $1.25 (−$0.18);  labor +$0.38, transport +$0.25,
            defects +$0.10 (+$0.73 total)  →  net +$0.53 per unit

Implication. The minimum of the curve is the economic ceiling, and it is below the physical ceiling. When Finance asks why you will not plan to 100%, this is the table you show: the last 12 points cost 53 cents a unit.

7. Lever cost per unit of capacity — ranking the stack

Lever cost ($ per unit of gap closed) = Incremental cost ÷ Units of gap it closes
Rank ascending, subject to lead time ≤ weeks remaining to peak

Worked (7A.6 lever stack, 650K/wk gap over a 4-week peak):
   placement shift   $60K/wk × 4 ÷ (150K × 4)   ≈ $0.40/unit    lead 1–2 wks    reversible
   inbound shaping   carrying cost on pulled-forward stock ≈ $0.30/unit   lead 6–8 wks
      reversible
   OT / extra shift  $1.2M ÷ (250K × 4)         ≈ $1.20/unit    lead 4–6 wks    reversible
   peak hiring       $2.0M ÷ (200K × 4)         ≈ $2.50/unit    lead 10–12 wks  semi-reversible
   overflow / 3P     $1.50–3.00/unit all-in                      lead ~8 wks     contractual
   demand shaping    conversion loss — unpriced, so last

Implication. Flow before hours, hours before hiring, hiring before overflow, overflow before the promise — but the ranking is an output of this arithmetic, not a slogan. If placement shift costs $0.40 and the next region has no slack, it drops off the list and hours move up.

8. Build vs no-build — the break-even forecast

Build (annualized)     = Capex ÷ Life + Lease/fixed opex + Fixed labor        ← paid regardless
                         of volume
No-build (annual)      = Σ_weeks [ Overflow$ + Transfers$ + PremiumTransport$
                                   + SpeedDowngrade × ConversionCost + P(peak miss) × PeakMiss$ ]
Break-even demand D*   = the demand at which the two are equal; report it as a forecast quantile

Worked (illustrative): build = $28M/yr all-in
   no-build cost at P50 demand = $19M/yr;  at P75 = $31M/yr;  at P90 = $44M/yr  →  D* ≈ P68
   sizing to P80: the build pays.  sizing to P50: it does not, and overflow is the plan.

Implication. Do not argue "we need the building." Show D*, show where the planning quantile sits, and let the decision fall out. Also show the middle path — retrofit for +20% throughput at a third of the capex — on the same axis.

9. Learning curve and attrition — why peak heads are not peak hours

Effective hours (wk) = Σ_cohorts Heads_c × ShiftHrs × Attendance × Productivity_c(weeks since
                       hire)
   Productivity ≈ 0.60 (wk 1)  0.80 (wk 2)  0.95 (wk 3)  1.00 (wk 4+)      [illustrative]
   Ramp attrition ≈ 10–15% of a cohort → hire ~1.15× the heads you need

Worked: need +700 effective associates in peak week 0
   hire 300 in wk −3 and 500 in wk −2 (800 gross)
   effective in wk 0 ≈ 300 × 0.95 + 500 × 0.80 = 685 → 15 short, borderline → add 50 to the wk −3
                       cohort

Implication. A labor plan in heads is wrong by the learning curve. Plan in effective hours by cohort, and the hiring trigger moves earlier than the heads math suggests.

10. Speed — the CPT cliff and what a late unit costs

A unit that misses its CPT by 1 minute loses a full truck cycle — often a calendar day of promise.
Speed loss (units/day)  = Σ_CPT max(0, Units due at CPT − Pack capacity in the window before CPT)
Cost per missed unit    ≈ premium-transport uplift (if upgraded)
                          or P(cancel | late) × margin + trust (unpriced)

Worked: 18:00 CPT has 180K due;  pack capacity 14:00–18:00 = 4 h × 36K/h = 144K  →  36K miss,
        daily
   60% upgraded to premium at +$2.50 → 21.6K × $2.50 = $54K/day in transport alone, before cancels

Implication. Weekly utilization can be 80% and the 18:00 CPT can miss every day. Capacity must be planned at the CPT window grain for peak weeks. This is Case C in Section 7, in numbers.
