S1 — Two warehouses into one omni DC, robots throttled at 30% (Sephora UDC)

LPs: Customer Obsession · Insist on the Highest Standards · Bias for Action · Deliver Results. Maps here: this is a capacity consolidation and a launch. It is your closest analog to Launch / WW Design / FC Ops work.

"Sephora combined two warehouses — store replenishment and dotcom — into one omnichannel DC on Blue Yonder 2023.1 with Locus robotics. I ran it as a program: fifteen-plus named tracks, sixty-plus stakeholders, about a dozen systems touched — WMS, ESB and Kafka, OMS, finance, the data platform. Dated public test gates — November 22, December 20, January 30 — and a hard October-to-December change freeze because the building had to survive peak. One issues log, 836 rows over fourteen months, every row with an owner and a date. Week two after robots went live, pick accuracy printed 94% against a 98% gate I had published before the bots arrived. I throttled robots to about 30% of volume, kept the launch green on manual process, sat the zone-logic debug, and restored full volume in week three when they cleared the bar. Processing efficiency ended plus fifteen percent. Zero customer incidents. The gate existed so that decision needed no debate."

What did YOU do? Published the 98% gate, made the throttle call the day 94% printed, owned the issues log and the freeze.

Why not push through to hit the automation date? A gate that cannot stop the program is decoration. I will not ship a customer-facing miss to protect a robotics narrative.

How did you measure? Pick-accurate throughput versus the pre-published 98%, hourly by zone, not a building average. Customer incidents: zero. Processing efficiency +15% against the pre-consolidation baseline.

Differently? Shadow the bots on one zone for a week before they touched 100% of volume.

Data pull? WMS pick-confirm versus gold-standard location, by zone, by hour.

S2 — Cyber Monday, 4 AM: allocation across three DCs and a hundred stores (Manhattan Associates)

LPs: Bias for Action · Are Right, A Lot · Dive Deep · Customer Obsession. Maps here: balancing capacity and demand at peak in a closed network — the core of this job's bad weeks.

"As a distribution-OMS consultant I owned order allocation and promising across three mega-DCs and about a hundred stores. The lead time we could show a customer was an output of which node we sourced from — choose the node, and you have chosen the promise. Cyber Monday, 4 AM, a VP call: backorder lines piling up, millions at risk, full diagnostic an hour away. The noise said capacity. I triaged on what we had: ranked orders by promise-at-risk, rebalanced allocation across the three DCs, one bridge, one log, every decision timestamped. Differently? A scheduler heartbeat and a DST checklist before peak. Action without a mechanism is heroics; I do not want a second 4 AM.

S3 — Two hundred lanes: promise at the tail, release the inventory (Elementum)

LPs: Customer Obsession · Ownership · Dive Deep · Think Big · Frugality. Maps here: turning measured data into planning parameters; the kind of input SCOT models consume and S&OP depends on.

"A planner asked me for a lead-time-variability report. The room had normalized carrier-brochure lead times — one optimistic number per lane, never measured — and the inventory plan ran on them. I treated the brochure as a lie the business was paying for in safety stock. I pulled shipment history across two hundred lanes and built mean, spread and tail per lane, then moved every planning lead time to the tail — the p99 — not the average. Ninety-nine percent lead-time accuracy held. Because the measured spread was tighter than assumed on most lanes, 580,000 units and about a million and a half of safety stock came free. Then a drift flag per lane, so a widening spread triggered a review instead of a silent re-plan. The ask was a report. The product was lane-level planning as a system."

What did YOU do? Pulled the history myself, built the distributions, pitched leadership with mockups, productized it, designed the drift flag.

Why the tail and not the mean? A lead time set at the mean is a coin flip — in a right-skewed lane, roughly two in five shipments arrive after it. The p99 is the smallest number of days by which 99 of 100 have already arrived. I set it off the empirical quantile per lane, not a normal approximation, because the normal understates the tail.

How did you measure? Actual arrival inside the planned lead time, by lane, by week — 99%. Safety stock released only on lanes where measured spread was tighter than assumed.

Differently? Start every new domain with a measurement audit in week one instead of discovering the brochure problem mid-project.

Data pull? Shipment-level: lane key, ship date, arrival date, carrier, source of the transit number (brochure vs scan).

"Planners learned about shortages when the line stopped. I did not ship a threshold email. S5 — Austria: the noise said carriers, the data said measurement (Elementum)

S10 — S/4HANA: one definition of "available," returns as first-class stock, and a cutover with a numeric rollback (Sephora S/4HANA ↔ ISM)

LPs: Ownership · Invent and Simplify · Earn Trust · Dive Deep · Deliver Results · Are Right, A Lot (being wrong). Maps here: this is the JD's "optimize and manage the flow of inventory within the network," literally. Returns and quality-hold are reverse flow; they consume storage and throughput and are usually invisible to the plan until they are promised to a customer. It is also your cleanest "I was wrong" story and your cleanest one-way-door cutover story.

"Sephora was standing up S/4HANA as the inventory system of record for stores, the retail DC and dotcom, with a warehouse execution system beside it. The problem was not the software. 'Available' meant something different in every system, because each definition protected a different number — fill rate for stores, labor for the DC, the customer promise for dotcom, the credit memo for finance. Customer returns, return-to-vendor stock, quality-hold and lost-and-found were being promised to customers because somewhere they counted as available.

I redesigned the inventory operating model. Every stock state — available-to-sell, reserved, in-transit, quality-inspection, blocked, lost-and-found — got one definition, one owner, and one rule about who may promise against it, signed by the business before anyone configured anything. Returns got a plant of their own. Return-to-vendor stock-transport orders were modeled as pick-reserve so credit could never run ahead of the physical unit: receive, grade, then restock, scrap or RTV. Nothing in that path was promiseable until disposition.

I ran it as a program, not a configuration: a RAID log, a weekly CIO/COO pack across sixty-plus stakeholders on frozen metrics so a red stayed red, and a 343-task weekend cutover runbook with rollback written as a number before the weekend — interface sync under 99% for four hours, or p95 latency over twice budget for one hour. It never fired. The legacy inventory system, MCS, came out only after the new definition held in production. Zero customer-facing incidents on the staged go-lives.

One rule I wrote was wrong. I forbade every channel from promising against units on a truck. It felt safe. It starved dotcom of sellable units while physical inventory in transit rose. The data showed the miss, and the fix went through the same signed policy — a defined in-transit promise window per lane, from real lane data. Conservatism has a price, paid in the same currency as risk."

What did YOU do? Wrote the stock-state definitions and took them to steering with the failure mode attached. Designed the returns plant and the pick-reserve RTV model. Owned the RAID, the weekly pack, the runbook, and wrote the rollback triggers. Made the MCS retirement call.

Why a signed policy before configuration? Most oversell "bugs" are vocabulary bugs. A config change nobody signed becomes next quarter's argument. Once the rule was signed, the design arguments got small — the plant and the STOs were just the implementation of a sentence everyone had already agreed to.

Why rollback as a number? A rollback that is a paragraph gets debated at 2 AM by tired people. A number gets called by whoever is on the bridge, no meeting. The triggers existed so the weekend could start.

How did you measure? Binary, in production: could a unit in quality-hold still be promised? It could not. Sync and latency against the published triggers through the weekend. Customer-facing incidents: zero. MCS off.

How did you know the in-transit rule was wrong? Sellable units by channel fell while units in transit by lane rose. Two lines diverging is the signal. Fix: a per-lane in-transit window from measured lane transit — the same idea as the 200-lane work, applied to promising instead of planning.

Differently? Set the in-transit window from lane data on day one instead of starting from "never." And sample the OMS-to-WMS handshake daily in hypercare from day one, not after the first discrepancy.

Data pull? Stock by state by plant by day; promise log joined to stock state at promise time; sellable units by channel against in-transit units by lane; interface sync rate and p95 latency by hour through cutover.

If they ask how it maps to capacity: "Returns are inbound volume the plan did not forecast. Quality-hold is storage you cannot sell. If the capacity plan counts them as available, it is overstating usable capacity and the promise is lying. 7. The functional block — capacity planning

You will get at least one of these. Format they expect: clarify out loud (60–90 seconds) → structure (60 seconds) → a math checkpoint → recommendation with owners, triggers and risks. Say "both constraints — storage and throughput" early in every answer. Label every number you make up as illustrative, and say what you would replace it with on day one.

Primer: the shape of a capacity plan

Demand side          units by week × building type × speed tier, with a peak factor
                     (forecast owner: S&OP / SCOT; your job is to interrogate its grain and mix)

Required capacity    storage:    units on hand ≈ daily outbound units × days of cover → cube needed
                     throughput: units/week each process step must push (inbound, stow, pick, pack, ship)
                     both, by building type; the binding one is the one you plan to

Available capacity   buildings × design rate × a utilization ceiling (congestion starts before 100%)
                     minus planned downtime, retrofits, launches still ramping

Gap by week          required − available, by region, by type; where and when it binds

Levers, cheapest and fastest first
   1. flow: move units — placement / allocation to buildings with slack (days to weeks)
   2. hours: shifts, overtime, labor plan (weeks)
   3. inventory policy: pull inbound forward or push it back; trim slow movers (weeks)
   4. overflow: 3P / temporary space (weeks to months)
   5. retrofit / automation: more throughput in the same cube (quarters)
   6. expansion / new build: more cube (OP cycle; years)
   7. demand shaping: speed tier, promise, selection (the lever nobody wants to admit)

Plan                 named owner per lever, trigger that fires it, review cadence, and what you will not do

Case A — "Build next year's capacity plan for a region."

Clarify (say these out loud). Which building types are in scope? Is the demand forecast handed to me or do I own it? What is the peak factor and what week does it peak? What are the policy ceilings on utilization? Which launches and retrofits are already committed? What is the planning grain — week, building, type?

Structure. The primer, top to bottom. Spend the most time on the gap-by-week and the lever ranking.

Math checkpoint (illustrative). "If a building ships 1.0M units a week at average and the peak factor is 1.6, peak week is 1.6M. If its throughput design rate is 1.4M and the ceiling I am allowed to plan to is 90%, usable is 1.26M. The gap is 340K units in peak week. Flow first: how much of that can neighboring buildings absorb inside their own ceilings? Then hours. Overflow only for what is left. I would replace every one of those numbers with the S&OP demand file and the engineering design rates on day one."

Recommendation. A one-page plan: gap by week by region, the lever stack with owners, the triggers that promote a lever (e.g., "if the week-8 forecast is still 5% above plan, overflow contracts sign"), and the two things you will not do — plan to 100% of design rate, or pad every building's forecast to hide a placement problem. Risk: forecast mix shifts between types; you plan by type, not by total.

Case B — "Peak forecast just came in 10% above capacity. Go."

  1. Verify the number before you spend on it. Grain and mix: is the 10% real demand, or a type-mix shift that makes one building type bind while another has slack? Same cohort weights as last forecast?
  2. Find where it binds. Storage or throughput? Inbound dock, stow, pick, pack, or outbound? One region or network? "A network 10% is usually three buildings at 25% and the rest fine."
  3. Cheapest lever first, with a trigger. Placement and allocation shifts this week. Labor plan next. Pull slow-moving inbound back; pull fast-moving inbound forward. Overflow for the residual. Speed tier only if the customer promise is the last protection.
  4. Publish the plan with a date on every lever and a freeze on non-essential change into peak — the UDC October-to-December freeze.
  5. What you will not do. Spread a national pad across every building. That hides the cell that is actually breaking and spends cost everywhere.

Story to attach: S2 — rank by promise-at-risk, rebalance across nodes, timestamped log, then a mechanism so you never discover the regime in November again.

Case C — "Utilization looks fine but speed is missing."

Utilization is a weekly average. Speed misses live at the hour, the dock door, the sort type, the cutoff. Change the grain: by building by hour by process step. Check mix: did fast-promise demand land in buildings far from the customer? Check cutoff cliffs: units arriving an hour late lose a calendar day. Story: S5 — the reports were wrong in both directions because they measured the wrong grain. Line to say: "If I am still talking about network utilization after two follow-ups, I am failing the question."

Case D — "Finance says the new building is not justified."

Do not argue. Price both columns. Cost of the build, annualized. Cost of not having it: overflow contracts, transfers and split shipments, speed downgrades and their conversion cost, labor premiums, the risk-adjusted cost of a peak miss. Show the sensitivity: at what forecast does the build break even? Offer the middle path — retrofit or flow redesign — priced in the same table. Story: S7 — the two-column table that flipped an eighteen-month plan; Finance attacked it and it survived. Line: "I would rather lose this argument to a better number than win it with a louder voice."

Case E — "Define the metrics for a network capacity program."

Outputs (what the business feels): capacity delivered vs plan by week; utilization vs ceiling by type; cost per unit; customer speed attainment. Inputs (what a named owner can move this week): launch and retrofit milestones on dated gates; placement compliance; forecast accuracy at the planning grain; labor plan attainment; inbound ASN accuracy. Mechanism: a weekly review where reds arrive pre-decomposed — which input moved, whose lever, what correction, verified by the same number next week — and a correction-of-error write-up for any miss above a published threshold that ends in a mechanism change, not "try harder." Line: "Inputs without outputs are a hobby. Outputs without inputs are weather."

Fermi, if they throw one

  • State assumptions. Compute out loud. Label it illustrative. Say what you would replace it with.
  • "How many units does a large sortable FC ship in a week?" Structure: shifts × hours × headcount × units per hour, cross-checked against outbound dock capacity. Do not quote an Amazon number you have not seen. "I would take the design rate from Ops Engineering on day one."
  • "How much storage for a week of demand?" Daily outbound × days of cover ÷ units per cubic foot ÷ bin utilization. Say the structure, pick round numbers, flag the two you are least sure of.
  • "Cost of a 4-point speed miss at peak?" Volume × delta × cost per late unit, plus the unpriced trust cost. Name the grain. Never say "huge."

Lines that read as judgment, not worship

  • "I plan to a ceiling, not to design rate. Congestion starts before the building is full."
  • "A network average hides the three buildings that are actually breaking."
  • "Flow is the cheapest capacity in the building. Steel is the most expensive. I spend them in that order."
  • "The forecast is an input I interrogate, not a fact I plan to."
  • "Every lever gets a trigger and an owner before peak, or it is a wish."

12. Traps

Do not say "we" for a decision. "I published the gate. I made the throttle call. I wrote the tolerance."

Do not invent a headcount. Matrix leadership, said plainly, is fine at L6. A fake org chart is not.

Do not solve the case before clarifying. Sixty seconds of questions out loud is scored, not tolerated.
