Infra — Why 98%, and what did you actually do at 94?

98% was set from the pre-ramp baseline and the incident budget before the event, and DC Ops owned it. At 94 in week two I throttled the bots to about 30% of volume for a week, root-caused the zone and slotting logic, and ramped again — full volume by week three. A gate that cannot stop the program is decoration; mine did.

Infra — Why two go-lives instead of one big one?

So we never hunted two defect classes in the same week. January proved reserve rules against live demand while the floor was untouched; May left warehouse execution as the only remaining class. One go-live would have put a reserve defect and a floor defect in the same room.

Infra — What made you say go in May?

Counted rows, not the room: Blocker + Critical at zero, mock 3 inside the 0.5% tolerance, the 343-task runbook rehearsed, rollback armed per interface. I would have said no-go on one open Blocker; I never had to.

Infra — What were the rollback triggers and did any fire?

Sync below 99% for four consecutive hours, or order-flow p95 above twice budget for one hour — rollback per interface behind them. Armed for both go-lives; never fired. The one irreversible instant was step 4, zero-then-publish, so no SKU was ever sellable in two places.

Infra — The labor number is the biggest piece of your $1.6M. How firm?

The fifteen percent is measured and signed; the 120 FTE and the $28 loaded rate are my assumptions. Pull the DC's direct hours for the two quarters before and after and multiply by payroll — that turns the model into a record in an afternoon. If the split-shipment row didn't move, the total is about $1.1M.

Infra — What would you do differently?

Staff the interim reporting workstream on day one, not month three — for four months every report had to declare which system of record it read. And the Locus slip: escalated the week it surfaced, but I would have put the RTL on the register as a risk, not a task, from the start.

Probing — challenge Q&A tuned to this interviewer

Infra — Why publish a tolerance before you knew you could hit it?

So the number belonged to Finance and me before the test, not after. Mock one's 7% was the price; mock three's 0.3% was worth it — and the tolerance was never renegotiated.

Infra — How do you read a mock?

Never by the headline alone. Every delta got a root-cause class — units of measure, location drift, status vocabularies, then in-transit timing. If the total shrank without a named cause, the mock was not evidence. 7 → 1.8 → 0.3 each removed a category, and the fixes were mapping tables as data, not code.

Infra — A quarter of the RF devices failed at cutover. Why not roll back?

Because the fallback was rehearsed and the rollback was not needed: inventory was inside tolerance; the failure was device conversion, not data. I paid about $45K of paper-pick productivity — modeled — to protect about $2.5M a month of pipeline, and took the miss upstairs the same afternoon with my name on the cause.

Infra — What was the freeze and why 72 hours?

A 72-hour in-transit freeze, set at or above the longest lane transit, so in-transit could be converted as its own batch with Σ legacy = Σ JDA — the class that took mock two from 1.8% to 0.3%. Legacy stayed read-only for 30 days as the failback.

Infra — The $875K avoided slip — are you banking it?

No. It is record arithmetic — 10.5 days at about $83K a day — on a slip that did not happen; it is the price that made the fallback the obvious call. What I would measure is the pipeline's first program start against plan, which was the next month.

Infra — Lesson, and what rule did you leave behind?

Any single-point dependency in front of an exit criterion gets a what-if plan regardless of estimated effort. Differently: dual-path device conversion on day one, and a device model × OS × script pass/fail table before training week.
