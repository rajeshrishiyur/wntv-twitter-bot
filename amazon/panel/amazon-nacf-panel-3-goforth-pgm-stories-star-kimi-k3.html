How I run a program — cadence and the cost-vs-speed framework (one page, say it in under a minute)

If he asks “how do you run a large program?” — 45 seconds

I run every program on the same machine. First, baseline the number before touching anything. Second, one scorecard on a fixed cadence with the same definitions every week — two dashboards is zero dashboards. Third, stage gates with numeric entry and exit criteria, published before the data arrives, so the gate can actually stop the program. Fourth, when a number goes red, I chase it to the root cause before spending money on the symptom. Fifth, the correction lands in the same weekly review that surfaced it, with an owner and a date. Every story I’m about to tell is that machine applied — two DCs, a robotics deployment, and a platform retirement.

P2 — RP → JDA WMS migration across two DCs: eight months, on time, about 3% under budget full STAR

Company: Sephora (two distribution centers) · My role: program lead, full SDLC owner · Team: 14 core + 6 DC Ops record
Why Rocky: a large deployment run outside my expertise on gates, cadence and a published pass line — the cleanest “program management play” I have. LP tags: Deliver Results · Insist on the Highest Standards · Ownership · Earn Trust.
60-second pitch

At Sephora two distribution centers had to move from an old RP system to JDA WMS in eight months, with a twenty-million-dollar pipeline of four strategic projects waiting behind the date. I had never run a WMS migration and nothing was documented. So I ran gates instead of experience: discovery, design, build, test, cutover and hypercare, each with a written entry and exit, and no phase opened until the previous exit was signed. The pass line for data accuracy — half a percent — was agreed with Finance before the first mock cutover. Mock one came back at 7%, mock two at 1.8%, mock three at 0.3%, and the go-live recommendation cited that scorecard, not confidence. We went live on time, about 3% under a roughly $1.8M budget, and hypercare met its written exit in week three of four. The one miss inside it — an RF-device script that moved training a week and a half — is the reason every dependency I own now has a what-if plan.

S — Situation. Sephora, two DCs on a legacy RP warehouse system, a hard eight-month deadline to JDA WMS, and a $20M pipeline of four strategic projects that could not start until the migration landed record. No strategy, scope, milestones, dependencies or risks were written down when I was handed it.

T — Task. I owned the program end to end: deliver on time, on budget (about $1.8M record), with cutover data accuracy proven before go-live — and run it as a full lifecycle, not a technical swap.

A — Action. I sat with the sponsor first on objectives, assumptions and success criteria; turned the lifecycle into five gated phases (below); ran one weekly cross-functional RAID review as the program heartbeat; published the 0.5% reconciliation tolerance with Finance before mock one; gave every miss class an owner; and wrote the go-live recommendation off the scorecard. The JDA configuration was the team’s; the governance that made three honest mocks possible was mine.

R — Result. On time; about 3% under a ~$1.8M budget (roughly $54K — my division); mocks 7.0% → 1.8% → 0.3% against the 0.5% line; the 72-hour in-transit freeze held; hypercare exited on “under two blocker tickets a day” in week three of four; the $20M pipeline started the month after record.

Trade-off I made — cost vs speed

Options. (a) Compress discovery and overlap build to buy calendar against a hard date; (b) hold every gate and pay for reversibility at cutover.

Chose (b). I spent schedule and political capital holding discovery until every flow was documented, and I made the business accept operational cost — a 72-hour in-transit freeze and 30 days of RP read-only — so no flow was irreversible.

Rejected (a) because a skipped discovery shows up as a mock-three surprise, and a slip on this date stalled the whole $20M pipeline — about $2.5M a month on average my division, not banked. Cost of my choice: freeze pain for the DCs and a hard few weeks defending the hold.

Failure / learning (Apple G2, told honestly). Training sign-off was a go-live exit criterion that depended on hundreds of RF guns, printers and workstations converting. The devices team called it three to four days; I planned it as low effort. I scheduled a device test before training anyway — the upgrade script failed on newer RF hardware. With key people on COVID night shifts, manual conversion could not finish in time, so I told leadership straight: training moves 1.5 weeks, my miss. Go-live held. Root cause was my risk model — I sized by effort, not impact. Since then every cross-functional dependency gets a what-if line (what breaks, who owns the fallback, how long it takes).

Probing Q&A

Q1. You set the 0.5% yourself and passed at 0.3% — isn’t that grading your own exam?

The line was agreed with Finance, in writing, before mock one — the opposite of negotiating under pressure. A rehearsal passing inside a pre-agreed line is what rehearsal is for. Zero-defect cutovers don’t exist; the remaining tail was documented, priced and accepted by the business.

Q2. What was your program cadence, concretely?

One weekly cross-functional RAID review as the heartbeat — risks, actions, issues, decisions, with owners and dates; phase-gate reviews with the sponsor at each exit; a mock every rehearsal cycle with a scorecard readout; daily blocker counts in hypercare against the “under two a day” exit.

Q3. What was in mock one’s 7%?

Mostly vocabulary, not loss: unit-of-measure mappings, location naming that had drifted, and open-order status codes that meant different things in RP and JDA. Counted by class with an owner each, so the rate fell predictably. Learning: I’d now reconcile a thin sample before mock one — vocabulary classes surface on a small sample weeks earlier.

Q4. What would you do differently?

Put the discovery exit criterion in front of leadership on day one, before calendar pressure arrived — holding discovery was right, but publishing what “done” meant earlier would have made the hold cheaper to defend. And the what-if line per dependency from kickoff, which would have turned the RF failure into a non-event.

Angle rule: the full UDC dual-inventory program is UDC pack B1; the slip told as a failure is Bar Raiser bank F4. This story is the deployment: vendor scope, readiness gates, throttle, communication.
60-second pitch

At Sephora’s Salt Lake City DC we added Locus robots to retail outbound in a building that was changing its warehouse system at the same time. My job was to land the robotics without adding risk to the conversion. I ran it as five readiness gates — physical, hardware, software, integration, then testing and go-live — and the network expansion went through a written SOW: two new network closets, thirty-one new access points and a fiber ring, with photo evidence before anything powered on. Probing Q&A

Q1. Why 98% and not 100%?

100% is a fantasy bar that teaches teams to hide misses; much below the high nineties is a manual process with robots for decoration. The bar sat where the building’s accuracy promise to stores lives. The point was that the number could stop the rollout — and it did, at 94.

Q2. What was actually wrong in week two?

Zone logic. The bot map and the induction rules disagreed about which zone owned which work, so bots were routed into contention. Locus drove the fix with our WMS engineers; the throttle bought the time to find it. Not a hardware problem — the network SOW and photo checks had done their job.

Q3. How did you run the vendor and the SOW — what was your cadence?

One retail-track status page with a program colour, five readiness categories, an action-item table with owner and due date, and a Jira dashboard behind it, read weekly inside the program’s two-block status. The SOW was the physical scope baseline — it was at version 1.4 when the expansion was specified — and acceptance was evidence: photos of the rack, closets and access points, fiber connected before switches powered on.

Q4. Whose fault was the slip, and what did you change?

The slip came from the vendor-and-integration track; how I carried it was mine — a task, not a risk. Now every vendor dependency on the critical path goes on the risk register with a trigger date and a named owner on both sides, and a slipped vendor doesn’t get to lower the bar. VERIFY the vendor-side root cause of the slip — safe sentence: “The integration items open at the time were the API style, the customer-owner configuration and tote mapping; I won’t pin the slip on one of them.”

At Flexport we were retiring a legacy visibility platform, Crux, that still served 79 paying customers — about $186K of annual recurring revenue and close to 295,000 containers a year. Probing Q&A

Q1. Why not just switch Crux off on one date?

Because most of the revenue sat with customers the new platform did not fit — brokers and carriers. A single date would have handed them to a competitor and broken trust with the rest. Segmenting let us move fast where the fit was clean — the SaaS competitors were cancelled in 2022 — and slow down only where a partner path existed.

Q2. How did you decide who to cancel?

By segment and partner status, written in the RAPID. One broker was already a partner, so the other two were offered the same partner path; carriers with no partner relationship were cancelled at wind-down; the two SaaS companies were potential competitors for the same shippers, so they were cancelled, one after legal review.

Q3. What was your cadence and how did you communicate?

The decision went through the RAPID steps — recommend, input, agree, decide — then the Inform step listed who needed to know, and the Commitments step had every impacted party confirm, verbally in the meeting or in writing by email. Day to day, shadow-account bugs were read in the daily stand-up and wave readiness in the weekly program review. VERIFY the customer-facing notice timing — safe sentence: “Customers were told from the dated list; I won’t quote a notice period I can’t point to.”

Q4. Was Crux actually shut down on time?

VERIFY the final shutdown date. Safe sentence: “The plan targeted end of 2022, and the record shows the cancels dated through October 2022 and every remaining customer on a path; I’d rather not state a final switch-off date I can’t show you.”
