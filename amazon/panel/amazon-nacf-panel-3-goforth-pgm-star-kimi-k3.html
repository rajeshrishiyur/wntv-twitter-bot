HOW I RUN A PROGRAM

60-second spoken answer

“I run five gated phases: Discover, Design, Build and Test, Cutover, then Hypercare and Close. Each phase has an entry bar, an artifact I personally deliver, a graduating bar that says the work is mature enough to review, and an exit bar that can stop the program. I use one requirement table, one RAID log, one decision log, and one counted issues log from discovery through production. Weekly steering has an IT block for architecture, interfaces, quality and release risk, then a business block for process readiness, training and adoption; the join is a decision, never two status reports. Before cutover, every critical path is rehearsed, every rollback trigger is numeric, and go/no-go reads counted rows. Hypercare ends on blockers per day and control ownership, not because the calendar says the launch is old.”

Weekly steering: one meeting, two blocks

IT block: architecture decisions, interfaces, environment/access, quality trend, critical path, release and rollback risk.

Business block: process readiness, control owners, training, cutover communications, adoption and customer impact.

Join: metric → trend → driver → decision needed. I escalate only a decision, with a recommendation and expiry date.

Four logs, four jobs

Requirements: what must be true, owner, acceptance evidence.

RAID: threat or dependency, trigger, mitigation, owner, date.

Decision: options, decider, date, rationale, consequences.

Issues: observed defect, severity, owner, aging, closure evidence. Status never substitutes for one of these.

BUILDING CONSENSUS — MAKE THE COUNCIL A DECISION, NOT A REVIEW

LPs: Earn Trust · Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit · Ownership · Insist on the Highest Standards.
Spoken framework

“Consensus is not everyone liking the answer. It is every material objection represented accurately, a named decider choosing with the consequences visible, and the owners committing to execute. Before a council, I pre-wire every requirement row with its owner. Working sessions converge on one table, not competing decks. I convert objections into spec, option, risk, or out-of-scope rows. The council receives the unresolved decisions and my recommendation; it does not discover the program live. After the decision, I record who disagreed, what evidence would reopen it, and what commitment now means.”

S — Situation. UDC was one physical building moving from two systemic warehouses to one omni facility while upgrading the WMS, with seven system families and about two dozen named systems on the impact view (record-from-docs).

T — Task. I had to get architecture, engineering, finance, digital, operations and planning to accept one transition design without turning sign-off into serial vetoes.

A — Action. I put 44 requirements in one owner-tagged table across 23 named systems; pre-wired each row; ran disagreements in working sessions; turned “this will break my flow” into an interface, acceptance, or risk row; kept current state, target state, interim state and rejected options visible; then asked council for three decisions: architecture, staged sequence, and scope / freeze boundary. Once decided, owners either committed or documented the evidence that could reopen the row.

R — Result. The sign-off pack created one buildable contract. The planned late-July FSD landed August 14, so I do not claim the gate held to plan; the correction was to expose predecessor evidence earlier.

Disagree-and-commit mechanics

  1. Restate: I write the strongest version of the objection and get the owner to confirm it.
  2. Classify: requirement, risk, option, assumption, or preference. Preference does not silently become a blocker.
  3. Price: impact on customer, schedule, cost, reversibility, and the owner’s operating burden.
  4. Decide: named decider, date, rationale, rejected option, and evidence that can reopen it.
  5. Commit: dissent remains in the log; execution follows the chosen row. I follow up privately if the owner cannot commit.
TRADE-OFF I MADE: I accepted upstream build at risk from June 9 before July 31 council sign-off, but fenced it to reversible merchandising work. WMS conversion, outbound, finance, and EDW waited for the decision.
Proof story + number: UDC council — 44 requirement rows / 23 named systems with owner-tagged sign-off (record-from-docs).
SAFE-SAY: “The primary deck records 44 requirements and 23 named systems; I use ‘about a dozen domains’ only as a spoken simplification.”
If asked who built it: “I owned the council mechanism and integrated specification; system architects owned their designs and engineers owned implementation.”
Failure / learning: Pre-wiring agreement is not enough; I now pre-wire the evidence required to graduate each row, because UDC’s FSD date still slipped.

AMBIGUITY — REDUCE UNCERTAINTY WITHOUT WAITING FOR CERTAINTY

LPs: Bias for Action · Are Right, A Lot · Ownership · Deliver Results.
Spoken framework

“I separate unknowns from decisions. Every unknown goes into an assumption log with an owner, expiry date, impact if false, and the cheapest test. Discovery is staged: map the flow, test the highest-consequence seams, then deepen only where the decision depends on it. I decide reversible, bounded choices inside the approved guardrails. I escalate irreversible choices, cross-org priority, policy, customer exposure, or a risk above the agreed tolerance. That lets engineering move while leadership retains the decisions only leadership can make.”

I decide alone

  • Sequence of working sessions and discovery tests.
  • Format and operating cadence of logs.
  • Reversible work inside signed scope and budget.
  • Issue classification against published definitions.
  • When evidence is incomplete, a bounded experiment with switches and named blast radius.

I escalate for a decision

  • Irreversible data or truth migration.
  • Scope, architecture, policy, or peak-freeze change.
  • Cross-org resource collision I cannot resolve with sequencing.
  • Customer exposure above tolerance.
  • Go / hold when evidence and business urgency conflict.

The assumption-log sentence

“I do not say ‘we need more information.’ I say: assumption, owner, expiry, cheapest test, consequence if false, and which decision it blocks.”

S — Situation. UDC’s architecture and FSD were not yet signed, but some upstream merchandising work had a long lead time and the integrated schedule could not absorb waiting for every downstream answer.

T — Task. Preserve speed without letting provisional work harden into an unapproved architecture.

A — Action. I split the plan by reversibility. MCS / FRAME work began June 9 as a fenced, reversible track. I documented assumptions and prevented the WMS-conversion, outbound, finance, and EDW tracks from opening until the council decision. The July 31 council then decided the target architecture and staged timeline; the FSD closed August 14.

R — Result. We did not lose the entire lead-time window, and the highest-blast-radius work stayed behind the gate. I also gained a reusable rule: start early only where rollback is cheap and the provisional boundary is explicit.

TRADE-OFF I MADE: I accepted rework risk on a reversible slice to protect schedule, and rejected at-risk starts for irreversible truth migration.
Proof story + number: UDC ambiguity — build began June 9 before the July 31 sign-off, fenced by reversibility (record-from-docs).
SAFE-SAY: “I did not start the whole build early; I allowed a bounded upstream slice while the higher-risk tracks stayed gated.”
Source sentence: “Those dates and track boundaries are on the council timeline.”
Failure / learning: The FSD still landed August 14. I now put assumption expiry and evidence owner directly on the integrated milestone, not only in the RAID log.

Escalation test

I escalate when: the decider is outside the team; the decision changes scope, policy, architecture, customer exposure, or shared priority; and the delay has a date. I do not escalate status, interpersonal discomfort, or a problem without a recommendation.

S — Situation. UDC crossed merchandising, WMS, digital, finance, reporting, HR, robotics, and distribution operations, each with a valid local optimization.

T — Task. Keep approximately two dozen named systems aligned without forcing every stakeholder into every meeting.

A — Action. I used a stakeholder map by decision and phase; separated the steering agenda into IT and business blocks; kept one RAID, requirement, decision, and issue grammar; pre-wired owners; and escalated the peak-freeze boundary as a decision with options rather than as a red status.

R — Result. The council received decision-ready material, E2E dates became shared commitments, and the staged go-lives completed with zero customer-facing incidents (record).

TRADE-OFF I MADE: I rejected one giant weekly meeting. Separate blocks reduced attendance cost, while the shared rows and join decisions prevented business / IT drift.
Proof story + number: UDC stakeholder system — seven system families and about two dozen named systems on one impact view (record-from-docs).
Failure / learning: A late FSD proved that communication frequency is not decision closure; I now end every forum with decision, owner, evidence, and expiry.

My five-column decision read

  1. Cost: direct spend plus operating burden and rework.
  2. Speed: earliest useful capability, not merely code complete.
  3. Risk: probability × customer / operational blast radius, expressed plainly.
  4. Reversibility: switch, rollback time, data restoration, and who can invoke it.
  5. Cost of delay: customer, revenue, peak, or resource consequence by the next real decision date. If not sourced, I describe it without inventing dollars.

S UDC combined an inventory truth change with a WMS upgrade in a live building. T Protect peak and customer continuity without turning safety into indefinite delay. A I kept big bang on the options page, recommended two waves, preserved rollback boundaries, and required E2E evidence before each step. R Both go-lives completed with zero customer-facing incidents (record), and DC Ops measured +15% processing efficiency after stabilization (record).

TRADE-OFF I MADE: I bought safety with an interim state and its support burden; I rejected one faster date with a much larger recovery domain.
Proof story + number: RP→JDA — three mocks moved variance 7.0% → 1.8% → 0.3% against a pre-published 0.5% tolerance (record).
Failure / learning: A date is not a benefit if readiness was silently removed to reach it; after G2, prerequisite tests are never traded away without naming the new risk.
