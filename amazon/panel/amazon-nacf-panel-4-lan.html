Peer — You owned no system. How did you get three organizations to sign one definition?

Pre-wired every row of the systems-impacted table with its owner's level of effort before the council — nobody learned their cost in the room — ran working sessions to one table, converted objections into FSD rows, and asked the council for three decisions rather than a review. Peer — Why lower-of-two and not an average or the book?

Because oversell is a customer defect and undersell is a planning variance. A positive out-of-balance means the book is above the floor; promising the difference risks oversell, so the basis is the minimum, and Inventory Control chases the variance through the Negative AFS and AFS Correction screens. The asymmetry is the policy.

Peer — Merchandising wanted merch reserve promisable. What happened?

I priced it — unpickable stock sold — and offered visibility without promisability. The no went into the FSD as a row, not into a meeting minute. A no that lives in a document survives; one that lives in a meeting gets re-litigated weekly.

Peer — How do you know it was a tenth of a percent?

I don't, to the decimal. The record says incidents were recurring and went to zero; the rate is a benchmark set deliberately low — a twentieth of a typical e-commerce inventory-cancel rate. First thing I would measure: the "item unavailable after order" cancel-reason code for the twelve months before January. If the $1M MCS figure already includes enhancement labor, I drop the change-premium row and the headline is about $2.2M.

Peer — What did you get wrong in this program?

The first in-transit rule I wrote barred every channel from promising units in transit — safe-feeling, and it starved dotcom of fast movers a day from the building. I corrected it through the same three signatories with a per-lane promise window and a kill switch, released on the normal cadence. Lesson: both sides of a rule get a number before it ships, and back-test a ruleset against history before publishing it. (S4 — pointer; full story on request.)

Peer — Finance wanted their own reconciliation. How did you turn that around?

I showed the same number arriving from the physical side first and gave them the real report to own — the unmatched-posting aging report, reviewed daily with Inventory Accounting. A parallel spreadsheet dies when its owner owns the real report. They signed inside the FSD on 6/5/25.

Peer — Why decrement only on a matched posting? Isn't that just moving cost to Replenishment?

Yes, and I priced it that way on purpose: an unmatched posting under-states availability and shows up as an aged row Finance clears the same day, so the over-order is small and visible. Decrementing on an estimate would hide the gap as oversell. The measure is the aging report's p95 in hours and the match rate inside 24 hours; above 99.5% the Replenishment side rounds to zero.

Peer — Your cancel row looks borrowed from S1. Double-counted?

Different window: S1's rate is the vocabulary problem across node types; this is the posting-lag window inside one node. If the room wants one number, I keep S1's and drop this row — the mechanism still stands on the record.

Peer — Why not post every line to S/4?

Throughput and posting cadence — S/4 posts aggregates for a reason. ISM is the real-time ledger; S/4 stays the settlement authority. The price is a matching cycle to maintain; the benefit is one book.

Peer — 32 hours a week of Finance time — source?

Benchmark, not record — four people at eight hours a week is what Inventory Accounting described, not what a timesheet says. I would count manual journal entries and bridge lines per month before and after; that is in the posting log and it is not opinion.

Peer — Lesson?
