My role, honestly: Technical Program Manager for the Shipment Visibility initiative (Path to 4PL names Thanai Kumar and me as TPMs; DRI Frank te Pas). Product decisions were co-owned with Frank; the pilot machine, gates, testing, monitoring and the 3PL ingestion decision were mine.

60-second pitch

The customer was an importer who could see their Flexport freight perfectly and everything else — the containers moving with other forwarders — not at all. Flexport's own track-and-trace was human-powered: people in Data Services keying milestones, at about sixty-six cents a container. The product bet was to replace that with an automated visibility service and open it to the customer's whole supply chain — the 4PL “single pane of glass.” I was the TPM across nine teams and close to a hundred people; the product DRI and I co-owned the scope. The product decisions that mattered: we launched to two pilot customers, free, with CSV upload before API; we published six graduation criteria — verification SLA ninety percent of the time, data at least as accurate as the legacy Crux product, milestone accuracy eighty percent, stable production, real engagement of at least a hundred containers, and no show-stoppers — and a wave graduated only when it met them. We held Wave 3 once on ETA accuracy and never rolled anything back. We said no to a public tracking website until data quality and cost to serve were fixed. Result: seven pilot customers to sixty-eight — nine-point-seven times — both public targets two months early, and cost to serve from sixty-six cents to three cents a container, about ninety-five percent. The miss I own: monitoring came late, and for weeks a bug took hours to localise.

S — Situation. Flexport's visibility team was created in May 2020 with a mandate to own all track-and-trace milestones; by the start of 2021 only 18 of 99 milestones were actively worked and none were fully automated (2021 Product Development Plan, record-from-docs). Track-and-trace was a cost line — Data Services people keying updates — and the customer could only see Flexport-moved freight. The 2021 plan set two funded initiatives (automate Flexport milestones; build non-Flexport “Shipment Visibility” in the Client App) and one unfunded (Visibility-as-a-Service), with a legacy acquired product, Crux, still serving 79 paying customers.

T — Task. Launch Shipment Visibility as a 4PL product on new infrastructure, fully integrated into the Flexport platform, to pilot customers by late 2021, and scale it in 2022 while the cost to serve fell — without letting a half-accurate product reach customers. I owned the program and the pilot machine; I co-owned scope with the product DRI.

A — Action (the product decisions, in order).

  • 1 · Customer problem and persona, written first. Target: importers and exporters, Enterprise and global key accounts first, with a plan to scale to SMB later (E-Team takeaways, Feb 1 2022). Non-target, not anti-persona: drayage operators and forwarding partners. The job story set — “4PL Shipment Visibility Job Stories — MVP” — was the single reference every tester used when unsure of intended behaviour (Product Testing Approach).
  • 2 · Discovery and the MVP cut. MVP = tracking request by single input, CSV and API; dashboard, shipment list, shipment details, reporting, messaging. Cut from v1: public tracking website (deferred until data quality and cost to serve were fixed — an E-Team decision I supported), source-toggling and usage-audit for future charging (deferred until additional sources arrived, Q2–Q3 2022), paid tiers (free for the whole pilot). Light-weight onboarding by design: API integrations and letters of authority only after Wave 2, once we were confident the product was meeting expectations.
  • 3 · Build vs buy. We did not buy Project44 data: “invest in making our solution better even if it is with Data Services to start” (E-Team, record-from-docs). The reason was product, not pride — visibility was the competitive advantage and the data flywheel, and P44 served a different segment. We did keep buying raw sources (CargoSmart first) and human-in-the-loop for the automation gaps.
  • 4 · Graduation gates instead of dates. I wrote the pilot into waves — Wave 1 two customers (~Q4 2021), Wave 2 four, Wave 3 twenty-plus, full roll-out H2 2022 — and six published graduation criteria: verification SLA met 90% of the time (automated review within 24h, manual within 24h, nothing “in progress” beyond 48h); internal data quality ≥ 90% and at least as accurate as Crux; milestone/ETA accuracy ≥ 80%; production stable at Flexport standards; meaningful engagement (≥ 100 containers uploaded, key features tried); customer feedback with no show-stoppers and CSAT at parity with average consignee CSAT. Entry bar: a new customer gives us at least 50 containers so the product is calibrated before they log in (Pilot Roll-out Schedule, record-from-docs).
  • 5 · Testing as product discovery. Four tests I ran before launch: the “100 container test” (random containers and bills of lading, bugs to Jira, daily stand-up), bi-weekly bug bashes against job-story ranges, shadow customer accounts fed with the customer's own Crux uploads so we compared side by side, and UAT with business users once 80% of shipments had minor or no issues. Go/no-go had two meetings — business and tech — on three criteria: stable production, one pilot customer's data quality ≥ 80%, no show-stoppers.
  • 6 · Volume as a product input. With 4PL volume growing slowly, I took the decision to create a production test account and ingest 3PL volume — 300 containers, Trans-Pacific lanes first, Crux-only customers as the source — to find inaccuracies faster, with explicit non-goals (not a performance test, not a cost-to-serve comparison) and a switch list so no live customer could be disturbed (3PL ingestion one-pager, record-from-docs).
  • 7 · Prioritisation of data quality. Carriers, terminals and milestones were prioritised on the Trans-Pacific eastbound and trans-Atlantic westbound lanes, ordered by cost-to-serve reduction and then by ease of getting the data; a weekly visibility review with POD-ETA accuracy and interaction elimination as the first two numbers (E-Team actions).
  • 8 · How success was measured. Three ladders kept separate: adoption (customers), usage (monthly active users, containers uploaded, features touched) and outcome (cost to serve per container, automation rate, ticket response). The weekly review format was borrowed from the LSA review: input → output, red/green at ±5%.

Compact failure STAR inside V1 — monitoring came late (owned in the year-end review)

S. Data travelled through six-plus system layers, many brand new, with limited monitoring in each. T. Reach the data-quality bar for pilot launch while bugs from the bug bashes were being triaged daily. A. I noticed while reading application logs that no unique id tied an event from the downstream source to the customer-facing screen, so every blocker took two to four hours just to locate. I proposed a design change — trace the load event end to end, count events in versus events out at every system boundary — and built the “customer-ready by 11/19” monitoring list across Client App, Forwarding, Ocean, Visibility, Data Access and the public API, with Datadog monitors and a consolidated view prioritised for production. R. Faster localisation of blocker bugs (my note at the time claimed about half the time to resolve — treat as my estimate, not a measured record); monitoring became part of the launch definition and of the later 3PL test. In the year-end review I wrote the miss plainly: “we should have increased focus on implementing robust system monitoring earlier in the lifecycle.” What I do differently: observability is a line in the MVP, with an owner and a date, before the first bug bash.

Probing Q&A

You were the TPM. What product decision was actually yours?

Three I will defend as mine: graduation by six published criteria instead of a date, including the 50-container calibration entry bar; the production 3PL test account to get volume for accuracy work with explicit non-goals and kill switches; and the trace-id design change that made monitoring possible. Scope cuts — public website deferred, free pilot, CSV before API — were co-owned with the product DRI and ratified by the E-Team; I argued for them, I did not decide them alone.

Why not just buy Project44 data and ship faster?

Because visibility data was the moat and the flywheel: every tracking request taught us where coverage was weak. The E-Team chose to invest in our own solution, with Data Services covering gaps, and to compare ourselves to P44 explicitly rather than depend on it. Different target segment too — they served global key accounts; we needed Enterprise and later SMB. I would make the same call; I would start the competitive analysis a quarter earlier.

How did you know the product was working and not just adopted?

Three ladders, never collapsed into one: adoption — customers on the platform; usage — monthly active users, containers uploaded, features touched, with a hundred-container engagement gate; outcome — cost to serve per container and automation rate. A customer who uploaded ten containers and left counted against us even though they were “adopted.”

What did you get wrong?

Testing effort — I estimated it as if the product were one system and it was six. And monitoring came after debugging instead of before. Both are in the year-end review in my words. The fix I carry: observability and test effort are sized per system boundary, not per product.

60-second pitch

Crux was a container-tracking product Flexport had acquired; by 2022 it had seventy-nine paying customers, about a hundred-and-eighty-six thousand dollars of annual revenue and nearly three hundred thousand containers a year — and we were building Shipment Visibility to replace it. The product question was not “how do we migrate,” it was “who should we migrate.” Importers and exporters — two global accounts, fourteen enterprise, thirty-nine SMB — were our target customer and moved to Shipment Visibility. Three-quarters of the container volume sat with two truck brokers. I was input to that decision and I owned the mechanics that made it safe to make: the migration plan tied to the pilot waves, shadow accounts where we ran the customer's own Crux uploads through the new product side by side, and a graduation gate that said the new data had to be at least as accurate as Crux before a customer was moved. The decision, segment by segment: transition the importers; offer the brokers a partner path through our trucking product or cancel at wind-down; offer forwarders the API with no extra development; cancel the two software competitors after legal review. The learning I keep: a stakeholder asked, “is the feature gap between Crux and the new product written down?” — and the honest answer was “not well enough.” Parity has to be a document before it is a promise.

20-second version

We were retiring an acquired tracking product with seventy-nine paying customers. The product call was who to carry over: importers and exporters moved to the new product behind a gate that said the data had to be at least as accurate as the old one on their own containers; brokers were offered a partner path, forwarders the API, and two competitor platforms were cancelled after legal review. We walked away from three-quarters of the volume on purpose.

Probing Q&A

You walked away from most of the volume. How is that customer-obsessed?

Because those were customers of the product we were retiring, not of the one we were building: truck brokers tracking non-Flexport containers and software platforms re-selling our data. The customer-obsessed act was offering each a real path — a partner arrangement in our trucking product, the API — and dated notice, rather than a quiet degrade. The importers who were our customers got a parity gate and a shadow account before they were asked to move.

What was your decision and what was someone else's?

Someone else's: the segment decisions — Recommend was product, Decide was the executive; I was input. Mine: the migration plan tied to the pilot waves, the “at least as accurate as Crux” gate, the shadow-account method and the production test account with its kill switches. If you ask who could have stopped a migration on data quality, that was me.

How did you communicate the retirement to a customer being cancelled?

Through the RAPID's inform-and-commit steps — the account owner carried the message, with a date and, where it applied, an alternative. I will not claim I wrote the customer letters; I can tell you the dates are in the record: August 1, September 1 and October 24, 2022 for the three cancel-now accounts.

What would you do differently?

Write the feature-parity matrix first, with a column per segment, and put the cost of keeping Crux alive on the same page. Two stakeholders asked for exactly those two things in the RAPID, and the fact they had to ask is my miss.

P1 · “Tell me about something you decided NOT to build.” — the public tracking website and the charging plumbing

S. February 2022, Shipment Visibility was in pilot with a waitlist from FORWARD and an E-Team keen on a public container-tracking website to pull in SMB demand and cut acquisition cost. At the same time there was a request to build source-toggling and usage auditing so visibility could be charged for later. T. Help the product DRI and the E-Team decide what the small team would and would not build in the next two quarters. A. I put the goals in order with data from the pilot: Goal 1 was lower cost to serve and data quality — the thing pilot customers were actually complaining about; Goal 2 was a lead funnel. The E-Team wrote it down: “we prioritise (and cannot be distracted from) Goal 1”; the public website would wait for a PR-FAQ and a market where we already had coverage; the charging plumbing was deferred until additional data sources arrived. In parallel I chose not to make the 3PL ingestion test measure performance or cost to serve — two explicit non-goals so the test stayed small. R. The team's capacity went to POD-ETA accuracy and interaction elimination — the first two numbers on the weekly review — and to the automation that took cost to serve from $0.66 to $0.03 a container (record). The website became a PR-FAQ, not a build. Trade-off: slower SMB funnel against a product that was accurate first. Failure / learning line: the thing I did under-build — monitoring — cost more than the things I correctly declined; “not building” has to include a list of what must still be built.

Probe: wasn't the public website a cheap way to get the volume you said you needed for data quality?

Yes — and the E-Team said exactly that, which is why it was a two-way door we kept open with a PR-FAQ rather than a no. My position was sequencing: get the production 3PL test account first — three hundred containers under our control — before inviting the public to judge coverage we knew was uneven.

P2 · “How do you tell adoption from usage from outcome?” — three ladders, and the one I got wrong first

S. Early in the pilot the number everyone quoted was customers on the platform; a customer who had logged in once counted the same as one tracking three hundred containers a week. T. Define success so that adoption could not masquerade as outcome, and so a weekly review could steer. A. I separated three ladders and gave each a gate. Adoption: customers onboarded — with a 50-container calibration bar before anyone was counted. Usage: monthly active users, containers uploaded (≥ 100 to graduate), key features tried (dashboard, shipment list, details, reporting, messaging). Outcome: what the customer and Flexport paid for — verification SLA met 90% of the time, data at least as accurate as the legacy product, cost to serve per container, automation rate. Ticket volume and response were tracked as a cost-of-quality line, not as “engagement.” The weekly review was built input-to-output, red/green at ±5%, in the format borrowed from an existing operating review. R. Adoption 7 → 68 customers, 9.7× (record); usage 170 monthly active users — the public target, landed (record); outcome $0.66 → $0.03 per container, about 95%, automation 40% → 90%, ticket response 72% → 90% at five times the volume (record). Trade-off: three numbers are harder to headline than one; I accepted a slower story for an honest one. Failure / learning line: I conflated adoption with success for the first wave — a customer that uploaded a handful of containers was a “win” on my slide; the 100-container usage gate exists because of that slide.

Probe: which of the three would you cut if you could keep one?

Outcome — cost to serve per container with the accuracy gate attached. Adoption without it is a free product; usage without it is a busy one. But I would not report outcome without the other two, because the day a 99.4% week turned out to rest on three manual touches, it was the usage and touch counts that caught it.

P3 · “Tell me about a time you sunset something.” — what we refused to carry over from Crux

S. Crux, the acquired tracking app, had 79 paying customers and features the new product did not: tracking of non-Flexport containers for trucking partners, and an open door for software platforms and brokers to pull our milestones. T. As owner of the migration mechanics, make sure what we carried over was chosen, and what we did not was said out loud with a date. A. I framed the sunset as a carry-over list rather than a shutdown list. Carried: container tracking by CSV and API, milestone display, reporting — behind a gate that the data be at least as accurate as Crux on the customer's own containers, proven in shadow accounts first. Not carried: non-Flexport tracking inside the trucking product (“we will not do so at this time” — decision), a standalone service for motor carriers and brokers (strategy doc), data access for the two SCM-SaaS competitors (cancelled after legal review). Cancellations dated: August 1, September 1, October 24, 2022 (record-from-docs). R. 55 importer/exporter customers in the transition segments; 24 in redirect-or-cancel, carrying about three-quarters of the 294,581 containers — volume we gave up on purpose. Trade-off: revenue and volume against a product with one target customer. Failure / learning line: the feature-parity list was asked for in the decision record before it was fully written; I now treat a sunset as a product launch with a parity matrix as its first artefact.

Probe: what would you have carried over if you had had another quarter?

Non-Flexport container tracking for partners who move our freight — the one truck broker already on Transmission is the proof there was a real customer. I would have scoped it as a partner tier of the same visibility service, not as Crux kept alive. It was the right thing to defer and the wrong thing to forget.

P4 · “Tell me about a conflict between a customer's ask and the platform roadmap.” — the truck brokers who wanted to keep tracking everything

S. The two largest Crux customers by far were truck brokers — the segment carried $139,512 of the $185,892 ARR and 221,044 of 294,581 containers (record-from-docs). Their ask was simple: keep letting us track every container, ours and everyone else's, the way Crux did. The roadmap said Shipment Visibility was for importers and exporters, and the trucking product, Transmission, would not add non-Flexport tracking now. T. Give the decision-makers an honest view of the conflict and a transition path that was not just “no.” A. I made sure the input on the record included the full picture: one broker was already a Transmission partner and should keep visibility; one was a large destination-management competitor who also sat inside a six-hundred-truck drayage company we wanted a relationship with; one was in the full-truckload space with no partnership. I argued against treating them as one segment and for a path each could actually take: white-label through Transmission like an existing partner, with the limit stated plainly — no non-Flexport containers — and cancellation at wind-down only if they declined. The pilot-schedule gate meant any importer customer they introduced would still go through the same parity bar. R. Decision: transition the two named brokers to Transmission partners, cancel at wind-down if not interested; motor carriers cancelled at wind-down; the capability to track non-Flexport shipments in Transmission explicitly deferred (record-from-docs). Trade-off: the largest revenue line against roadmap focus; we chose focus and said so with a date. Failure / learning line: I did not have the cost of keeping Crux alive for them on the same page as the decision — a stakeholder had to ask — so the “no” looked more arbitrary than it was.

Probe: the customer was paying you. Why does the roadmap win?

Because a product that serves the customer it was not designed for degrades for the customer it was. Our data quality bar was set for importers' containers on specific lanes; a broker tracking everyone's freight would have dragged coverage and cost to serve back toward the human-powered product we were retiring. The right answer to a paying customer outside your segment is a path and a date, not a quiet yes.
