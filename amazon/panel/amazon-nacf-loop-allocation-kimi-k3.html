ADD-ON 1 · A4 — Apples to apples before decommission Real

Best owner: James · LPs: Are Right, A Lot · Dive Deep · Ownership · Headline: 79 legacy customers, $185,892 ARR and 294,581 annual containers received a segment path (record-from-docs).
20-second version
“Leadership was comparing our 25% reliability with a competitor’s 63% on unlike source mixes. I normalized both to the same basis; the competitor re-scored to about 27%, exposing measurement—not product—as the immediate gap. I then assigned all 79 legacy customers a destination and retired the duplicate truth while improving reliability to 90% against an 80% north star.”

S — Situation. At Flexport, the new visibility product was being judged at 25% schedule reliability against a competitor’s 63%, while a legacy source still billed 79 customers and $185,892 in annual recurring revenue. The source mixes were unlike, yet the comparison could have written off the new product and preserved two competing truths.

T — Task. I owned the retirement risk: establish an honest comparison, expose any residual product gap, and give every legacy customer an explicit path rather than letting decommission drift across another planning cycle.

A — Action. I put decommission at the top of the risk register with an owner, mitigation and date. I rebuilt the benchmark on identical sources; on that basis the competitor’s 63% re-scored to about 27% versus our 25%. I showed the old dashboard’s defenders that their figure was valid on its own basis, then changed the basis rather than attacking the team. I did not stop at normalization: it also exposed variance of sixteen days versus eight as the real gap, so I secured six months for source additions. In parallel, I segmented all 79 customers into migrate, white-label or cancel, with one canonical reliability definition and one executive forum signing both the improvement and retirement plans.

R — Result. The 79-customer legacy base—294,581 annual containers—received a disposition, the duplicate truth retired, and reliability reached 90% against an 80% north star after source work. Leadership could now separate measurement correction from product improvement. The durable mechanism was “reconcile before compare.”

TRADE-OFF I MADE: I retired $185,892 of legacy ARR rather than preserve a small revenue stream that forced two products and two scoreboards. Normalization bought time; it did not substitute for the six months of product work.
FAILURE / LEARNING: I should have established the canonical-source rule before the first executive comparison. Once dashboards have constituencies, a cheap definition becomes an expensive negotiation.
  • Are Right, A Lot: I changed the decision by making both products use the same evidence.
  • Dive Deep: I exposed the remaining variance gap instead of using normalization only to flatter us.
  • Ownership: every customer received a destination and date.
SAFE-SAY: “The transition document records 79 customers, $185,892 of annual recurring revenue and 294,581 annual containers. The 90% result is the workbook record; the normalized comparison is about 27% versus 25%.”
If asked for source: “The Crux transition RAPID and my 2022 product-management record, reconciled in Apple workbook v11 A4.”

Was normalization spin?

No. It cut both ways: it removed the false 63-versus-25 alarm and exposed the unfavorable sixteen-versus-eight-day variance. That residual drove the six-month backlog.

What was yours versus the team’s?

I rebuilt the comparison, wrote the one-source rule and set the sequence. Engineering added sources; transition owners built the customer paths with me.

ADD-ON 2 · A5 — A hundred containers before a customer Real

Best owner: Rocky · LPs: Insist on Highest Standards · Deliver Results · Dive Deep · Headline: 500 containers in 20 minutes at 99.6% connectivity; dry run 100% processed and 86% tracked (record-from-docs).
20-second version
“I replaced a launch date with two evidence gates. A hundred real containers carried seven checks and every miss had a defect ID. The performance proof processed 500 containers in 20 minutes at 99.6% connectivity; the end-to-end run exposed an 86% tracked gap, which I shipped only as a named, owned workstream. Both checkpoints passed and the first pilot launched on schedule.”

S — Situation. Before Flexport’s first paying 4PL visibility customer could launch, leadership wanted a date, but the product’s promise—trustworthy tracking—had not been proven through a representative production path. If the date became the test, the customer would absorb our defects.

T — Task. I owned the readiness verdict. I had to define business and technical gates before decision week, create evidence that joined defects to real containers, and balance an imperfect tracking gap against the customer and schedule cost of indefinite delay.

A — Action. I published two go/no-go gates, both required to pass. I made a hundred real containers the UAT spine, with checks for creation, subscription, returned data, route, milestones, exceptions and webhooks. Every mismatch carried a Jira ID, so clusters—not anecdotes—burned down. I set an 80% minor-or-no-issues bar before customer-facing UAT and used named shadow accounts first. The weekly data-quality bar rose through the final four weeks, and a thin sample could not print green. I also required load evidence: 500 containers in 20 minutes at 99.6% connectivity. The end-to-end run processed everything but tracked 86%; I refused to hide that fourteen-point gap, assigned its causes and owner, and put it on the launch dashboard.

R — Result. Both checkpoints passed, defect clusters reached zero before customer exposure, and the first pilot launched on schedule. The unresolved tracking gap shipped measured and owned, not as a surprise. Decision week became a readout of pre-published evidence rather than a negotiation over the date.

TRADE-OFF I MADE: I chose evidence with a visible imperfection over either perfection-driven delay or an unmeasured launch. The cost was carrying a named follow-on workstream and defending 86% tracked in public.
FAILURE / LEARNING: I named the processed-versus-tracked gap too late. I now put the gap on the dashboard from the first test cycle.
  • Highest Standards: a date could not substitute for two published gates.
  • Deliver Results: the pilot launched on schedule after both gates passed.
  • Dive Deep: each container-level failure mapped to a defect cluster and owner.
SAFE-SAY: “The source records 500 containers in 20 minutes at 99.6% connectivity and an end-to-end run at 100% processed, 86% tracked. That was launch evidence, not a steady-state scale claim.”
If asked for source: “The launch-status guide, product-testing approach, hundred-container CSV and November 2021 program status.”
