What is Shipment Visibility? — the one-minute explainer (from the program documents; record-from-docs)

The minute. Shipment Visibility — "SV" — is Flexport's client-facing visibility product in the Client App: one pane where a client watches container milestones across their whole book, Flexport-carried and non-Flexport-carried shipments alike, so track-and-trace stops being emails to an ops desk and becomes a product the client logs into. It also created a "visibility-only" client segment — companies that bought visibility first were a deliberate upsell path into Flexport freight. The 2021 plan priced the problem it solved: roughly $3.92M a year of manual track-and-trace salary across the ops org against about $263K of vendor spend, with only 18 of 99 milestones automated at the time. record-from-docs

The machinery behind the story. Product standards: coverage 90%+, accuracy 95%, timeliness 90%, a fail-safe switch, dashboards and alarms — under the operating rule "edits are the enemy": every human correction is an automation defect, not a fix. Rollout: a free pilot in graduated waves — wave 1 two named clients (Ta Chen International, FAM Brands) around Q4 2021, wave 2 four of a seven-client pool around Q1 2022, wave 3 twenty-plus around Q2 2022, full roll-out H2 2022 — each client entering on a ~50-container calibration and graduating on six published criteria. Testing: four gates before launch — a 100-container live test, biweekly bug bashes on the client job stories, shadow accounts fed real Crux data, and UAT gated on data quality (80% of shipments minor-to-no issues). And Crux — the acquired visibility product — was transitioned onto SV by end 2022: 79 customers, $185,892 ARR, 294,581 containers. record-from-docs

Doc-vs-canon footnotes: the pilot schedule phrases the SLA criterion as "90% of verifications within 24–48h"; canon says "reviewed within 24h" — canon wins, say 24h. The 95% accuracy standard is the 2021 plan's bar for milestone data, not this story's ETA-accuracy metric (60% → 90%, canon) — never mix them. Probing — challenge Q&A tuned to this interviewer

HM — Why is this your Deliver Results story and not just a metrics story?

Because the results landed behind gates, not around them: both public targets two months early, 7 → 68 customers on the same ops team, zero rollbacks. The review and the graduation criteria are the mechanism; the −95.5% and the 9.7× are the outcome.

HM — The direct dollars on a 95% cost reduction are small. Why lead with it?

Because it is honest and because the point was never the direct dollars: at the 32K-shipment target the direct saving is ≈$50K a year, modeled on an illustrative container count. What it bought is boarding 9.7× the customers without 9.7× the ops headcount — roughly two and a half people never hired — and a rollout unlocked two months early. The ~$1.5M pre-prod figure is a separate record and I never add it to this.

HM — Sales wanted everyone boarded. How did you hold the line without becoming the blocker?

By publishing the six criteria to Sales before wave one, so the rule did the refusing. Wave 3 was held in public on an ETA-accuracy miss traced to one bad source; the customers we held became the cleanest wave, and Sales had seen the gate before it fired.

HM — You discarded a 99.4% week. Walk me through that decision.

Three touches is not a week — a 95% confidence interval on N = 3 is wider than the metric. Printing it would have taught the VP a number that could not survive the next Monday. I printed a data miss. A review that flatters once is not trusted twice.

HM — What would you do differently, and how does this translate to my team?

Start the review at kickoff, not after the critique. At Amazon this is a WBR story told to the company that invented the WBR — the respectful version is that I copied a trusted format, froze definitions with their owners, tagged inputs to outputs and let the review make the rollout decision. Graduation criteria are launch gates published before the wave and held in public.

HM — What was yours and what was the team's?

Mine: the diagnosis, the seven definitions, the Monday build by hand for over a year, the criteria, the holds, the pre-prod measurement design. The nine teams built and operated; each input owner signed their own definition; the VP decided in the room. The format came from the LSA review — I did not invent it.

HM — Why waves at all? What did the calibration actually buy?

The pilot was free, so every boarded client cost engineering, data and ops before revenue — boarding the whole pool would have spent the program's credibility on clients we could not yet serve. The ~50-container calibration per client gave the data team a verified baseline on the client's own freight before scale, and the six published criteria converted "ready" from a feeling into a rule: wave 1 two clients, wave 2 four of a seven-client pool, wave 3 twenty-plus record-from-docs. The canon version: waves boarded only through the published gates, and the held wave became the cleanest.

HM — How did you test before the first paying customer touched it?

Four gates, all from the program record record-from-docs: a 100-container live test watched end-to-end; biweekly bug bashes on the client job stories; shadow client accounts — real Crux data fed into dedicated demo accounts so the clients' own data exposed the defects; and UAT that did not start until 80% of shipments showed minor-to-no issues. UAT was gated on data quality, not on the calendar — the testing version of the graduation rule.

HM — You were rolling out while an acquired product's customers sat on the old platform. How did you handle that risk?

As a parallel migration with its own decision rules, not an afterthought record-from-docs: 79 Crux customers, $185,892 ARR and 294,581 containers, segmented — transition where SV fit, cancel honestly where it did not (two competitor SCM-SaaS accounts were canceled on published dates), per-customer calls for the largest accounts. Internal pushback on migration cost and feature parity was priced into the decision document, not overridden. The pilot gates kept quality honest while the migration ran beside it.
