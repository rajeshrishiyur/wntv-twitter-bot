1. J5 · E8 — Release and regression governance: how a change earns its way to production design case

DESIGN CASE + LIVED RECORD. The monthly SAP + integrations release train (E8) is a design I wrote in an interview-prep engagement; volumes illustrative. The record under it is lived: S15 — the Sephora 20-person Product/Engineering/QA team moved from a six-week hybrid cadence to four weeks in six weeks, QA authored the release gate, critical defects −50% (record: resume; master S15). Sephora UDC facts used here are record: hard peak change-freeze October–December; anything touching the frozen spec went through the gate with an estimate; hypercare exited into the monthly release train (record: Apple v11 B1 p. 21–22; GFiber v8 S2).
His bullet it answers: “configuration governance — pre-deployment validation, change-management SOPs, post-incident corrective actions; fewer unintended deployments; auditability across hundreds of weekly changes.”
LPs: Insist on the Highest Standards · Hire and Develop the Best · Earn Trust · Invent and Simplify  |  Headline: four-week cadence in six weeks; critical defects per release −50% (record).
60-second pitch

“At Sephora the VP asked me to move a 20-person Product, Engineering and QA team from a six-week hybrid cadence to four weeks. QA feared compressed test windows, and they were right. I had no line authority, so I made the skeptic the owner: the QA lead wrote the definition of done, I coached the frame, and we added a QA drop date with reason codes so late, heavy stories were dropped on the record instead of squeezing the test window. Four-week cadence live in six weeks; critical defects per release down 50%; neighbouring teams copied the model. Later I designed the same mechanism as a monthly release train for SAP plus integrations: scope frozen at T-minus-15, regression cells owned at the true grain, a go/no-go the QA lead owns with blocker-plus-critical at zero, rollback per transport group, and hypercare that exits on a number. The principle for your world is the same: a change rides the gate or it does not ship, and every emergency change is counted.”

S — Situation. Lived (S15, record). Newly joined at Sephora, the VP of Supply Chain asked me to convert a 20-person Product/Engineering/QA team from a six-week hybrid cadence to four-week agile. QA (a contracting firm) and in-house engineering were heading for a standoff: QA feared compressed test windows, and the Sev-1 trend was partly from story-accuracy defects. Design case (E8, design). The prompt: SAP configuration, custom objects, integration maps, the CRM, the 3PL interface and the field app all change; last month a release broke MRP. Design a train so changes ship monthly with no surprise on the floor.

T — Task. Convert the cadence in weeks, not quarters, with VP air cover and no line authority over anyone in the room — and keep escaped criticals from rising. In the design: one book of record for change (the transport/change log and the release manifest) and one primary metric — release-caused Sev 1–2 incidents per release driven to zero with the cadence held.

A — Action. What I did (lived), then what I designed on top of it.

  • Discovery (lived). One-on-ones with every person; an influencer per function; the problem statement written as “QA’s fear of compressed windows is correct.” Exit: every real objection heard privately; the QA lead agreed to author the gate.
  • Gate ownership (lived). The QA lead wrote the definition of done — entry criteria per story type at the Ready-for-QA transition. Engineering got review, not veto, and shaped the entry criteria. I wrote the success criteria with the VP: four-week cadence, no rise in escaped criticals, QA window protected by rule.
  • Risk-scoped intake (lived). Jira fields for benefit ranking and review status; a QA drop date with reason codes so a late heavy story was re-scoped on the record; QA seated on product-business calls with pre-approved scenarios; Sev-1 causes classified.
  • Freeze and change control (lived, Sephora UDC). Hard peak change-freeze October–December; anything touching the frozen spec went through the gate with an estimate, never absorbed quietly; after hypercare the design was handed into the monthly release train.
  • The train (design, E8). Commit and freeze at T−15 (definition of ready cites a regression cell) → promote to integrated QA at T−10, imported in production sequence → regression matrix plus end-to-end chain at T−5 → go/no-go at T−2, QA lead owns it: Blocker + Critical open = 0, rollback plan per transport group, freeze check → deploy → hypercare with a numeric exit. Release notes and the go/no-go record are the audit artifacts.
  • Regression scope by risk (design). Cells at the true grain (order type × fulfillment path × node × stock-state transition; interface round-trips per partner); an MRP-protection cell that diffs exception counts and planned-order totals by plant before vs. after the transport — an unexplained delta fails the build. Build the manual matrix first; automate the cells that fail most.
  • Corrective action (design). Emergency changes are allowed, counted in the postmortem and 100% retrofitted into the train; configuration and code ride separate transports so a config rollback never drags code.

R — Result.

  • Four-week cadence live in six weeks for a 20-person Product/Engineering/QA team record.
  • Critical defects per release −50% under the drop-date rule record; the QA lead defended the gate to engineering without me; neighbouring teams copied the ceremony from the user guide record.
  • Rollback (revert to six weeks) named at kickoff as a two-way door; never used record.
  • E8 target design metric: release-caused Sev 1–2 per release → 0, twelve trains a year with no skipped month, emergency changes ≤ 1 per release and 100% retrofitted design — a target, not a result.
TRADE-OFF I MADE. Options: keep six weeks; go to four weeks and absorb late stories; or go to four weeks with a drop-date rule. I chose the rule and dropped low-impact stories on the record — engineering’s date protected, QA’s window protected — and covered some of the drops myself. Cost: visible scope loss each cycle. In the design, a four-week train delays small fixes; I accept that and keep a counted emergency path rather than let urgency become a side door.
FAILURE / LEARNING. Development that depends on me staying is not development: the gate became durable only when its owner, not I, defended it. Hard feedback that I routed sideways through issues-log rows turned out partly to be genuine overload, and the evidence got those people help — I now check load before I treat pushback as resistance record: master S15 probes.
Insist on the Highest Standards — the gate could say no and survive a release-day argument; criticals −50%.
Hire and Develop the Best — I handed the load-bearing artifact to the skeptic and coached her to own it.
Earn Trust — I said publicly that QA’s fear was correct before asking for a faster cadence.
Invent and Simplify — a drop date with reason codes replaced a last-two-days bug bash.
SAFE-SAY. “The six-week conversion and the 50% cut in critical defects are my record at Sephora; the dated monthly train with T-minus-15 and the MRP diff cell is a design I wrote from that experience, not something I claim to have run at that scale.” If asked for source: “My resume line on the VP-sponsored conversion, and the master story record S15; the train is design case E8.”
20-second version

“I moved a 20-person Product, Engineering and QA team to a four-week cadence in six weeks by making the QA lead the owner of the release gate and adding a drop date with reason codes. Critical defects fell 50%. I later designed the same gate as a monthly SAP release train: frozen scope, risk-scoped regression cells, a numeric go/no-go and counted emergency changes.”

Probes James is likely to ask

1. You run hundreds of configuration changes a week, not a monthly train. What carries over?

The gate, not the calendar. I would classify changes by blast radius: low-risk rows go self-serve with automated validation and an audit row; high-risk rows (new node types, escape routes, capacity signals) ride a scheduled window with a go/no-go and a named rollback. The invariant is the same as at Sephora: nothing deploys that is not on the manifest, and every exception is counted. design

2. How did you scope regression by risk rather than testing everything?

Lived: stories were benefit-ranked in Jira and the QA lead’s entry criteria differed by story type; heavy late stories got a drop date. Design: the matrix is at the grain the failure happens — node × channel × transition — and the cells that failed most get automated first.

3. What happens when a release still breaks production?

Roll back the transport group, not the whole release; open a corrective action with a root-cause class; add the missing regression cell so the same class cannot pass again. In the design, the MRP diff cell exists because a release broke MRP. The cell, not the apology, is the corrective action.

4. How did you get engineering to accept a gate written by QA?

Review, not veto: engineering shaped the entry criteria, and I covered some of the low-impact stories they had to drop. Objection became co-ownership. Two cycles on four weeks, and the QA lead was defending the gate herself.

2. J7 · E9 — Migrating a legacy rules engine without unintended deployments: tolerance first, mocks by cause, one publisher design case

DESIGN CASE + LIVED RECORD. E9 (the ERP cutover from a legacy WMS) is a design I wrote in an interview-prep engagement; volumes illustrative. The record under it is lived: S18 — Sephora RP → JDA WMS migration across two DCs in eight months: a 0.5% reconciliation tolerance published with Finance before mock 1; three mocks at 7% → 1.8% → 0.3%; a 72-hour in-transit freeze; legacy read-only as failback; a $20M ROI pipeline of four programs unblocked (record: resume; master S18). The “one publisher” rule is lived on Sephora UDC: no SKU with two published availability values (user anchor: GFiber v8 S2). Angle for James: the governance mechanics — parity, mapping as data, validation before volume, one source of truth per node. Rocky hears S18 as a program and cutover story; do not retell the budget or the team here.
His bullet it answers: “leads migration of a legacy placement rules engine to a next-generation centralized policy platform … architecture decisions, stakeholder alignment, sequencing, training delivery” and “fewer unintended deployments.”
LPs: Insist on the Highest Standards · Dive Deep · Learn and Be Curious · Bias for Action  |  Headline: 7% → 1.8% → 0.3% against a 0.5% tolerance published before the first mock (record).
60-second pitch

“The closest thing I have run to your rules-engine migration is a WMS migration at Sephora: two DCs on a legacy system with no documentation and an eight-month deadline, and I had never run a WMS migration. The risk was the same as yours — the new platform behaves differently from the old one in a way nobody notices until it ships. So I made ‘good enough’ arithmetic before anyone tested: a 0.5% reconciliation tolerance published with Finance before the first mock. I treated mappings — units of measure, location names, status vocabularies — as data in governed tables, not code. Each mock was read by cause class, never by the headline: 7% was mapping, 1.8% was in-transit timing, 0.3% passed. At cutover a 72-hour freeze, the legacy system read-only as failback, and one rule that I designed from it: zero the old publisher before the new one publishes, so consumers read one source at a time. When hundreds of RF devices failed the conversion script, the rehearsed paper-pick fallback fired instead of a rollback; training moved 1.5 weeks and the date held.”

S — Situation. Lived (S18, record). Two Sephora DCs ran a legacy WMS with zero inherited documentation; a hard eight-month deadline gated a $20M ROI pipeline of four programs; inventory accuracy across two DCs could not wobble at cutover. I had never run a WMS migration. Design case (E9, design). A site runs a legacy system with its own book; the business wants the new platform to become the book of record with no double-promising during the switch and a Finance-grade reconciliation at go-live.

T — Task. Own the lifecycle — charter, design sign-off, interface inventory, UAT, cutover, hypercare — accountable to the sponsor and Finance, with one test of success that nobody could negotiate after the fact. Translated to a rules-engine migration: prove the new platform produces the same decisions as the old one, within a published tolerance, before it decides anything live.

A — Action. Five gates; each step is lived unless marked design.

  • Discovery — inventory before opinions. Charter written in week one with the success criteria (on the date, inside a published tolerance, zero accuracy incidents, hypercare exit under two blockers a day). Interface inventory v0 built from message logs; every interface got a named owner and a test cell. I learned the floor in month one until I could walk receive → put-away → pick → ship unaided.
  • Design — tolerance and mapping as data. The 0.5% tolerance published with Finance before mock 1. Conversion order fixed: masters → open orders → on-hand → in-transit as its own batch. A mapping-table standard: units of measure, locations and status vocabularies as governed data, not code. Stories slotted by business criticality × effort.
  • Build & Test — mocks read by cause. Three mocks on production data with written root causes. Mock 1 at 7%: units of measure, location names that mapped to nothing, order-status vocabulary. Fixed in the mapping set → 1.8%. In-transit timing → the 72-hour freeze and a separate batch → 0.3%. A total that shrank without a named cause was not evidence.
  • Cutover — one door, one publisher, rehearsed fallbacks. Written go recommendation off mock 3; the 72-hour in-transit freeze; the legacy system read-only as failback; rollback triggers per interface written in advance (mismatch above 0.5% for two days, or ship-confirm posting failing beyond two hours) — rehearsed, never used. Hundreds of RF devices failed the conversion script; I invoked the rehearsed paper-pick fallback rather than roll back and told leadership training would slip 1.5 weeks. Design (E9): zero the legacy availability first, then publish the new — never the reverse.
  • Hypercare — numeric exit. Blockers per day read daily; exit at under two a day sustained, in week 3 of 4; the exit memo carried the single-point-dependency lesson.

R — Result.

  • Reconciliation variance 7% → 1.8% → 0.3% against the 0.5% tolerance published before mock 1 record.
  • Two DCs live on JDA in eight months, on time; the $20M ROI pipeline of four programs unblocked the next month — slip avoided, not banked record.
  • Per-interface rollback rehearsed and never needed; the paper-pick fallback fired for the RF miss; training moved 1.5 weeks and the go-live date held record.
  • Hypercare exited in week 3 at under two blockers a day record. E9 target metric: cutover variance inside the published tolerance with zero double-promises design.
TRADE-OFF I MADE. Options: one mock and a long hypercare, or three full mocks. I chose three — they cost weeks that would otherwise have been spent live. I also chose the 72-hour freeze, which delayed receipts, priced with operations and scheduled off-peak. At the RF failure I chose fallback over rollback: a rollback would have reopened every converted interface to save one floor task.
FAILURE / LEARNING. The RF miss was mine: RF conversion sat on the log as a task, not a risk, because I scored it by effort when impact mattered. Device testing now precedes training, and every single-point dependency gets a what-if plan regardless of size record.
Insist on the Highest Standards — the tolerance was published before I knew I could hit it.
Dive Deep — every mock delta carried a root-cause class and an owner.
Learn and Be Curious — I had never run a WMS migration; I learned the floor in month one.
Bias for Action — the rehearsed fallback fired in minutes instead of a rollback debate.
SAFE-SAY. “Hundreds of RF devices failed the conversion script; we ran the rehearsed paper fallback, training moved a week and a half, and the go-live date held.” Never state a device count. If asked for source: “My resume line on the dual-DC migration — three mocks to a published 0.5% tolerance — and the master record S18; the rules-engine framing is design case E9.”
20-second version

“I migrated two DCs off a legacy WMS in eight months by publishing a 0.5% reconciliation tolerance with Finance before the first mock, treating mappings as governed data, and reading every mock by cause: 7%, 1.8%, then 0.3%. At cutover one source published at a time and the fallbacks were rehearsed — one fired, and the date held.”

Probes James is likely to ask

1. How would that translate to a placement rules engine with no inventory to reconcile?

The “variance” becomes decision parity: replay a frozen set of real placement requests through both engines and diff the decisions by cause class — rule missing, rule translated differently, data mapping, intended change. The tolerance is published before the first replay; intended changes are listed in advance so they are not counted as defects. design

2. How do you avoid two engines deciding at once?

One publisher per node at a time: the legacy engine stops deciding for a node before the platform starts, never the reverse, with the switch logged. On UDC the rule was no SKU with two published availability values; here it would be no node with two active rule sources.

3. What were your rollback triggers?

Written in the charter before go-live: mismatch above tolerance for two days, or posting failing beyond two hours, with rollback per interface and the legacy system read-only as the failback. Rehearsed; never used. Per-interface rollback is why one bad interface never forced a full rollback.

4. What would you do differently?

Score dependencies by impact, not effort. The RF conversion looked small, so it was a task rather than a risk. Now any dependency that can stop the floor gets a rehearsed fallback and a test before training.

3. AI-assisted configuration tooling — operator self-service with guardrails one page

Lived analog and angle. I1 / Apple A01 — an LLM incident-triage agent I built independently (Python/FastAPI), not an employer production deployment (record: Apple v11 I1, pp. 41–42). Venkata may hear I1 as a product story; James hears only the guardrails, validation and operator self-service angle.
The answer (about 60 seconds)

“I built an independent LLM triage agent as an operations product, not a demo. Three guardrails made it safe. First, the eval set came before any prompt or model change: I graded historical incidents with known-correct outcomes, and every change had to keep the past passing — a pre-deployment validation gate. Second, ‘no citation, no proposal’: every recommendation had to cite the ticket evidence and the runbook line. Third, tiered routing: easy, well-understood classes went to small models; the expensive path was reserved for the hard tail. Cost went from $25 to $0.40 per incident, a 98% cut, with accuracy held on the eval set and 100% citation coverage. For configuration tooling I would keep those three and add one: the tool proposes, a validator decides, and the operator approves a readable diff before anything deploys.”

S — The naive agent cost $25 per incident and could answer without checkable evidence, so the human repeated the work. T — Quality held on known cases, every proposal checkable, unit cost below a dollar. record

A — Eval set graded first and made the release gate; “no citation, no proposal”; cost measured per class and routine classes routed to smaller models; accuracy, citation coverage and unit cost reviewed together. record
