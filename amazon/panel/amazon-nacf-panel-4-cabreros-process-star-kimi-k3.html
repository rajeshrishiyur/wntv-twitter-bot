Light math that carries the process point (J5, modeled from the cadence)

Releases per year go 52 ÷ 6 ≈ 8.7 → 52 ÷ 4 = 13 (+50%). If critical defects per release fall 50%, then escaped criticals per year go 8.7c → 13 × 0.5c = 6.5c, about −25% in absolute escapes even while shipping 50% more often. A faster change cadence is safe only if the gate improves faster than the cadence does. The −50% is record; the per-year figure is my arithmetic on the cadence, with c being whatever the baseline count was.

For his “hundreds of weekly changes”: the same six steps scale only if steps 3–5 are automated per change class (validation is a test, not a meeting) and step 6 writes a new test whenever something escapes. That is how I’d expect “fewer unintended deployments” to be engineered.

60-second pitch

“Situation. In a design case I built for a marketplace delivery-promise role, the promised date was order time plus seller handling plus predicted transit plus a buffer, and the buffer was a flat 1.10 days on every lane. In my model of twelve lane cohorts, about a million orders a week, it printed 95.8% accuracy but only 7.6% of orders got a fast promise. Task. Turn a hand-tuned rule into a governed policy without breaking the 95% accuracy floor. Action. I decomposed accuracy into seller × carrier × Φ(buffer/σ), showed the flat buffer was 5.0 standard deviations on the tightest cohort and 0.69 on the widest, put four policies on one priced table, and recommended one dial, b = 1.645σ, validated in four shadow weeks before any customer saw a date. Result. In the model, fast-promise share goes 7.6% to 39.6% at exactly 95%, and the average promise drops from 3.09 to 2.75 days. The dial is printed in the weekly review header, with a named owner. The lived version of this is my Elementum lead-time work: 200 lanes, promise at the tail, 99% accuracy.”

S — A marketplace promises delivery on inventory it never touches: seller picks, a partially controlled carrier carries, and the customer blames the marketplace. Promised date = order time + configured handling + predicted transit + buffer. The incumbent buffer was uniform, about 1.10 days, set once and owned by nobody between reviews. Model scale: 12 lane cohorts, ~1M orders/week, σ from 0.22 to 1.60 days (illustrative). Baselines in the model: accuracy 95.8% vs a 95% floor, average promised day 3.09, fast-promise share (≤2 days) 7.6%, weekly modeled profit $5.69M. The real baseline: no governed owner of the speed-vs-accuracy trade.

T — My role in the case was promise-policy lead: decompose where the buffer was wasted and where it was thin, put priced alternatives on one table, design the validation gates, and leave a governance instrument, not a tuning habit. The constraint: hold 95% accuracy on every cohort. The model’s stated economics: a breach costs about $12 against about $6.50 of contribution margin (model assumptions, not a measured P&L).

A — Five gated phases, as I designed them:

  • Discovery: find the defect. Wrote the accuracy factorization (P(seller ships in window) × P(carrier meets transit) × Φ(b/σ)) and the buffer-waste audit. The indictment in two lines: 1.10 ÷ 0.22 = z 5.0 (Φ ≈ 99.99997%) on the tightest cohort; 1.10 ÷ 1.60 = z 0.69 (Φ ≈ 75.4%) on the widest. Exit: the question changes from “how much buffer” to “where should it sit.”
  • Plan & Design: options on one table. A uniform (incumbent) · B σ-proportional, one dial k · C minimum-days at the floor · D profit-optimal on the priced breach. Gates designed up front: shadow mode, a promotion bar, a warm rollback. Exit: the dial k named as a published policy instrument with an owner.
  • Build & Test: validate before deploying. Φ(1.645) = 0.95 holds the floor on every cohort by construction; the Q4 drill (σ × ~1.45) shows a frozen summer dial printing 91.9%, below the floor, so a re-solve rule is written. Exit: every policy reproducible independently by the engineering/data-science owners.
  • Cutover: shadow, then promote. Challenger computed and logged per order for four consecutive weeks, never displayed; promote only if would-be accuracy holds the floor on logged live orders with no rise in contact signals. Rollback = one flag back to the incumbent. Exit: promotion bar published before shadow week 1.
  • Hypercare: close the loop. k printed in the Monday review header; p90 error and buffer exceedance next to the mean; k re-solved per regime (October for peak). Exit: a business owner, not data science, owns the dial.

Mine vs theirs (in the case): mine were the factorization, the waste audit, the four-policy table, the proofs and the gates. The engine, the transit models and σ estimates belong to engineering and data science; the breach price belongs to Finance.

Trade-off I made

Options: per-lane ML-learned buffers (data science’s preference) vs one governed dial. Chose: the dial first. Rejected: learned buffers, because they add value on paper but governance is invisible, and nobody can audit a hundred micro-decisions on a Monday. Cost: the widest lanes got honestly slower promises (2.63-day buffer in the model), and the last increment of profit (policy D) was left as a Finance decision. Metric: fast share at the held floor.

Failure / learning. From the lived F4 record: I shipped the lane distributions before the drift flag, and a lane quietly degraded until a planner caught it by hand. In this design, the regime re-solve and the tail metrics ship with the dial, not after.

Probing Q&A

Q1. Why not let ML learn per-lane buffers? Isn’t that more accurate?

Possibly more profit on paper, but it’s ungovernable: a hundred micro-decisions no business owner can audit. The dial captures most of the value, and when σ estimates improve, that flows into speed visibly. I put the ML option on the table as a priced row, so the no was a decision, not an opinion. For a placement rules platform I’d apply the same rule: a parameter a human owns beats a policy nobody can read.

Q2. How do you validate a policy change before customers see it?

Shadow mode: the challenger is computed and logged per order for four weeks, never displayed. It’s promoted only if would-be accuracy holds the floor on logged live orders with no rise in contacts. The bar is published before shadow week one, and rollback is one flag.

Q3. What breaks this at peak?

Variance regime change. In the model, σ × ~1.45 drops a frozen summer dial to 91.9%. So k is re-solved per regime and published in October. Never frozen through peak.

Q4. Did you deliver this?

No. It’s a design case I built for a marketplace delivery-promise role. What I delivered is the same idea at Elementum: measured lead-time distributions on 200 lanes, promise at the 99th percentile, 580,000 units and about $1.5M of carrying cost released.

Probing Q&A

Q1. How is this a capacity signal for a network?

The Andon is a throttle at the grain where variance lives. A cell breaching at 2× baseline gets its fast badge pulled or its buffer widened automatically, so a degrading node or lane stops taking promises it can’t keep. For a new node type I’d wire the same: a sustained signal, a minimum sample, an automatic action, and an auto-restore.

Q2. How do you stop the WBR from becoming a viewing?

Pre-wire rule: reds arrive with cause, owner and date or they count as a process miss. The meeting decides; it never discovers.

Q3. What’s the verify step for?

To falsify the lever map itself. If an input moved and its output didn’t, the roll-up hypothesis is wrong, and that gets written down. That’s what keeps the instrument honest after I leave.

Q4. Have you run a WBR for real?

Yes, at Flexport; you may have heard about it yesterday. This design is where I carried the decomposition math and the control loop furthest, as a design case.

Probing Q&A

Q1. How does this apply to non-specialists self-serving network changes?

Same three guardrails: show the outcome before the flip (shadow on their own data), make a bad flip reversible, and measure retention of the change, not just the count of changes. An AI-assisted config tool should preview the downstream effect and refuse a change it can’t validate.

Q2. Why all-or-nothing?

Partial adoption lets a seller keep the risky declared handling and still claim protection. The all-or-nothing bundle makes the hedge self-selecting: a seller who trusts their own ops keeps control; one who doesn’t converts.

Q3. What would kill the program?

One protection promise the scorecard fails to honor. So the kill condition is written before launch: stop enrolment until it’s fixed.

Q4. What did you actually deliver?

Nothing in production; this is a design case. The delivered adoption-behind-gates record is Flexport: 7 to 68 customers behind published graduation criteria.
