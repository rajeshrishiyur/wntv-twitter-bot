Analytics — How was 70 → 85% measured, and on what?

Accuracy = 1 − MAPE on the scoped class-A SKUs, with bias published alongside it from month two, at the SKU-family grain the planners decide on. The planners owned the forecast; I owned the measurement and the input integrity — I have not owned a demand forecast end-to-end and won't claim it.

Analytics — Why exclude unfirmed MRP planned orders from supply? That understates it.

Deliberately. Unfirmed orders breathe every MRP run; counting them amplifies noise and inflates supply, which is exactly how the line-down surprises happened. Released planned orders only — accepted by planners before the first screen was built.

Analytics — The $2M — how do I know it is not a prettier average?

Because of the release rule: buffer came out only on SKU families where forecast error and lead-time error had both demonstrably improved, signed by Planning and Finance. That is why the release survived audit. It is one-time working capital; the recurring value around it is my model and labeled.

Analytics — Your expedite row is the softest. Defend it.

Agreed — about sixty expedites a year pre-empted at a $5K premium, both illustrative. The record is the mechanism: the alert fires at days-of-cover ≤ lane p90. To firm it I would pull the expedited-freight ledger for the six sites twelve months before and after the alert went live and count the premium directly.

Analytics — Why p90 for the alert threshold and p99 for the promise (S8)?

Different decisions. The alert is a re-plan trigger — p90 gives the planner a window before the tail bites. The promise is a commitment — it sits at the tail you can defend. Same measured distribution, two policies read off it.

Analytics — Define your two error metrics: MAPE versus bias. Which one steered?

MAPE is mean absolute percentage error — how wrong the forecast is on average, direction ignored; bias is the signed mean error — whether it runs systematically high or low. MAPE without bias hides a chronic over-forecast inside a decent average, and a chronic over-forecast is exactly how buffer quietly accumulates. So accuracy was reported as 1 − MAPE on the scoped class-A SKUs at the SKU-family grain, with bias published alongside from month two — and the release rule required forecast error and lead-time error both to improve, so neither metric could carry the decision alone.

Probing — challenge Q&A tuned to this interviewer

Analytics — Why p99 and not p95? That is a lot of buffer.

Per lane, the cost of a line-down on a fat-tailed lane dwarfed the carrying cost of the extra day. I showed the asymmetry lane by lane and let planning choose — they chose p99. On reliable lanes the measured σ was so far below the brochure that p99 still released buffer; on fat-tailed lanes the buffer stayed.

Analytics — How did you compute p99 on skewed lanes?

Cumulatively from the empirical distribution — the smallest d with P(L ≤ d) ≥ 0.99 — not μ + 2.33σ. On one right-skewed lane the normal shortcut understated the true p99 by about 3.4 days; that lane is why the definition is cumulative.

Analytics — Is the $1.5M measured?

The release is record: 580K units out, zero stockouts in the watch window, Finance signed each lane. The ≈$2.59/unit is the derived average. What is modeled is the recurring value around it — carry at 20% all-in (capital-only would be ≈$150K at 10%) and avoided expedites — each input labeled.

Analytics — The expedite number assumes what?

Ten thousand shipments a year on the 200 lanes and one late arrival in ten forcing an expedite before p99 — illustrative. I would pull the expedite ledger by lane for the year before and after and compare directly.

Analytics — What is lead-time accuracy, exactly?

1 − |actual − promised| / promised, tracked per lane against the p99 promise; the bar was 99% on the whole 200-lane set. Below it, no release — written into the act before anyone asked.

Analytics — At what grain did you measure lead time, and over what window?

At the lane grain, from actual ship-to-receipt durations — one empirical distribution per lane, never a network average. The record carries the per-lane distributions, the refresh cadence and the drift flag (p99 recomputed on refresh; the buffer decision re-opens if accuracy falls); it does not carry a quoted window length, and I will not invent one — what mattered design-wise was a window long enough to hold seasonality and recent enough that a dead lane's history could not vote, with the drift flag as the safety net either way.

Analytics — What does "99% lead-time accuracy" tolerate, exactly?

The metric is a ratio — 1 − |actual − promised| / promised — so the tolerance scales with the lane instead of being a flat day-count: on a 5-day lane the debit starts at roughly a quarter-day of miss; on a 30-day lane, about a day and a half. A flat day tolerance would have been strict on short lanes and blind on long ones. The bar was 99% across the whole 200-lane set before any buffer released — per-lane measurement, no pooling to hide a bad lane inside a good average.

Shadow / closing — How does the promise stay honest after you leave?

Drift flag per lane with a refresh cadence: p99 is recomputed on refresh, and the buffer decision re-opens if accuracy falls. The stockout-and-expedite watch became a standing report owned by planning.
