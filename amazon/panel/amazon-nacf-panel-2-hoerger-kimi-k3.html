Situation / Task — the business process problem, scope, scale, baselines

S — A strategic manufacturing customer with six sites: planners learned about a component shortage when the line stopped; S&OP ran off private spreadsheets; shipment data was 60% complete; eight data domains lived in SAP and site systems but never met. Leadership's question — "which customer orders are at risk this quarter?" — had no data path.

The Plan process underneath was running on an unsafe definition of supply (unfirmed MRP orders counted because they looked better), an unmeasured lead-time error, and a shortage signal that arrived at line-down. The cost: expedites instead of re-plans, buffers sized on noise, and planners spending ~40% of their week reconciling rather than root-causing.

T — Nobody owned the seam — no spec, no agreed grain, no definition of "supply," no data-quality bar. I chose all four, as owner of the definitions, the data-integrity program and the delivery. Goal: a real-time supply/demand view with honest definitions and a shortage visible a lead-time early — and planner trust, which was the real product.

Baselines on the table: forecast accuracy 70% on scoped class-A SKUs · shortage detected at line-down (target: a lead-time early) · shipment-data completeness 60% · planner effort on reconciliation and firefighting (≈40% of the week, illustrative share; the −50% is record).

Action — the process / operating-model redesign, in gated phases
  • Discovery (4–6 wks) — profiled every domain (completeness, latency, joinability) to my own gap templates; wrote the definitions memo — grain SKU × location × date; supply = on-hand + production plan + released planned orders only — and the metrics tree (OTIF, inventory $, MAPE/bias, lane lead-time accuracy, completeness, latency, adoption). Refused Planning's request to count unfirmed MRP orders as supply. Exit bar: signed gap findings per domain; scope frozen in writing; planners agree the supply definition before any screen.
  • Plan & Design (parallel) — master-data foundation plan; MPN mapping worklist with its coverage gate; domain-wave sequence by trust; DQ scorecard spec; the 10-step onboarding playbook. Exit bar: master-data gates green; unmapped-MPN list under threshold with a named owner; wave order signed by domain owners — who could fail their own wave.
  • Build & Test (8–12 wks) — domain waves behind published DQ gates — inventory and production first (numbers planners already believed), then purchasing/ASN, then transportation; shortage-alert rule specified (days-of-cover vs lane lead-time p90); daily reconciliation to MD04/COOIS/VA05/MB52; weekly root-cause loop with false alarms worked as defects. Exit bar: per-domain completeness ≥ gate; reconciliation passing daily; false alarms trending down; pilot planners using the alert.
  • Cutover & Go-Live (4 wks) — views live for pilot planners with the at-risk list replacing the Monday spreadsheet; nobody mandated off the spreadsheet — rollback stayed free. Exit bar: planners answer "where is my product" from the platform in the live S&OP meeting.
  • Hypercare / Close (4 wks) — exception workbench staffed; weekly root-cause reviews handed to domain owners; the safety-stock release rule signed with Planning and Finance — release only where forecast error AND lead-time error both improved. Exit bar: blockers/day under threshold two consecutive weeks; the release survives audit.

Influence and ownership. Mine: the definitions memo, the metrics tree, the DQ scorecard spec, the wave sequence, the release rule and the onboarding playbook. The client's SAP teams and our integration engineers built the feeds; planners owned the forecast — I owned its measurement (MAPE/bias per SKU family) and the release rule; domain owners held sign-off over their own gates. The MPN worklist gated longer than planned — escalated to the sponsor as a re-sequenced date with the coverage number attached, not a slip.

Designed the process; others own it now. I designed the definitions, the DQ gates, the release rule and the onboarding playbook; domain owners now run the gates and the weekly root-cause reviews, and planners run the alert without me. Trade-off put to leadership. The recommendation to Planning and Finance outlined the competing metrics — working capital released vs service risk — and resolved it as a rule: release only where forecast error and lead-time error both improved.

Probing — challenge Q&A tuned to this interviewer

Analytics — How was 70 → 85% measured, and on what?

Accuracy = 1 − MAPE on the scoped class-A SKUs, with bias published alongside it from month two, at the SKU-family grain the planners decide on. The planners owned the forecast; I owned the measurement and the input integrity — I have not owned a demand forecast end-to-end and won't claim it.

Analytics — Why exclude unfirmed MRP planned orders from supply? That understates it.

Deliberately. Unfirmed orders breathe every MRP run; counting them amplifies noise and inflates supply, which is exactly how the line-down surprises happened. Released planned orders only — accepted by planners before the first screen was built.

Analytics — The $2M — how do I know it is not a prettier average?

Because of the release rule: buffer came out only on SKU families where forecast error and lead-time error had both demonstrably improved, signed by Planning and Finance. That is why the release survived audit. It is one-time working capital; the recurring value around it is my model and labeled.

Analytics — Your expedite row is the softest. Defend it.

Agreed — about sixty expedites a year pre-empted at a $5K premium, both illustrative. The record is the mechanism: the alert fires at days-of-cover ≤ lane p90. To firm it I would pull the expedited-freight ledger for the six sites twelve months before and after the alert went live and count the premium directly.

Analytics — Why p90 for the alert threshold and p99 for the promise (S8)?

Different decisions. The alert is a re-plan trigger — p90 gives the planner a window before the tail bites. The promise is a commitment — it sits at the tail you can defend. Same measured distribution, two policies read off it.

Shadow / closing — What would you do differently?

Start the MPN-mapping worklist on day one — it is never "done" and it gated more than I planned. And define supply honestly before any screen, which held.

Probing — challenge Q&A tuned to this interviewer

Analytics — Why p99 and not p95? That is a lot of buffer.

Per lane, the cost of a line-down on a fat-tailed lane dwarfed the carrying cost of the extra day. I showed the asymmetry lane by lane and let planning choose — they chose p99. On reliable lanes the measured σ was so far below the brochure that p99 still released buffer; on fat-tailed lanes the buffer stayed.

Analytics — How did you compute p99 on skewed lanes?

Cumulatively from the empirical distribution — the smallest d with P(L ≤ d) ≥ 0.99 — not μ + 2.33σ. On one right-skewed lane the normal shortcut understated the true p99 by about 3.4 days; that lane is why the definition is cumulative.

Analytics — Is the $1.5M measured?

The release is record: 580K units out, zero stockouts in the watch window, Finance signed each lane. The ≈$2.59/unit is the derived average. What is modeled is the recurring value around it — carry at 20% all-in (capital-only would be ≈$150K at 10%) and avoided expedites — each input labeled.

Analytics — The expedite number assumes what?

Ten thousand shipments a year on the 200 lanes and one late arrival in ten forcing an expedite before p99 — illustrative. I would pull the expedite ledger by lane for the year before and after and compare directly.

Analytics — What is lead-time accuracy, exactly?

1 − |actual − promised| / promised, tracked per lane against the p99 promise; the bar was 99% on the whole 200-lane set. Below it, no release — written into the act before anyone asked.

Shadow / closing — How does the promise stay honest after you leave?

Drift flag per lane with a refresh cadence: p99 is recomputed on refresh, and the buffer decision re-opens if accuracy falls. The stockout-and-expedite watch became a standing report owned by planning.
