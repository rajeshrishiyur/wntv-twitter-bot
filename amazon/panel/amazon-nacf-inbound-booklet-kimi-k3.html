The 60-second answer — "what is NACF inbound?"

Say this version out loud, then stop

"Inbound is everything from the vendor or seller's dock to the moment an item is pickable in front of a customer promise — and it is managed as two coupled problems. Capacity: three physical currencies — dock-appointment slots, labor hours, and cubic storage — planned from the demand forecast through a receive plan and a UPH-based labor plan, with peak buffers sized off the forecast distribution, not the average. Inventory: where the units land and how long they last — placement by distance-to-customer against cost, weeks of cover by SKU, safety stock positioned where the variability actually is, and a transfer loop that rebalances what placement got wrong. The information flow travels ahead of the freight: forecast → inbound plan → placement decision → capacity reservation, and the actuals come back through the WBR as variance. My job in a seat like this is to keep that loop honest — measured lead times, not brochure ones; buffers released on evidence; capacity added against the constraint that actually binds."

Everything after this page unpacks that paragraph. The discipline that makes it credible: every mechanism carries its label, and every quantity in the worked examples can be recomputed from the row above it — do the arithmetic out loud in the interview and the fluency reads as experience.

The one mental model to keep on top. Inbound is a reservation system feeding a positioning system: you reserve capacity (slots, hours, cube) against a plan, and you position inventory (placement, cover, buffers) against demand. Most inbound failures are one system lying to the other — freight arrives that capacity never reserved, or inventory sits where demand isn't. The metrics in §2–§3 exist to catch exactly those two lies early.

3 · Inventory management, inbound lens — where the units land and how long they last

Capacity decides how fast freight moves; inventory decisions decide whether the right units are in the right building. This is the hiring manager's home turf — placement was her EU IXD charter — so the placement math (INV-C) gets said slowly, with the constraint and the shadow price named.

Placement strategy — distance-to-customer vs cost

The mechanism. Amazon positions inventory close to forecasted demand so that the outbound leg — the expensive, promise-bound leg — is short [well-known public Amazon practice]. Inbound placement is the upstream half of that decision: which node receives the units, given regional demand, node capacity and inbound cost. The trade is never "cheapest inbound" — it is distance-weighted delivered cost under a capacity constraint, with the customer promise as the boundary condition. Speed and cost are one table, not two arguments.

Network safety-stock positioning. Because pooling variability upstream beats duplicating buffers everywhere [industry standard], the cheap place to hold uncertainty is upstream (AWD-style bulk) and the fast place is forward (FC pickface); the worked question is how much buffer each layer carries (INV-B) and when a transfer beats a stockout (INV-D).
