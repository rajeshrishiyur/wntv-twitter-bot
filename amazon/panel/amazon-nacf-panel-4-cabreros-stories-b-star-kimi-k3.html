1 · S17 — Elementum: the semi-annual carrier bid — a lane master and explicit constraint rules cut the cycle from about six weeks to about three lived record

Company / role: Elementum, customer transportation team · I owned requirements, the constraint rules and the parallel run — not the award (Transportation and Procurement ran it).
LPs: Invent and Simplify (primary) · Dive Deep (the time audit) · Frugality · Earn Trust (the dollar I refused to claim).
Headline: carrier-bid cycle about six weeks → about three (record); pilot region finished three weeks early (record); back-test mismatch under 5% against the last two awards before the optimizer could run (record, the published gate). No award dollar claimed.
His bullet: policy platform migration (rules engine → explicit, reviewable rules) · pre-deployment validation (the back-test gate) · AI/tool-assisted configuration for non-specialists.

60-second pitch

Say this, then stop on the primary metric

Situation. The customer's transportation team ran a carrier bid twice a year: hundreds of lanes, dozens of carrier rate files, constraints like incumbency floors and volume commitments. Each cycle took about six weeks and everyone in the room called it an optimization problem. Task. I owned the requirements, the constraint rules and the parallel run — Transportation, Procurement and engineering did not report to me, and I never owned the award. Action. I sat through one full cycle logging hours, and the audit contradicted the room: the six weeks went to reconciling lane names and mode codes across carriers, not to analysis. So I wrote a lane master — one normalized origin, destination and mode key — and turned every tribal constraint into one explicit, written rule. I published a validation gate before any optimizer ran: the rules had to reproduce the last two real awards with under five percent mismatch, and every scenario had to be explainable in one sentence to an incumbent carrier. I rejected a black-box cheapest-lane solver in writing. Then I parallel-ran one region's real bid, tool against manual. Result. The pilot region finished three weeks early and the cycle went from about six weeks to about three; the other regions adopted the tool without a mandate because each region's own constraints lived in it as rules. I do not claim a dollar on the award — the people who ran the negotiation own that.

Probes — answer the one asked, then stop

Q1. Why does this count as process improvement rather than a tool build?

Because the artifact that mattered was the rule set and the gate, not the code. The lane master made the data configurable by non-specialists — a regional analyst could add a lane or a constraint as a row, not a ticket — and the back-test gate was a pre-deployment validation that stopped a bad configuration from reaching an award. Engineering built to that spec; the process was mine.

Q2. How did you validate the rules before anyone trusted the output?

Against two historical awards: the model's award had to match the actual award on more than 95% of lanes evaluated. Every mismatch was classified — naming, mode code, constraint semantics — with an owner, in one read-out. That is where most of the reconciliation logic got fixed, and the optimizer did not run below the bar.

Q3. What went wrong, and what did you change?

Procurement was not in discovery. "Incumbency floor" meant different things to Transportation and the category manager, and the rule carried only one meaning. I brought Procurement into the constraint definitions late, and for the next cycle they were in discovery on day one. The checklist change: a rule's words are owned by every function that uses them.

Q4. Surely the faster bid saved freight dollars?

Possibly, and that claim belongs to the people who ran the award. What I can defend is the cycle halving, the five-percent back-test and the analyst hours returned — the hours modeled and labeled. Refusing the dollar I cannot defend is what keeps the ones I do defend credible.

60-second pitch

Say this, then stop on the primary metric

Situation. Transportation at the customer's Austria site could not measure on-time delivery. The report said data quality was 40%, the team spent eight to ten hours a week reconciling promised against actual dates by hand, and everyone — the site, the carrier account teams, my own sponsor — believed the carriers were late. Task. I owned the analysis and the rules design. Action. I pulled three years of shipment history in SQL myself and ran a defect census: missing rates, wrong-grain lanes, five spellings of one carrier, brochure transit times posing as promises. True quality was about 55%, not 40%, and the problem was measurement, not carriers. I designed the promised-delivery-date as a routing-guide rules engine — lookup by ship-from, ship-to, mode, volume band, Incoterm and SLA, with a no-hit paging a human instead of defaulting a carrier. Before any dollar was claimed I had Finance sign the savings method: contracted guide rate minus paid rate, executed shipments only. I treated defects as classes with reason-code alerts and a weekly burn-down, and I would not release the carrier scorecard until automated on-time delivery matched a manual sample per lane cohort. Result. Data quality went from about 55% true to about 99% within a quarter; manual reconciliation fell from eight to ten hours a week to one or two; the carriers never disputed the scorecard. Austria delivered $500K a year on the signed method, and the same rules file plus one data-quality cycle per site rolled to 25 EU sites, about $8M.

Q1. How is this a configuration-governance story?

The routing guide is a rules engine; a no-hit paged a human rather than defaulting a carrier, which is the "no unintended deployment" principle. The scorecard had a pre-release gate (automated equals manual sample per cohort). And the post-incident loop was corrective action at the defect-class level — alerts by reason code, burned down weekly — not one-off fixes.

Q2. Why pull the data yourself instead of asking the team?

Because the room already had an explanation. Three years in SQL was faster than a request queue and could not be argued with. Once the sponsor saw every blank classified, the reframe held.

Q3. How did you make the rollout to 25 sites repeatable?

Each site cost a rules file and one data-quality cycle. A regional super-user ran the file after two shadowed rule reviews using a one-page runbook: add a lane, read a no-hit, when to escalate. That is a change-management SOP; adding a site took days.

Q4. What would you do differently?

Start with the industry benchmark for lane transit times instead of disproving the brochure lane by lane — same answer, weeks faster. And I would publish the scorecard gate on day one so the sponsor's impatience had a visible finish line.

60-second pitch

Say this, then stop on the primary metric

Situation. Newly joined at Sephora, the VP of Supply Chain asked me to move a 20-person Product, Engineering and QA team from a six-week hybrid cadence to four-week agile.
