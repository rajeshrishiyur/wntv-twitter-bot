V1 · S11 — Flexport Shipment Visibility as a product: 7 → 68 customers behind six published graduation criteria

LPs Customer Obsession · Deliver Results · Earn Trust · Insist on the Highest Standards

20-second version. Leadership told me the visibility program was not showing value. I was program owner across nine teams and about a hundred people. I published six graduation criteria to Sales before wave one, held wave three in public on an ETA-accuracy miss from one bad source, and threw away a flattering 99.4% week. We went from 7 to 68 customers with zero rollbacks, and cost to serve fell from $0.66 to $0.03 per container.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. Sales wanted the whole pool boarded at once. I chose waves behind criteria, which cost us speed in the first two quarters; the VP still unlocked the 68-customer rollout two months early.

Failure / learning. I started the weekly review after the critique, not at kickoff. Next time the review starts on day one.

Three levels deep. L1: I wrote the seven metric definitions and the six graduation criteria. L2: the owner of each input — Data Services, Support, GTM — signed the definition they would be red against; a 300-container sample gave ±1.6% at 95% confidence. L3: what proves it held is that the customers we held in wave three became the cleanest wave.

SAFE-SAY. “Seven to sixty-eight customers is 9.7 times, and cost to serve per container went from sixty-six cents to three cents — about ninety-five percent.” Source? “The State of SV and pilot roll-out documents, and the master packet's launch record.”

V2 · Crux customer transition — 79 customers, a segment rule each, honest cancels

LPs Customer Obsession · Earn Trust · Are Right, A Lot · Have Backbone

20-second version. Flexport had acquired a visibility product, Crux, with 79 customers — $185,892 of ARR and 294,581 containers in 2021 — and the aim was to move them to Shipment Visibility by end 2022. Not every segment fit. The decision gave each segment a rule: shippers to Shipment Visibility, forwarders, customs brokers and 3PLs to the SV API with no extra dev, two brokers to a partner product, and the two competitor SaaS accounts cancelled on published dates.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. Building parity features for small segments versus a clean path or a clean cancel. The decision chose no extra build; the cost was some revenue and some goodwill in the long tail.

Failure / learning. The dissent in the document asked for the cost of keeping Crux running; that number should have been in the first draft, not requested by a reviewer.

Three levels deep. L1: the segment table — GKA 2, Enterprise 14, SMB 39 to SV. L2: the two brokers carried most of the volume, so they got individual calls; one relationship mattered because of a 600-truck drayage partner. L3: cancels dated August 1 and October 24, 2022 are the proof that the hard part was done, not deferred.

SAFE-SAY. “Seventy-nine customers, about $186 thousand of ARR — I do not claim any preserved-ARR number, because the document doesn't have one.” Source? “The RAPID decision document for the Crux customer transition, the 2022 version.”

V3 · S14 — Manhattan: the $2.5M OMS↔WMS discrepancy my manager wanted fixed quietly

LPs Earn Trust · Have Backbone · Customer Obsession · Bias for Action

20-second version. Two days before an executive review, OMS and WMS did not reconcile — about 200 orders, $2.5M of order value — and a $10M initiative rode on the review. My manager and the room favored a quiet back-end fix. I built the discrepancy census by cause, argued risk not virtue, offered to run the war room myself, and we told the customer first. The prioritized orders were patched line by line, and the initiative was won.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. Quiet script versus transparency. I chose transparency and put the cost — my time in a war room, my risk of being wrong in front of the customer — on myself.

Failure / learning. I should have proposed the standing reconciliation report before there was a crisis to justify it.

Three levels deep. L1: the census by cause and the options memo were mine; the consultants traced the handshake and wrote the patch. L2: a cross-table script written in two days had no rollback; per-line patching did. L3: the standing reason-coded reconciliation report, read before every executive review, is what outlived the war room.

SAFE-SAY. “The $2.5 million is order value in discrepancy; the $10 million is the initiative the customer awarded — I don't count it as savings.” Source? “My Manhattan consulting record, as captured in the master packet.”

V5 · S9 — Elementum: a thousand lanes asked, 597 built first

LPs Have Backbone · Customer Obsession · Insist on the Highest Standards · Success and Scale

20-second version. The customer's Global Head of Supply Chain wanted inbound visibility on a thousand lanes in two months; data completeness was 60% and the high-value lanes were blind. I built the spend Pareto — 597 lanes carried 80% of freight spend — pre-validated it with Transportation and Procurement, and took it two levels above my sponsor. I published a 95% valid-ASN gate and held a cohort twice when it failed on a timestamp format. Completeness reached 95%.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. All thousand lanes at once versus spend-weighted cohorts. I chose cohorts and an XML/portal floor so small carriers could stay in the standard; the cost was telling an executive no on lane count.

Failure / learning. Automating dirty data manufactures confident wrong answers; I would publish the gate even earlier, in the kickoff deck.

Three levels deep. L1: I owned the scope analysis and the onboarding standard; engineers built the maps. L2: the lane score was spend × criticality × mode × volume, and no lane owner could contradict the cut. L3: freight spend fell about 10%, roughly $2M in six months, and about 200 lanes followed behind the same gate.

SAFE-SAY. “A thousand lanes were asked; 597 carrying 80% of spend went first — that is spend share, not lane count.” Source? “The Elementum program record in the master packet, S9.”

V6 · S13 — Manhattan: Cyber Monday, 4 AM, the scheduler that died

LPs Bias for Action · Customer Obsession · Deliver Results · Dive Deep

20-second version. I was the OMS consultant on allocation and promising for a retailer with 3 DCs and 100 stores. At 4 AM on Cyber Monday the VP called: backorders piling up with inventory present, about $6M of sales at risk against a 95% fill goal. I ranked clears by promise at risk instead of first-in-first-out, rebalanced so no DC drowned, and logged every move. The root cause was an allocation scheduler that died silently at the daylight-saving change. We delivered 99% fill and protected about $8M of peak sales.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. Act before knowing the cause, or wait. I acted, with every rebalance reversible and timestamped.

Failure / learning. Nobody had written a clearing rule for failure — including me. Now the rule is written before the peak.

Three levels deep. L1: I owned the response and the post-mortem mechanisms; engineering fixed the scheduler once I isolated it. L2: the contradiction — available to promise above zero while backorders rose — was my evidence to declare an incident. L3: the heartbeat, DST checklist, 3σ page rule and rehearsed rebalance playbook meant the next peak ran boring.

SAFE-SAY. “About eight million of peak sales protected against about six million at risk — both are the record; any margin figure is my model.” Source? “My Manhattan consulting record in the master packet, S13.”

V7 · I1 — independent LLM triage agent: eval first, no citation no proposal

LPs Invent and Simplify · Frugality · Learn and Be Curious · Dive Deep

20-second version. I built an independent LLM triage agent for operational incident queues and treated it as a product, not a demo. The naive version cost $25 per incident and could answer without evidence. I graded historical incidents into an eval set, made it the release gate, required every proposal to cite the ticket and runbook, and routed easy classes to smaller models. Cost fell to $0.40 per incident, 98%, with 100% citation coverage and accuracy held on the eval set.

TRADE-OFF I MADE. One expensive model for everything versus tiered routing. I chose routing, which added complexity and eval maintenance.

Failure / learning. The early eval set grew while I was judging prompts; I would freeze and version the baseline before the first change.

Three levels deep. L1: I built the stack, the eval harness and the operating rule myself. L2: cost by class showed most incidents were easy; small models held on those against the eval set. L3: at FBA scale, access review, data boundaries and drift become programs of their own; I'd keep a human approval gate until production evidence beats the human baseline.

SAFE-SAY. “This was my independent build, not an employer production program.” Source? “My build records, captured in the Apple workbook, I1.”
