2 · Bar Raiser playbook NEW

What a Bar Raiser is actually testing

LP depth, not LP coverageOne story, three levels down. A story that only works at level one counts as a "mild concern" in the AIQB rubric. The proof is a mechanism you can name (the gate, the ruler, the log), plus the number it moved and the date.
Ownership vs "we"Every sentence that carries a claim starts with "I". Every sentence about the build names who built it. He will ask "what exactly did you do?" at least twice, so answer with an artifact you wrote: the rules spec, the RAID log, the trace-ID proposal, the eval set.
Data-driven honestyThe basis word goes with every number: record, from the doc, my estimate, modeled. A Bar Raiser rewards "I don't know, and here is how I would find out" over a confident decimal.
Raising the barWould this person raise the average of the team? Show that standards were set before the pressure arrived (the 99% data gate, the <5% mismatch gate, the 98% pick bar), and that a control outlived the program.
Long-term thinkingThings I gave up in the short term for the long term: the engine waited for the data, the cheap path was proved on an eval set first, and the access review now sits on every kickoff checklist.
Self-awarenessA real failure, owned in the first sentence, with the root cause in my own process and a standing control that proves the lesson stuck.

How the three-level dig goes, and the pre-emptive line for each level

Level 1, "What exactly did YOU do?" Pre-empt it inside the Action: "I wrote… I ran… I decided…; engineering built…; Finance signed…". End the Action with one sentence that separates mine from theirs.

Level 2, "What was the data? How did you know?" Pre-empt it with the baseline number, the measurement grain and the source in the Situation: "three years of shipment history I pulled in SQL", "836 rows, March 2024 to October 2025".

Level 3a, "Who disagreed, and were they right?" Name a real dissenter and concede the part where they were right: the procurement team I brought in late, QA's fear of compressed testing, leadership pulling my engineers.

Level 3b, "What would you do differently?" One concrete change in my own process. It is never "communicate more". Examples: build the audit ledger during the pilot, version the eval set on day one, ship the quick-reference guide earlier.

Level 3c, "What happened after?" The control that outlived me: Finance reused the savings method, other regions adopted the tool without a mandate, the trace ID became the standard way to debug.

Pre-empt technique (say it once, early): "I'll tell you what I owned, what the team built, and the number with its source. Stop me where you want depth." After that, every answer runs Situation with baseline, Task with my scope, Action with my artifacts, Result with the number and its basis, then one line of what I'd do differently.

R1 · Austria first, then 25 European locations — the routing-guide engine waits for the data FREE

Company: Elementum (supply-chain SaaS), client logistics program · My role: program lead for the analysis, the rules design and the rollout gate · Sources: Apple v11 F2 p35–37; GFiber v8 S10 p120–121; master S10
Headline: about $8M validated freight savings across 25 European locations (record) · Austria pilot about $500K a year (record) · data quality ~40% reported / ~55% true → 99% within a quarter (record)
60-second pitch

"At Elementum, a client's transportation team in Austria could not measure on-time delivery. The report said data quality was 40%, the team spent eight to ten hours a week reconciling dates by hand, and everyone believed the carriers were late. I owned the analysis, the rules design and the rollout gate. I pulled three years of shipment history in SQL myself. True data quality was about 55%, and the problem was measurement, not carriers. I set one rule: the routing engine waits for the data. Nothing scales until quality hits 99%. I also wrote the savings method with Finance before anyone claimed a dollar: contracted rate against what would have been paid, per shipment, executed only. The Austria pilot went live inside a month, measured about 97% on-time accuracy and about $500K a year, and that green-lit 25 European locations. It closed at about eight million dollars of validated freight savings, and Finance reused the method afterwards."

S — Situation. The client's Austria site had a routing guide on paper and no enforceable on-time delivery (OTD). Reported inbound data quality was about 40%, and logistics spent 8–10 hours a week reconciling promised dates against actual dates (record, Apple F2). The site, the carriers' account teams and my own sponsor all believed the carriers were the problem. The freight base had no SLA anyone could hold a carrier to.

T — Task. I owned the analysis, the rules-engine design, the savings-validation method and the scale gate. Transportation, Finance and engineering did not report to me. The targets were set with the business: data quality to 99%, the manual reconciliation retired, and every savings dollar auditable.

A — Action.

  • Baseline first. I pulled three years of shipment history in SQL and ran a defect census. The defects were missing contracted rates, lanes keyed at the wrong grain, carrier names in five spellings, and transit times copied from a brochure. True quality was ~55%, not 40%. I took the reframe to the sponsor: we are not measuring lateness yet.
  • Method before money. I co-wrote the savings definition with Finance before the first claim: contracted guide rate minus counterfactual paid rate, per shipment, per lane, executed savings only, never "negotiated".
  • The gate. The engine waits for the data. The quality gate was 99% before scale, tracked weekly by defect class, with each class owned and burned down on the same weekly read.
  • Rules written down before configuration. Ship-from, ship-to, mode, volume, Incoterm and SLA. A lane with no match pages a human; it never falls back to a default carrier. Gaps after go-live surfaced as alerts with reason codes.
  • Pilot on the ugliest mix, then scale off the pilot's own numbers. Austria went live inside one month. Super-users per region and a one-page runbook carried the rollout to 25 European locations (runbook and super-users: user anchor, GFiber v8).

R — Result.

  • About $8M of validated freight savings across 25 European locations. Every dollar is auditable back to a shipment (record, Apple F2 and resume).
  • Data quality ~55% true → 99%, "within a quarter" (record. Apple says two months, the master says about three, so the quarter is true under both).
  • Austria pilot: ~97% on-time measurement accuracy and ~$500K a year at one site. Those were the numbers that made the scale call (record).
  • Manual reconciliation of 8–10 hours a week retired (record). The "1–2 hours after" figure is a user anchor (GFiber v8), and I say it only if asked.
  • Finance reused the validation method for the next claims, so the control outlived the program (record).
Who I influenced: the sponsor (from "carriers are late" to "we can't measure late"), using his own three years of data · Finance (co-owner of the savings definition before any claim) · transportation managers (from picking carriers to managing exceptions with reason codes) · regional super-users (they own the rules files).
Trade-off I made

Options: (a) turn on the engine now and clean the data in flight, or (b) hold the engine until quality reached 99%. I chose (b). I rejected (a) because an engine fed dirty lanes tenders with confidence and gets it wrong, and one visibly wrong tender costs more trust than a month of delay. It cost a slower start and savings deferred by weeks, and the sponsor had to wait for the first dollar.

Failure / learning: The audit ledger's format settled while the first claims were already being made. I would build it during the Austria pilot, which would have made the first CFO review shorter.
  • Dive Deep — three years of history in SQL showed 55% true, not 40% reported.
  • Think Big — one site's pilot was built as the template for 25 locations.
  • Insist on the Highest Standards — the 99% gate held before scale.
  • Customer Obsession — the customer asked for "fix the carriers" when the need was "make late measurable".

Probing — four questions he will ask

"$8M — how do you avoid double counting?"

Each saving is counted once, per shipment, as the contracted guide rate against the counterfactual paid rate, executed only. Double counting lives in programs that count "negotiated" and "executed" separately. Finance co-owned the definition before the first claim.

"What exactly did you do versus the engineers?"

I pulled the data and ran the census, wrote the rules spec and the gate, co-wrote the method, and read the pilot to make the scale call. Engineering built and configured the engine. The carriers' performance was the carriers'.

"Who disagreed?"

My sponsor and the site, at first. They wanted carrier penalties now. They were right that carriers had real misses, and the scorecard caught those later. They were wrong that we could prove it on 55%-quality data. The SQL census settled it, so I didn't have to argue.

"What happened after you left the program?"

Finance kept the validation method for the next claims, and the super-users kept the rules files. A new site cost one rules file and one data-quality cycle (user anchor). That is the mechanism, not me.

SAFE-SAY (most attackable number): "About eight million of validated freight savings across twenty-five European locations, counted per shipment, executed only, by a method Finance co-owned. That is the record figure, and I never say one million or a lane count."

"What's your source?" "My program record and resume: the Austria pilot read and the Finance-validated ledger."

20-second version

"Austria said carriers were late. Three years of data in SQL said we couldn't even measure late: 55% true quality, not 40%. I held the engine until data hit 99% and wrote the savings method with Finance first. The pilot was 97% accurate and worth about $500K a year, and the rollout to 25 European locations closed at about $8M validated."

R2 · The semi-annual carrier bid — a reconciliation problem everyone called an optimization problem FREE

Company: Elementum, client transportation team · My role: owned requirements, constraint rules, validation gate and parallel run, not the award · Sources: GFiber v8 S17 p166–167; master S17
Headline: bid cycle 6 weeks → ~3; pilot region 3 weeks early; adopted by other regions without a mandate (all user anchor — GFiber v8 only; no Apple or primary-doc support). No dollar claimed.
60-second pitch

"A client ran a carrier bid twice a year: hundreds of lanes, dozens of rate files, six weeks a cycle. Everyone assumed it was an optimization problem and wanted a better solver. Before building anything, I sat through one full bid cycle and logged where the hours went. Most of the six weeks was reconciliation, because every carrier named lanes and modes differently. So I specified a lane master, wrote the constraints as explicit rules, and set a gate: the tool had to match the last two real awards within 5% before anyone trusted its output. We parallel-ran it on one region's live bid. The cycle went from about six weeks to about three, the pilot region finished three weeks early, and other regions adopted it without a mandate. My miss: procurement joined late."

S — Situation. The client's transportation team ran a semi-annual carrier bid across hundreds of lanes and dozens of carrier rate files, with constraints like incumbency floors and volume commitments. Each cycle took about six weeks (user anchor). The team's working belief was "we need a better optimizer".

T — Task. I owned the requirements, the constraint rules and the parallel run. Transportation, procurement and engineering did not report to me, and the award itself was theirs.

A — Action.

  • Diagnose the problem class with a time audit. I sat through a whole bid cycle logging hours by activity. The six weeks went to reconciling lane names and mode codes, not to analysis. I wrote a one-page problem-class memo: reconciliation, not optimization.
  • Simplify the input, not the solver. A lane-master spec normalizing origin, destination and mode. Every constraint became one explicit, readable rule: volume commitments, incumbency floors, mode rules.
  • A trust gate before any output counted. Less than 5% mismatch against the last two award cycles before the optimizer could run. Scenario comparison was a first-class output, and I rejected a black box in writing.
  • Parallel run on a live bid. One regional team's real bid ran on both paths.
  • Keep it honest after cutover. Weekly rate-file refresh, with a stale-data banner after 10 days.

R — Result.

  • Bid cycle about six weeks → about three (user anchor). The pilot region finished three weeks early (user anchor).
  • Other regions adopted it without a mandate (user anchor).
  • No award dollar claimed. The award belonged to transportation and procurement. Any dollar figure for analyst time returned is modeled only, and I don't lead with it.
Who I influenced: transportation leadership (from "buy a better solver" to "fix the lane names", using their own hours) · the regional team that volunteered the parallel run · engineering (scenario comparison instead of a black box) · procurement, late, and that is the miss.
Trade-off I made

Options: (a) build or buy a stronger optimizer, which everyone expected, or (b) fix reconciliation first and keep the solver simple and explainable. I chose (b). I rejected (a) because a better solver on mismatched lane names gives a faster wrong answer. It cost one full bid cycle of observation before any build, and some impatience from a team that wanted a tool.

Failure / learning: Procurement joined late. They owned award rules I had to retrofit. Lesson: put every owner of a constraint in the room at discovery, not at design review.
  • Invent & Simplify — the usual approach (a better solver) wouldn't address it; a lane master would.
  • Dive Deep — a time audit across one full cycle, before any design.
  • Have Backbone — I said "it's not an optimization problem" to a team that had already decided it was.
  • Deliver Results — about half the cycle time, with the pilot three weeks early.

Probing — four questions he will ask

"How do you know the time saved wasn't just a quieter cycle?"

The same team on the same kind of bid, measured against my own time audit of the previous cycle. The honest caveat: those counts are my anchors from the program and not an audited report, so I say "about half".

"Why a 5% gate?"

It had to beat a planner's gut on the last two awards they already trusted. Below 5% mismatch, the planners trusted the tool enough to stop re-keying. Above it, they would have kept running the old spreadsheet beside it.

"Who disagreed, and were they right?"

Procurement, when they arrived. They were right that two award rules weren't in my constraint set, so I added them as explicit rules before the second region.

"What would you do differently?"

Bring procurement in at the time audit. I'd also publish the stale-data banner rule from day one, not after the first stale week.

SAFE-SAY: "About six weeks to about three. Those are my program anchors, and I claim no award savings because the award belonged to transportation and procurement."

"What's your source?" "My own time audit of the bid cycle and the program record. There's no published report, so I give you the shape and the gate."

S — Situation. The client's supply view depended on data an external contract manufacturer published from six site systems. The six systems carried six definitions of yield, lead time and order status. Two revisions of the same data could be partly valid at the same time for weeks, so auto-loading one would silently rewrite history. T — Task. I owned the ingest requirements, the per-site reconciliation gate, the wave plan and the weekly call with the contract manufacturer. Their engineers, our data engineers and the client's planners did not report to me.

A — Action.

  • Wave by risk, not by geography. Wave 1 was the two cleanest high-volume sites. Wave 2 was three sites on parameterized maps. Wave 3 was the hostile legacy system, scheduled last with buffer.

  • Quarantine, never auto-load. Overlapping revisions went to an effective-date quarantine queue for human disposition.
  • Engineer around the hostile system. With no change-data-capture, we used snapshot-diffing on the flat files instead of waiting for an upgrade the site couldn't fund.
  • Hand ownership to the sites. A named super-user at each site owned that site's mapping tables. The weekly call with the contract manufacturer became standing, framed as their protection, not our audit.
