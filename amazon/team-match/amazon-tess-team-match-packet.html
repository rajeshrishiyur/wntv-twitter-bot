I. Amazon transportation, end to end

This section is the physical path a unit takes, inbound and outbound, and the decisions that sit on that path. Amazon has published the shape of the network: fulfillment centers, sort centers, delivery stations, air hubs, a first-party middle mile, and a last mile run largely by its own logistics. It has not published the clock times, trailer rates, or cost-per-package stack used below. Those worked examples are the generic model, with every assumption stated. Where a figure is Amazon's, the year and the source are in the sentence and again in the list at the end.

I.1 Inbound: vendor and seller freight to a promiseable unit

Two inbound customers share the buildings. A first-party vendor sells the unit to Amazon. A third-party seller keeps title and uses Fulfillment by Amazon, so Amazon stores and ships it. In both cases the transportation question is the same: which building receives the freight, and who pays to put the units near the customers who will buy them.

Amazon's Carrier Central manual treats an inbound purchase order as collect or prepaid, and it will not book the two on the same shipment line. An Amazon reference number is mandatory for collect freight; the manual says that number is assigned to collect vendors, and that the purchase order has to show as collect in Amazon's system before an appointment is granted. Prepaid purchase orders go on their own carrier reference. In ordinary freight language, collect means the receiver controls and pays the carrier, and prepaid means the shipper does. The manual confirms the two types and the appointment rule. It does not publish a rate card.

The same manual is the public description of dock discipline. The carrier requests an appointment with the destination, the purchase orders, the pallet and carton counts, and a bill-of-lading or seller reference so Amazon can tie the truck to the advance ship notice. Amazon grants a time. A purchase order pointed at the wrong building, a collect order mixed onto a prepaid line, or a reference that does not match the advance ship notice is rejected or becomes a defect. The generic operating point, which the manual supports without using these words, is that the yard plan and the receive labor are built from the appointment, and the inventory record is built from the advance ship notice. A truck that arrives outside that pair consumes a door and does not become promiseable inventory on time.

Where the truck goes is the placement decision. A 2016 MIT thesis on Amazon transshipment describes the domestic cross-dock: the vendor sends the network quantity to the cross-dock, and the cross-dock splits it and transships to the destination fulfillment centers, instead of the vendor running a separate truck to each one. A 2019 FHWA briefing describes the same kind of building, the inbound cross-dock, close to ports and rail, destuffing containers and loading fulfillment-center trucks. This packet calls that node the inbound cross-dock. A distinct building officially named a receive center was not verified, so that name is not used as an Amazon fact.

The economic signal for sellers is public. In its late-2023 fee announcement, Amazon said it would charge an inbound placement service fee, effective March 1, 2024, averaging $0.27 per unit for standard-size products and $1.58 per unit for Large Bulky products, because placing inventory close to customers improves speed and lowers transportation cost. The seller can pay a reduced fee or no fee by shipping to more locations. Amazon's seller-facing explanation of the options is: no fee when the seller sends inventory to the recommended set of locations, generally four or more; a reduced fee for a partial split, generally two or three; and the fee when the seller sends everything to one location and Amazon spreads it. Outbound fulfillment fees were cut at the same time, by an average of $0.20 per unit for standard-size products and $0.61 for Large Bulky, starting April 15, 2024. Read together, Amazon unbundled the cost of placement from the cost of customer delivery, and handed the seller the choice.

Jassy's 2023 shareholder letter, published in 2024, named the same lever from the company side: inbound fulfillment architecture and the resulting inventory placement were a focus for 2024, after regionalization had already cut transportation distance. Placement is how a unit becomes available against the promise in the region where the order will be placed. Receive and stow are the building steps that make that true in the system. The generic model is: until the unit is received and stowed to a pickable location, it cannot be promised, however close the truck got.

I.2 Outbound: promise, pull time, sort, last mile

A customer order becomes a promise: a ship option and a delivery date. Amazon's customer help page says the date is the shipping date plus transit time, in business days, with an "order within" countdown when the clock is still open. That promise is the constraint. Everything after it is a path chosen so the attempt happens on or before that date, at a cost the network can live with.

Amazon Science (October 14, 2022) describes the path in the first-party network. Packages leave fulfillment centers for sort centers, which aggregate shipments from several fulfillment centers, and then go to delivery stations, where vans are loaded for the last mile. The connections are lanes, air and ground. The same article names the label step: SLAM, scan, label, apply, manifest. Amazon's own fulfillment-center tour uses the same expansion and places it after pack and before the outbound trailer: the package is identified, weighed, labeled, and manifested, then sorted to a trailer. In the generic model the label is the moment the package is committed to a lane and a departure. Change the lane after that and you are rehandling.

The sort center, in Amazon's public description, sorts by destination and hands trailers to delivery stations or to a carrier partner, including the local post office. A 2025 paper by Amazon authors in INFORMS Journal on Applied Analytics describes a further split that regionalization made useful: hubs, which protect trailer fill on the long cross-region haul, and last-mile sort centers, which consolidate for delivery stations. Direct fulfillment-center-to-delivery-station trucks are the bypass. Amazon Science (July 24, 2023) says regionalization created more of those direct trucks, because more of a destination's volume now comes from fulfillment centers inside the region, so a trailer can fill without a stop at the sort center.

The last mile is a mix. The 2023 sustainability report says more than two-thirds of Amazon packages that year were delivered by Amazon's own logistics network. The public programs on that network are Delivery Service Partners, independent businesses that run the routes; Amazon Flex, drivers using their own vehicles who pick up at a station and deliver a block, typically three to six hours for Amazon.com packages; and, on the rural-network page, Hub Delivery with local businesses. Amazon Science (2022) is explicit about the rest of the history: before the first-party network, deliveries were completed by partners such as the U.S. Postal Service and UPS. The 2025 Form 10-K still says Amazon relies on a limited number of shipping companies to deliver completed orders. A current split among DSP, Flex, USPS, and UPS was not published, so it is not stated here.

Same-day sites shorten this path by collapsing it. Doug Herrington, July 31, 2023: same-day buildings are smaller, sit near the metros they serve, pick directly to pack, and fulfill, sort, and deliver from one site. He gave the average time from pick to a package on the outbound dock as 11 minutes, more than an hour faster than a traditional fulfillment center, and said same-day was then available across more than 90 U.S. metro areas. Jassy's 2023 letter, published in 2024, counted 58 of these buildings, holding the top 100,000 SKUs, with millions of other SKUs injectable from nearby fulfillment centers, and called them the lowest cost to serve in the network. The 2025 letter calls them Same Day Fulfillment Centers, or SSDs: more than 85 in the U.S., carrying the top 90,000 SKUs. The phrase "sub-same-day" was not verified as Amazon's official expansion of SSD. The operating fact that was verified is the collapsed path: one building, no separate sort center, a very short clock.

Air is the long-lane version of the same idea. Amazon's August 11, 2021 press release opened the Amazon Air hub at Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport as the primary U.S. hub, an investment of $1.5 billion, with an 800,000-square-foot sort building meant to process millions of packages a week. The 2023 sustainability report says in-region shipping reduced reliance on air. Air remains the tool for distance that ground cannot cover inside the promise.

Amazon Freight and Relay are the middle-mile commercial layer, not the last mile. Amazon's supply-chain site distinguishes them: Amazon Freight, in the Relay for Shippers portal, is an inbound less-than-truckload and truckload option, including to non-Amazon destinations in the lower 48; Relay is the capacity side, the trailers and the carriers that haul them. The aboutamazon transportation article describes Relay as the tool small carriers use to book Amazon loads. Treat Freight as a way freight gets into and across the network, and Relay as how truck capacity is matched to those loads.

Critical pull time is the outbound clock. Amazon does not define it on a public retail page. Part 4 — How Amazon thinks about network design

II. How Amazon thinks about transportation network design

Three objectives are in tension, and Amazon's own writing names all three. Cost to serve has to come down. Inventory has to sit where the order will be placed. The customer has to get the unit on the date the site promised. Herrington, July 31, 2023, put the mechanism in one place: regionalizing cut miles and handoffs, the distance from sites to the customer was down 15 percent since the start of that year, middle-mile touchpoints were down 12 percent, and same-day was both the fastest path and one of the cheapest. Jassy's letters and the 2024 and 2025 earnings calls then reported the outcome: per-unit cost to serve down, and Prime speed up, in the same years. The design problem is to move all three, and to say which one gives when they cannot.

II.1 Minimize cost per package

In the generic model the cost of a package is the sum of handling, linehaul, sort, and last mile, divided by the packages that actually moved. Four levers change that sum.

Truck utilization is cube against weight. A trailer fills out on one of them first. Small parcels usually fill the cube while the payload still has room, so the useful denominator is usable cubic feet, not the weight rating. The 2023 sustainability report names truck fill, the share of truck volume used, as a lever Amazon actually pulled.

Consolidation against frequency is the same trailer counted in time. Two departures a day keep the pull time early and leave each trailer light. One departure fills the trailer and makes every package that missed the single cutoff wait until tomorrow's clock. Amazon Science (October 14, 2022) describes the operating version: a lane is one or more trucks across the day, and the plan assigns volume so the truck does not leave empty and the dock is not left holding a handful of packages.

Fewer touches is a bypass. A package that goes fulfillment center, sort center, delivery station is handled three times on the middle and last mile. A direct injection from the fulfillment center to the delivery station deletes the sort. Zone skipping, in the generic model, is the same idea aimed at a carrier: induct the package closer to the destination so you do not buy the carrier's long zone. Amazon Science (July 24, 2023) and the 2025 INFORMS paper both describe the direct fulfillment-center-to-delivery-station arc as a designed output of regionalization, because localized volume can fill a direct trailer.

Last-mile density is stops per hour, which shows up as packages per route. A van costs roughly the same whether it carries a light route or a full one. The catchment, the station's wave, and the promise together set how many stops fit before the window closes.

II.2 Put inventory where it changes the ship

Regionalization is an inventory decision whose output is miles and touches. Jassy's 2022 shareholder letter, published in 2023, describes the prior state: one national U.S. network, so a miss at the local fulfillment center shipped from somewhere else in the country, at higher cost and a longer delivery. The replacement was eight interconnected regions, each with broad selection, largely self-sufficient, still able to ship nationally. The letter says the redesign had just been completed and that shorter distances mean a lower cost to serve and a faster order.

Amazon Science, July 24, 2023, gives the selection logic and the result. Eight was as many regions as the model would support before speed suffered or inventory had to move between regions so often that the design defeated itself. The Northeast and Mid-Atlantic regions switched on January 18, 2023, and the other six were pulled forward. Orders fulfilled entirely from fulfillment centers inside the region went from 62 percent before the switch to 76 percent. Herrington, July 31, 2023, stated the customer-facing version: more than 76 percent of fulfilled orders came from within the customer's region, distance from sites to the customer was down 15 percent from the start of the year, and middle-mile touchpoints were down 12 percent. The 2023 sustainability report adds the volume and the miles: in the fourth quarter of 2023 Amazon shipped nearly 544 million more items from in-region fulfillment centers than in the fourth quarter of 2022, and in-region shipping to delivery stations avoided nearly 16 million miles in 2023. The 2025 INFORMS paper dates full deployment to March 2023 and describes the method: cluster ZIP codes, assign fulfillment-center capacity to the clusters, simulate, then judge the design on cost to serve and delivery speed.

On the Q4 2025 earnings call, February 5, 2026, finance said the U.S. regionalized network was operating at scale and that the next productivity work was still inventory placement: less distance, fewer touches per package, better package consolidation. The 76 percent, the 15 percent, and the 12 percent are 2023 results. They were not restated as 2025 rates.

The inventory math under that story is the generic square-root law, associated with Maister (1976). It is not an Amazon formula. If demand is split across n equal, independent regions, and lead time and the service level stay the same, total safety stock grows with the square root of n. Pool the slow movers, where a long ship is rare. Spread the fast movers, where almost every unit would otherwise travel.

II.3 The customer gets it on the day that was promised

A promise is a radius plus a clock. Same-day requires the unit inside the metro, in a building that can pick, sort, and dispatch before the afternoon route leaves. Herrington's 2023 description of the same-day building is that path. Next-day, or Prime one-day, requires the unit inside the region and a pull time that connects the fulfillment center, the sort, and the station inside one cycle. Two-day is the national backup when the region does not hold the unit. Jassy's 2024 letter, published in 2025, gives the selection that sits on those tiers: Prime began as two-day on a million items and had grown to more than 300 million items, with tens of millions available in one day or better, and a rising share same-day. The letter says the speed came from the regional redesign, new placement algorithms, and same-day fulfillment centers, and that a faster promise raises the rate at which customers complete the purchase and the rate at which they come back.

The cost curve is not "faster always costs more." Jassy's 2023 letter, published in 2024, called the same-day facilities the lowest cost to serve in the network, because they delete middle-mile distance. Herrington said the same thing in July 2023. The expensive version of faster is the one that keeps the old path and buys frequency or air on top of it. The cheap version deletes a touch.

Reliability is a separate axis from the promised speed. The customer help page explains how the date is built. It does not publish the share of attempts that hit it. II.4 How a network designer decomposes the problem

The decomposition Amazon has actually described, in the 2025 INFORMS paper and in Amazon Science (July 24, 2023 and October 14, 2022), lines up with the way an industrial engineer would expect a manager to hold the problem.

Define the demand surface. The paper treats five-digit ZIP codes as the points, on a surface that is dense in some corridors and thin in others. A national average is the wrong input. The Northeast and the Northwest do not cluster the same way, which is why the authors say a single algorithm was not enough and the boundaries were adjusted by hand.

Define the nodes and what each one costs. A fulfillment center costs labor, space, and a safety-stock position. A sort center or a hub costs a touch and a trailer. A delivery station costs routes. A same-day building costs a constrained assortment and deletes a middle-mile leg. Direct fulfillment-center-to-station lanes cost a trailer and save a sort. The cost function is lumpy: a truck costs nearly the same at 60 percent fill and at 95 percent, which is why fill shows up as cost per package.

Set the service constraint before the solve. Cost to serve and delivery speed were the success metrics the paper names for the regionalization pilot. A design that cuts cost and misses the promise fails the constraint. A design that makes the promise by emptying the trailers fails the other one.

Solve at the level a manager can say out loud. One new building wants to sit near the center of gravity of the demand it will serve, pulled toward the dense ZIP codes and away from cheap land that adds drive time. Several buildings sharing one market is a p-median idea: choose p sites so the demand-weighted distance, inside the service radius, is as small as it can be. Region boundaries are a clustering of ZIP codes under a cap on demand and on distance, which is the method the INFORMS paper reports, followed by an assignment of fulfillment centers to those regions. Lane frequency is an assignment of flow to paths, including the choice to skip the sort. None of these needs a formula in the conversation. Each one needs the input, the constraint, and the output.

Stress the answer on peak and on failure. Peak is a volume the average day does not see, and it breaks pull times, door counts, and route caps together. A node down, or a carrier late, is a missing path: the packages reroute, the fills change, and some promises move out by a day. Amazon Science describes the planning analogue of safety stock: buffer transportation capacity held against demand uncertainty, and a plan stable enough that the people who hired labor and booked trucks can actually run it. A design that only works on the mean, with no buffer and no second path, is not finished.

How to talk about this with an industrial engineer. Avinash's background is operations research, Lean Six Sigma, and production planning, so the useful altitude is the decision, the binding constraint, and the tail. A lane plan is a linear program: trailers by lane and by departure, cost as the objective, cube and the pull time as the constraints. You do not solve it in the room. You say whether fill or the clock is binding. A pull time is a quantile of a transit-time distribution, and a simulation is how you see the share of trucks that miss it, which the average hides. Dock doors are a queue: as utilization gets high, the wait grows out of proportion, and the packages in that wait miss the pull time. The sentence to offer is the binding constraint, the metric you would read the week after, and what you would roll back if the tail shows up. Leave the solver unnamed.
