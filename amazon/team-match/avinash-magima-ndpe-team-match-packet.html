2.1 Flexport: two scoreboards that were not measuring the same thing

Use it for: using data to change a decision; a deep dive; comparing two systems; handling a number that is about to kill a project. One-line version: leadership saw 63 percent against 25 percent and was ready to shut down the newer product; measured the same way, it was 27 against 25.

Say this — 267 words, about two minutes

Situation. At Flexport in 2022, after we launched Shipment Visibility, leadership had two numbers side by side. Our new platform showed 25 percent schedule reliability, meaning one prediction in four of when a container would arrive was right. Crux, an older tracking product that Flexport had acquired and still ran for 79 customers, showed 63 percent. The proposal was to shut down the new platform and keep Crux.

Task. I owned the comparison and the evidence for the decision meeting. I had to find out whether 63 against 25 was a real gap.

Action. I did not argue with either number. I took both apart, and found they measured different things: different containers, scored at different moments before arrival. Crux's 63 counted only the shipments its data feeds covered, with the prediction taken close to arrival, when predicting is easy. So I wrote one definition: same containers, same week, take the arrival date each product predicted thirty days before, and compare it with the actual date. I re-scored both. Then I looked for the real gap and reported it against my own product: our predictions were spread across sixteen days where theirs were spread across eight.

Result. On the same basis it was about 27 percent against 25. Thirty-six of the thirty-eight points were the measurement, not the product. Leadership kept the new platform and funded six months of new data sources, and reliability reached 90 percent against a target of 80. Crux was retired on a schedule, with a path for each customer. What I took from it: make two numbers comparable before you compare them.

Step A. What "schedule reliability" means, on one container (example dates)

Thirty days before a container is due, the product predicts the arrival date. When the container actually arrives, you compare. If the prediction was within the allowed margin, it is a hit. Otherwise it is a miss.

Schedule reliability = number of hits ÷ number of predictions scored. If 250 of 1,000 predictions are hits, reliability is 250 ÷ 1,000 = 25 percent.

The same container can be a miss at thirty days and a hit at five days, because close to arrival every prediction gets easier. That is why the moment of the prediction has to be fixed before two products are compared.

2.2 Elementum: two hundred shipping lanes planned on measured lead time

Use it for: breaking down a complex problem; using historical data for planning; an analysis that released money. One-line version: planners used the lead time printed in the carrier's brochure; I measured the real one on 200 lanes, and safety stock was re-sized from the measurement. A lane is one origin-to-destination route, for example one supplier's factory to one warehouse.

Say this — 262 words, about two minutes

Situation. At Elementum, between 2017 and 2019, I worked with a manufacturer that planned two hundred shipping lanes using the lead time printed in each carrier's brochure. Nobody had measured how long shipments really took. To be safe, planners held large safety stock, and added their own padding on top.

Task. I owned the measurement. The goal was a lead time for each lane that we could defend, and safety stock sized from it.

Action. For each lane I took about a year of shipments and looked at the whole range of transit times, not only the average. Take one lane as an example: the brochure said 18 days, the real average was 21, and it took 34 days before 99 shipments in 100 had arrived. I planned to the 34. Then I opened up the old safety stock formula and found that almost all of it came from a lead-time swing nobody had measured, a default value typed into the system. I replaced it with two measured pieces: cover for late shipments, and cover for swings in demand. Finance signed the rule before any stock moved, and the rule cut both ways: release stock where the measurement was tighter than the guess, add stock where it was worse.

Result. On that example lane the buffer goes from 12,937 units to 8,102. Across the two hundred lanes the program released 580,000 units, about 1.5 million dollars of inventory carrying cost, one time, with lead-time accuracy at 99 percent. Some lanes received more stock, not less. That is why Finance trusted the release.

If he asks

"Why not plan to the average lead time?" If you promise the average, about half of the shipments arrive after the promise. The commitment has to sit where nearly all shipments have arrived. That is the 34, not the 21.

"How did Finance come to trust the number?" The rule was written and signed before any stock moved, lane by lane, and it included the lanes where stock went up. It is a one-time release. I do not present it as an annual saving.

"What keeps it right over time?" A lane is re-opened if its measured late tail moves more than two days past the plan, if its spread widens by a quarter, or if four or more shipments in a hundred run late.

"What would you do differently?" Show the add-stock lane on the first page. It is what won Finance over, and I showed it second.

How this fits his team. I owned the measurement and the rule.

There are two kinds of measurement on the page, and the whole method rests on keeping them apart.

5.6 How to say all of this in two minutes

"How would you build a scorecard for our team?" — say this — 211 words

I would start with the decisions your team makes each week, because a number nobody acts on does not belong on the page. Then I would pick three or four output measures, the results the customer and the business feel: late deliveries, cost per package, and speed. I would break each output into its causes until I reached things the team controls that week, such as how close the volume plan was, how full the trailers are, whether trucks leave on time, and how many packages miss their truck. Those are the input measures, and they go at the top of the page, because they move first. For every measure I would write a definition card: top of the fraction, bottom of the fraction, the source, the owner and the target. Then I would build it from the raw records, show six weeks of trend, and color a cell when it misses the target or changes more than five percent in a week. Before anyone sees it, I would trace a sample by hand and run it beside the existing report until the two agree. I built the weekly review at Flexport this way: seven frozen definitions, inputs above outputs, and no switch-over until old and new matched within one percent.

If he pushes: "What makes a good input measure?" Three tests. The team can change it this week. It moves before the output does. And when you move it, the output follows; if it stops following, you replace it. "What goes wrong with scorecards?" Four things, each seen in this chapter: an average that hides one bad lane; a success rate near 100 percent that hides a doubling of failures; a cost measure that looks good while service is failing; and a number nobody has checked against the raw records.

Question 1. What share of packages was delivered on time, for each lane?

Think first. One row is one package. On time means delivered on or before the promised date. Top of the fraction: packages on time. Bottom: packages delivered. P09 has not been delivered, so it cannot yet be on time or late; it stays out of the bottom.

What each piece does. CASE WHEN … THEN 1 ELSE 0 END writes a 1 beside each on-time package and a 0 beside the others; SUM adds the 1s. COUNT(*) counts every row. COUNT(delivered_date) counts only rows where that column is filled in, which is why lane L2 shows 3 shipped but 2 delivered.

Three traps to mention aloud. One: the bottom of the fraction. With COUNT(*), lane L2 would be 1 ÷ 3 = 33.3 percent, and the undelivered package would be silently counted as late. Say which you chose and why. Two: whole-number division. In many databases 4 ÷ 6 with whole numbers gives 0. Writing 100.0 * first forces a decimal answer. Three: never average the three lane percentages to get the network figure. (66.7 + 50.0 + 100.0) ÷ 3 = 72.2. The right answer is 8 on time ÷ 11 delivered = 72.7 percent.

2JOIN or LEFT JOIN?An ordinary JOIN keeps only rows that match in both tables. A LEFT JOIN keeps every row of the first table, with empty values where the second has no match. In question 2, the ordinary JOIN would lose lane L4.

This packet uses plain words. He will use the trade words. Here is the bridge.

Word you may hearPlain meaning
Input metric, output metricA measurement of something the team controls (input), and of the result that follows (output). Chapter 5.
Weekly business review (often shortened to its initials, WBR)The weekly meeting where the scorecard is read and the exceptions are explained.
Deep dive; root causeTaking a number apart until you find what actually moved it. Section 2.3.
LaneOne route from one place to another, such as Warehouse A to Hub 1.
Fulfillment centerThe warehouse where items are stored, picked and packed.
Sort centerThe sorting hub, where packages from many warehouses are sorted by destination.
Delivery stationThe local depot where packages are loaded into vans.
Middle mile; linehaulThe truck trips between buildings. Last mile is the van to the customer's door.
Fill; cube utilizationHow full a trailer is.
Computer visionA model that looks at a photograph and returns a measurement. Section 6.6 uses it for the height of freight in an open gaylord. Design case, not a result you delivered.
Critical pull timeThe latest moment a package can leave a building and still be delivered on the promised day.
On-time delivery; delivery estimate accuracyThe share of packages that arrived by the date the customer was promised.
Cost to serve; cost per packageAll the transportation dollars divided by the number of packages.
Percentile (for example, "p90")The value that a given share of cases falls under. "p90 is 5.74 hours" means 90 trips in 100 finish within 5.74 hours.
MechanismAmazon's word for a repeatable process with an owner and a check, as opposed to good intentions.
